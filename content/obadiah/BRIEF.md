## What a chapter is for here

The book is the chapter. A reader meets twenty-one verses against a neighbor nation and asks plain questions: *Who was Obadiah, and when did he speak? Who is Edom, and why is it called Esau? What did Edom do? Why does this sound like Jeremiah 49? What is “the day of the LORD” here? Who has “drunk upon my holy mountain”? Where are Sepharad and Zarephath? Who are the “saviours,” and how did a verse about Edom become the Latter-day Saint phrase for temple work?* The chapter answers those from the text first.

**The chapter carries the book.** With one chapter there is no second `setting` to share the load: the `setting` (still about 150–250 words) gives the date and the occasion; the `thread` gives the turn at verse 15, where the day of Judah's calamity becomes the day of the LORD on all nations. The book page's intro says who Obadiah was in a sentence and does not repeat the setting.

**Say what Obadiah said to Judah before saying what latter-day prophets read in it.** The Church's sources read verse 21 of temple work for the dead, and so does the chapter, but each note first says what the words meant to Judahites after Jerusalem fell. Give the picture, then the reading, and keep them apart (as Amos's brief asks of Amos 8:11–12).

**Edom is already explained on the site; link, don't repeat.** Esau as Edom and the name “Red” (`[[Gen. 25:30]]`, `[[Gen. 36:1]]`); Edom's land and the kinship (the note “Edom, Judah's relative” at `[[Jer. 49:7]]`); what Edom did when Jerusalem fell, with the other passages gathered (“What Edom did” at `[[Ezek. 25:12]]`, and `[[Ezek. 35:5]]`, `[[Ps. 137:7]]`, `[[Lam. 4:21]]`); Teman (“The wisdom of Teman,” `[[Jer. 49:7]]`); Petra and the nest in the rock (“A nest in the rock,” `[[Jer. 49:16]]`); Bozrah (`[[Isa. 34:6]]`, `[[Jer. 49:13]]`; Bozrah is not named in Obadiah); Idumea as the world (`[[Isa. 34:5]]`, `[[Ezek. 35:14]]`, `[[Joel 3:19]]`); the cup of the Lord's fury (Jeremiah's theme page `cup-of-fury`); Edom the brother (`[[Amos 1:11]]`). A note here says what Obadiah adds and points to the note that has the rest.

## Divisions

`book.yaml` has one division, `obadiahvision`, range [1, 1]: divisions are chapter ranges, and the map on the book page shows the single chapter. The book's own structure goes into the chapter's `sections`. Pike divides it in two (1–14 judgment and charge; 15–21 the day of the LORD and Zion restored) and says “different scholars divide the book somewhat differently”; Gillum in three (1–9; 10–14; 15–21). **Proposed `sections`:** 1–4 (Edom's pride brought down from the rock); 5–9 (stripped by its own allies; the wise men of Teman); 10–14 (the charge: “the day of thy brother,” “day” ten times); 15–16 (the day of the LORD on all the heathen; “as thou hast done”); 17–21 (deliverance on mount Zion; the land repossessed; saviours, and the kingdom the LORD's).

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest is the coordinator's direction of where to look. None of it goes into the chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **When.** The book gives no king and no year. *Scripture Helps*: written after a conquest of Jerusalem, “probably its capture by the Babylonians in 586 BC.” The Bible Dictionary: “possibly by the Philistines and Arabians during the reign of Jehoram, 848–844 B.C., or more probably by the Chaldeans, 586 B.C.”; the Guide to the Scriptures gives the same two. Pike: “it is now common” to see 586 as the backdrop, and Obadiah's ministry “fits best in the 580s”; Van Dyke and Ogden and Gillum say after the destruction. Keil argues for Jehoram's reign and an Obadiah older than Joel. **Proposed: the chapter's `when` is “Probably soon after the fall of Jerusalem in 586 B.C.”** (from *Scripture Helps* or the Bible Dictionary), and the setting gives the earlier date in one sentence as the other reading the Church's dictionary allows. `book.yaml`'s period stays `babylon`. **The Bible Dictionary points to `[[2 Kgs. 14:7]]` and `[[2 Chr. 25:11–12]]` (Amaziah's war, about 790 B.C.) “for the fulfillment”**; that fits only the early date. Don't repeat the pointer; Pike says Edom's kingdom was ended by the Babylonians in 553 B.C., on evidence “fragmentary but fairly certain,” and that the full reversal of verses 17–21 “never happened in antiquity.”
- **Who.** The name means “Servant of the Lord” (Bible Dictionary; Pike, “servant (or worshipper) of YHWH”). Pike counts “thirteen different people named Obadiah in the Old Testament”; the Bible Dictionary names Ahab's steward (`[[1 Kgs. 18]]`) first, and Gillum reports the Talmud's tradition that he was the prophet. The Student Manual: “it is not likely he was the author.” **Proposed:** one note at 1:1 (“The vision of Obadiah”): nothing is known of him; the many Obadiahs; the tradition about Ahab's steward as a tradition, with Gillum. “Vision” (*ḥāzôn*) is “perhaps better understood here as ‘revelation’” (Pike). Bowen's pun on the name (*ʿōbēd*, “servant,” and *ʾbd*, “destroy,” in verses 8 and 12) may be its last sentence or a Language note at 1:8, not both.
- **Obadiah and Jeremiah 49.** Pike: “much of the content of Obadiah 1–7 is also found” in `[[Jer. 49:7–22]]`, and it is usually presumed that one prophet drew on the other “or that they both utilized an earlier source no longer extant.” Keil takes Jeremiah as the borrower. **The chapter does not say who borrowed.** The site's note “Jeremiah and Obadiah” at `[[Jer. 49:9]]` sets out the parallel; the note here points to it and says what Obadiah has that Jeremiah lacks: the charge of verses 10–14. Pike also notes “a number of similarities between the books of Joel and Obadiah” (`[[Joel 2:32]]` beside verse 17; `[[Joel 3:19]]`); link.
- **“We have heard a rumour” (1:1).** Who “we” is is not clear (Pike); the Septuagint and `[[Jer. 49:14]]` read “I.” The “ambassador” is “presumably a heavenly messenger.” Brenton's page (`lxx-rahlfs`) for the Greek.
- **Sela and Petra (1:3–4).** “The clefts of the rock” is *selaʿ*; Bowen, quoting Sweeney, reads “an obvious reference to the Edomite fortress city of Sela that was conquered by Amaziah and renamed Joktheel (2 Kings 14:7).” The Student Manual describes Petra and says “many scholars think it was the capital” and that its visible ruins “date from a later period.” Jeremiah's note at 49:16 already quotes the manual on Petra: here, give the wordplay and link for Petra. There is no Bible Dictionary entry “Sela,” “Teman,” “Bozrah” or “Sepharad.”
- **Esau and Jacob.** Edom twice, Esau seven times (Bowen); Pike: the name Esau evokes Genesis 25 and 27 and makes the quarrel one “between kin.” “Thy brother Jacob” (1:10) is the charge's ground. The Guide to the Scriptures (“Esau”): the descendants “became rival nations.” Link to Genesis for the brothers; don't retell them.
- **“The day” (1:11–14; 1:15).** Pike: *yôm* comes ten times in verses 11–14, the day of Judah's ruin, and verse 15 “pivots” to “the day of the LORD,” on Edom and “all the heathen.” Bowen: “their calamity” (*ʾêdô*) is a pun on Edom, three times in verse 13; “as thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee” (1:15) is a play on the verb. The day of the LORD itself is explained on the site at `[[Isa. 13:6]]`, `[[Joel 1:15]]` and `[[Amos 5:18]]`: link, and say only how Obadiah uses it. This is the chapter's thread.
- **What Edom did (1:11–14).** Read the verbs: stood aloof, rejoiced, spoke proudly, entered the gate, looted, stood in the crossway, handed over survivors. Van Dyke and Ogden name three charges (pride; joining the Babylonians; catching those who fled). The Old Testament's histories do not describe Edom's part in 586; Gillum names `[[Lam. 4:21–22]]` and 1 Esdras 4:45 (the Apocrypha, as Gillum's). One note, linking `[[Ezek. 25:12]]` for the rest.
- **“As ye have drunk upon my holy mountain” (1:16).** The “ye” is plural, unlike verse 15's “thou.” Pike gives both readings (the Edomites or Babylonians carousing on the temple mount; or Judah, which has drunk the cup of judgment) and says the second is “more generally accepted.” Give both, as his; link the cup to Jeremiah's theme page and `[[Jer. 25:15–29]]`.
- **Fire and stubble (1:18).** “The house of Joseph,” the northern tribes, beside “the house of Jacob”: Pike reads it as all Israel. The footnotes give `[[Zech. 12:6]]`, `[[2 Ne. 20:17]]`; Malachi 4:1 (`[[Mal. 4:1]]`, on the site) is the like image.
- **The land repossessed; Zarephath and Sepharad (1:19–20).** *Yāraš*, “to possess,” is the key word (Pike). Zarephath: the Bible Dictionary, “A city lying between Tyre and Sidon, where Elijah dwelt with a widow” (`[[1 Kgs. 17:9]]`, `[[Luke 4:26]]`). Sepharad is named only here; BDB: “location dubious,” with Sardis (Persian *Sparda*) among the identifications; the Septuagint reads “Ephratha.” That later Jewish usage made Sepharad Spain (whence “Sephardi”) is reception history: one clause at most, and only from a source read that is citable (the Bible Hub “Topical Lexicon” is not BDB and is not cited).
- **Names of God.** “The Lord GOD” (1:1) and “the LORD” elsewhere; copy every phrase from `data/kjv/obadiah.json`.
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** None: the footnotes list no JST reading, and Royal Skousen lists Obadiah among the books the manuscript declares “correct” in OT2. Say so only if a note needs it.
- **Modern revelation.** The footnotes give `[[D&C 1:36]]` (Idumea “or the world”), `[[D&C 103:9–10]]` and `[[D&C 76:66]]`. Pike: D&C 86:11 and 103:9–10 are the only places in scripture other than Obadiah where someone besides the Lord is a “savior” in a theological sense. A cross-reference that only repeats the footnote is not a note.

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in the chapter; don't re-explain what another book explains, point with a `[[reference]]`; a Witness of Christ does not open by saying what the chapter lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; Hebrew only where it changes what the reader understands; no question to the reader, and the `christ` section applies the principle in statements (`STANDARDS.md` §1).

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-22.

- **The date** (above): after 586, with the early date as the other reading, in one sentence; no note argues it.
- **Gloating over a brother; vengeance on Edom (1:10–18).** The book is a prophecy of doom on a people, and “there shall not be any remaining of the house of Esau” (1:18) is hard. Pike reconciles verse 18 with verse 21: the kingdom of Edom is meant in verse 18, while verse 21 “construes mount Esau more broadly as the nations of the world.” The charge is what Edom did to its brother, and the principle of 1:15 is the law of retribution (Pike, with `[[Ex. 21:23–25]]`). **The chapter doesn't apply the curse to any people now**; Edom stands for “the world” only as `[[D&C 1:36]]` and the Church's sources say.
- **“Saviours shall come up on mount Zion” (1:21).** The heart of the book for Latter-day Saints and the place a careful reader asks the most. One note of each kind, in this order, and no other note repeats it:
  1. *What the words meant* (a Language or Context note). The Hebrew *mōšiʿîm* is the participle of *yāšaʿ*, “deliverers” or “saviours”; the plural occurs elsewhere only in `[[Neh. 9:27]]`, of the judges (Pike; `[[Judg. 3:9]]`). The Septuagint vocalized the same consonants as a passive, “they that escape” (Brenton), which the NRSV follows; Pike: “the determination … is based on interpretive considerations, not on unambiguous textual evidence.” “To judge” is *šāpaṭ*, to govern as well as to decide (Pike). “Mount Zion” is Jerusalem, “particularly the temple mount” (*Scripture Helps*). “The kingdom” is *mĕlûkâ*, kingship.
  2. *How the Restoration reads it* (a Latter-day Prophets note). The Bible Dictionary: the verse “refers to the doctrine of salvation for the dead.” Joseph Smith on 3 October 1841 presented baptism for the dead “as the only way that men can appear as saviors on mount Zion” (the Joseph Smith Papers); on 15 April 1842, in the *Times and Seasons*, the Saints were “commanded to be baptized for our dead thus fulfilling the words of Obadiah when speaking of the glory of the Latter Day,” and he quoted the verse as “to judge the remnant of Esau” (the wording he printed: note it, don't explain it); on 21 January 1844, “By building their temples…” (point to it). Brigham Young read “the house of Esau” as “the Gentile nations” (Pike quotes the *Journal of Discourses*; cite Pike). President Hinckley: “Just as our Redeemer gave His life as a vicarious sacrifice for all men … even so we, in a small measure … become as saviors.” That is the Witness of Christ too: the saviours are saviours only as the Savior's work reaches through them (Hinckley; Elder Cook, “truly become saviors on Mount Zion for our own families”; `[[D&C 103:9–10]]`).
  3. *How the two fit* (see the question for the author, below). Pike's own answer, as his: Joseph Smith did with Obadiah 1:21 “what Peter did with Joel 2:28–32” at Pentecost (`[[Acts 2:16–21]]`; `[[JS—H 1:41]]`), applying to his own dispensation a prophecy whose fullest fulfillment Pike places at the Second Coming; Obadiah “does not appear to have been teaching his contemporaries” about proxy ordinances. Van Dyke and Ogden and the Student Manual read verses 15–21 of the last days directly (“prophetic dualism,” 25-31).
  **Not used:** the wider applications Pike lists and Gillum argues for, and Gillum's personal section (reception history and anecdote, `STANDARDS.md` §1).
- **Restoration connections.** Mount Zion in `[[D&C 76:66]]` and `[[D&C 133:56]]`; the saviours of `[[D&C 86:11]]` and `[[D&C 103:9–10]]`; the hearts of the fathers in Joseph Smith's 1844 sermon (`[[Mal. 4:5–6]]`, on the site; `[[D&C 128:15]]` if a source read joins it). Link Malachi's chapter rather than explain Elijah here.

**For the author (one question).** How the note on 1:21 says how Obadiah's meaning and the Restoration's fit. *Wording A:* give the ancient sense, then Joseph Smith's teaching that the Saints are “fulfilling the words of Obadiah,” and stop. *Wording B:* the same, then one sentence that one Latter-day Saint scholar compares Joseph Smith's use of the verse to Peter's use of Joel at Pentecost: a prophet applying an ancient promise to the work of his own day, with its fullest fulfillment still ahead. B answers the question a careful reader asks (did Obadiah know of temple work?) from a BYU source, but it can read as “only an application” beside the Bible Dictionary's “refers to.” **Recommended and proposed: B**, said as Pike's reading and after the Church's, since it explains the gap the reader sees without denying the Church's reading; the author may choose A, and the change is one sentence. Filed as OB1 in `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`.
