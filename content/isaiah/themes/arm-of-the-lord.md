“The arm of the Lord” is a way of speaking about His power: the Church’s *Book of Mormon Student Manual* says, “An arm is a symbol of power.”[@bom-student-manual-6] Isaiah uses the Hebrew word for arm (*zerōaʿ*) seventeen times. Thirteen are the Lord’s arm (counting [[Isa. 33:2]], where the people ask Him to be their arm), three are a human arm ([[Isa. 9:20]]; [[Isa. 17:5]]; [[Isa. 44:12]]), and in one, [[Isa. 48:14]], the verse does not say whose arm it is.[@oshb-wlc] The lexicon calls the Lord’s arm an “instrument of deliverance and judgment.”[@bdb]

Followed through the book, the image changes. It begins as the arm that brought Israel out of Egypt. Isaiah promises it again, then it is called on to wake, then it is announced as made bare before all nations. The next chapter asks to whom it has been revealed, and goes on to describe a servant who suffers. Later, when no one else can help, the arm acts alone. Restoration scripture says it will be made bare once more.

The Church’s manuals call the arm a symbol of the Lord’s power, and the Topical Guide entry “Arm” sends the reader on to “Might; Power; Strength.”[@lds-scriptures] Scripture and the Church also read several of these passages of Jesus Christ, and this page follows where they do.

Two more passages are left out of the table. In [[Isa. 30:30]] the arm comes down on Assyria in anger and fire,[@delitzsch] and [[Isa. 48:14]] is the unclear one.

## The arm Israel remembered

In the books of Moses, the Lord’s arm is the arm of the exodus. In Egypt He promises, “I will redeem you with a stretched out arm, and with great judgments” ([[Ex. 6:6]]). After the sea Israel sings, “by the greatness of thine arm they shall be as still as a stone; till thy people pass over” ([[Ex. 15:16]]). Deuteronomy keeps returning to the phrase: “by a mighty hand, and by a stretched out arm” ([[Deut. 4:34]]; also [[Deut. 5:15]]; [[Deut. 26:8]]).[@lds-scriptures] One study says that from the exodus on, “the ‘strong arm’ became a symbol of the Lord’s ‘strength’ and ‘salvation.’”[@schade-bowen-arm-2015]

Isaiah ties the arm to the exodus twice. “Art thou not it which hath dried the sea… that hath made the depths of the sea a way for the ransomed to pass over?” ([[Isa. 51:10]]). Near the end of the book, the people remember: “Then he remembered the days of old, Moses, and his people… That led them by the right hand of Moses with his glorious arm, dividing the water before them” ([[Isa. 63:11–12]]). In the Church’s edition, footnotes at both places send the reader to the parting of the sea in [[Ex. 14:21]].[@lds-scriptures]

## The arm trusted and promised

Early in the book the people pray, “be thou their arm every morning, our salvation also in the time of trouble” ([[Isa. 33:2]]). Then come the promises, and they are tender as well as strong. The Lord “will come with strong hand, and his arm shall rule for him,” and in the next verse the same arm does something else: “he shall gather the lambs with his arm, and carry them in his bosom” ([[Isa. 40:10–11]]). The [chapter page for Isaiah 40](/isaiah/40/) says more about the pairing. In [[Isa. 51:5]] the Lord speaks for Himself: “on mine arm shall they trust.”

## The arm called on to wake

Four verses later the arm itself is addressed: “Awake, awake, put on strength, O arm of the LORD; awake, as in the ancient days” ([[Isa. 51:9]]). The verse does not say who is speaking, and Church sources read it two ways. The *Old Testament Seminary Teacher Material* reads it as the Lord’s people “pleading with the Lord to ‘awake’… or to use His power to help them as He had done in the past.”[@ot-seminary-lesson-130] Delitzsch’s commentary reads it the same way, as a call for a second exodus: the arm “has already miraculously redeemed Israel once,” but “His arm is now in a sleeping state. It is not lifeless, indeed, but motionless.”[@delitzsch] The *Old Testament Student Manual* reads the call instead as one “from God to His latter-day children.”[@ot-manual-17]

The call is then turned on Jerusalem ([[Isa. 51:17]]) and on Zion ([[Isa. 52:1]]): “the same call, which was addressed in Isaiah 51:9 to the arm of Jehovah… is here addressed to Jerusalem.”[@delitzsch] The chapter pages for [Isaiah 51](/isaiah/51/) and [Isaiah 52](/isaiah/52/) treat the three calls in detail.

## The arm made bare

Then comes an announcement: “The LORD hath made bare his holy arm in the eyes of all the nations; and all the ends of the earth shall see the salvation of our God” ([[Isa. 52:10]]). The image is of a warrior who “is accustomed to make bare his right arm up to the shoulder, that he may fight without encumbrance.”[@delitzsch] The Church’s manuals say that making the arm bare means showing His power: “God will show his power to the entire world.”[@bom-student-manual-6][@dc-student-manual-2017-133]

The arm that was called on to act in 51:9 is here said to have acted. Delitzsch describes it as “that arm which has been hitherto concealed and therefore has appeared to be powerless.”[@delitzsch]

<h2 id="revealed">The arm revealed: who has believed?</h2>

The next time the word “arm” appears is at the opening of the next chapter: “Who hath believed our report? and to whom is the arm of the LORD revealed?” ([[Isa. 53:1]]). Shortly before the question, the servant is introduced: he will be “exalted and extolled, and be very high,” and his “visage was so marred more than any man” ([[Isa. 52:13–14]]). What follows the question is a man “despised and rejected of men” ([[Isa. 53:3]]). The Church’s heading for the chapter says, “Isaiah speaks about the Messiah—His humiliation and sufferings are described.”[@lds-scriptures] So 52:10 says that all the nations will see, and 53:1 asks who has believed, and to whom the arm has been revealed.

John says the verse was fulfilled when people did not believe in Jesus: “though he had done so many miracles before them, yet they believed not on him: That the saying of Esaias the prophet might be fulfilled… Lord, who hath believed our report? and to whom hath the arm of the Lord been revealed?” He adds that Isaiah “saw his glory, and spake of him” ([[John 12:37–41]]).

Abinadi comes at the same verses from the other side. The priests of King Noah, trying to find something to accuse him of, ask what Isaiah 52:7–10 means, ending with the bared arm ([[Mosiah 12:19–24]]). He first asks about the law, then says that “God himself should come down among the children of men” and “be oppressed and afflicted” ([[Mosiah 13:34–35]]). Then: “Yea, even doth not Isaiah say: Who hath believed our report, and to whom is the arm of the Lord revealed?” ([[Mosiah 14:1]]), and he quotes all of Isaiah 53. Only after explaining it does he go back to the priests’ verses and quote 52:8–10 himself ([[Mosiah 15:28–31]]).[@lds-scriptures]

One Latter-day Saint study reads this order as deliberate: Abinadi begins “where the priests had left off” and puts their passage “back into its surrounding context.”[@isaiah-in-bom, pp. 295–99] Another says the arm made bare is Abinadi’s proof that salvation would be seen “in a person, not just the law.”[@schade-bowen-arm-2015]

What is “the arm” in 53:1? Latter-day Saint writers differ. It “is often interpreted to mean that the arm of the Lord is revealed in the being of the mortal Messiah”; others take the question to mean “To whom has God revealed his priesthood, his gospel?” or “To whom is the power or might of the Lord revealed?”[@isaiah-in-bom, p. 496] Delitzsch takes the arm as the Lord’s power at work for the servant, “the omnipotence of Jehovah, which has set itself to effect his exaltation.”[@delitzsch] Isaiah does not say more than the question. John, as quoted above, applies it to those who saw Jesus’s miracles and did not believe. The chapter page for [Isaiah 53](/isaiah/53/) and the [Servant Songs page](/isaiah/themes/servant-songs/#song-4) take up the song itself.

<h2 id="alone">The arm that works alone</h2>

Later in the book the Lord looks for help and finds none. “And he saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no intercessor: therefore his arm brought salvation unto him” ([[Isa. 59:16]]). Compare: “And I looked, and there was none to help; and I wondered that there was none to uphold: therefore mine own arm brought salvation unto me” ([[Isa. 63:5]]). In the Church’s edition, the footnotes on these two verses point each to the other.[@lds-scriptures]

The Church reads the first of them of Jesus Christ. The heading of Isaiah 59 says, “The Messiah will intercede,” and a footnote explains the verse: “The Lord brought salvation to man.”[@lds-scriptures] The *Old Testament Student Manual* says verses 16–21 refer “to Jesus Christ, our intercessor with the Father,” and goes on: “Therefore, Jesus was sent to earth. ‘His arm brought [man’s] salvation unto him.’”[@ot-manual-18]

For chapter 63, latter-day revelation takes up the scene of verses 1–4 at the Second Coming: “Who is this that cometh down from God in heaven with dyed garments?… And he shall say: I am he who spake in righteousness, mighty to save” ([[D&C 133:46–47]]); “I have trodden the wine-press alone… and none were with me” ([[D&C 133:50]]). The section does not quote verse 5, the verse about the arm.[@lds-scriptures] The *Doctrine and Covenants Student Manual* says Isaiah “prophesied that when Jesus Christ comes again, He will be ‘red in [His] apparel.’”[@dc-student-manual-2017-133] The chapter pages for [Isaiah 59](/isaiah/59/) and [Isaiah 63](/isaiah/63/) cover the armor and the winepress.

The Church’s footnote on “arm” at Isaiah 59:16 sends the reader to the Book of Mormon’s arms of mercy and love: [[2 Ne. 1:15]], [[Jacob 6:5]], [[Alma 5:33]] and [[3 Ne. 9:14]], and each of those verses has a footnote pointing back to Isaiah 59:16.[@lds-scriptures] In the last of them Jesus says, “mine arm of mercy is extended towards you, and whosoever will come, him will I receive.” In the Doctrine and Covenants He speaks of His own arm: “listen to the words of Jesus Christ, your Lord and your Redeemer. For behold, I speak unto you with sharpness and with power, for mine arm is over all the earth” ([[D&C 15:1–2]]); and “Jesus Christ, your Redeemer, the Great I Am, whose arm of mercy hath atoned for your sins” ([[D&C 29:1]]).

## The arm still to be revealed

Restoration scripture uses Isaiah’s phrases for what is still ahead. Nephi says that “the Lord God will proceed to make bare his arm in the eyes of all the nations, in bringing about his covenants and his gospel unto those who are of the house of Israel” ([[1 Ne. 22:11]]). The risen Savior quotes Isaiah 52:10 with changes: “The Father hath made bare his holy arm in the eyes of all the nations; and all the ends of the earth shall see the salvation of the Father; and the Father and I are one” ([[3 Ne. 20:35]]). Where Isaiah has “The LORD” and “our God,” this has “The Father” and “the Father; and the Father and I are one.”[@lds-scriptures] In the Doctrine and Covenants, “the arm of the Lord shall be revealed” ([[D&C 1:14]]), and “he shall make bare his holy arm in the eyes of all the nations” ([[D&C 133:3]]). The *Doctrine and Covenants Student Manual* says that this baring “may refer to the power and glory that will be manifested at Jesus Christ’s Second Coming” and “may also refer to the Lord’s great latter-day work of gathering Israel.”[@dc-student-manual-2017-133]

## How the arc points to Christ

The arm is the Lord’s power to save, seen at the exodus. The *Old Testament Student Manual*, on the verses where the Lord says “on mine arm shall they trust,” says they “bear strong witness that Jehovah, the God of the Old Testament, is the same person as Jesus Christ of the New.”[@ot-manual-17] Isaiah introduces the suffering servant soon after the arm is made bare, and John and Abinadi read that passage of Jesus Christ. The Church reads Isaiah 59:16, where the Lord’s arm brought salvation when there was no intercessor, of Him as well. And He speaks of His own arm: “Behold, mine arm of mercy is extended towards you” ([[3 Ne. 9:14]]).
