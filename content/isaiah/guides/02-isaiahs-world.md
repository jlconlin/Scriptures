Nephi said that one reason he could understand Isaiah was that he knew “the regions round about” ([[2 Ne. 25:6]]). He had grown up in Jerusalem. He knew why Egypt was tempting, why Assyria was terrifying, and why Babylon was a byword. This guide tries to give you some of what Nephi knew.

## The big picture in one paragraph

Isaiah lived in **Jerusalem**, capital of the small kingdom of **Judah**, in the second half of the 700s BC. To the north was the larger kingdom of **Israel**, often called **Ephraim** after its leading tribe, with its capital at **Samaria**. Both kingdoms were squeezed between two great powers: **Egypt** to the southwest and **Assyria** to the northeast. During Isaiah’s ministry, Assyria conquered Israel and scattered the ten tribes (722 BC), then invaded Judah and besieged Jerusalem (701 BC). The Lord delivered Jerusalem that time. But Isaiah also foresaw a later empire, **Babylon**, which would destroy Jerusalem and carry Judah into exile (587 BC), and a Persian king, **Cyrus**, who would let the exiles go home (538 BC).

## The kings on the thrones

**Judah (the southern kingdom, capital Jerusalem):**

- **Uzziah / Azariah** (c. 792–740). Long, prosperous reign; struck with leprosy for usurping priestly duties ([[2 Chr. 26]]).
- **Jotham** (c. 750–732). A mostly good king ([[2 Kgs. 15:32–38]]).
- **Ahaz** (c. 735–715). Faithless and idolatrous. Refused Isaiah’s sign and made Judah an Assyrian vassal ([[Isa. 7]]; [[2 Kgs. 16]]).
- **Hezekiah** (c. 715–686). Reformer. Trusted the Lord in the Assyrian crisis; Isaiah’s friend and counselor ([[Isa. 36–39]]).
- **Manasseh** (c. 686–642). Undid his father’s reforms. Tradition holds that Isaiah was killed in his reign ([[2 Kgs. 21:1–16]]).

**Israel (the northern kingdom, capital Samaria):** In Isaiah’s day it went through six kings in about 25 years, most of them assassinated. The ones who appear in Isaiah are **Pekah**, “the son of Remaliah” ([[Isa. 7:1–9]]), and, by implication, **Hoshea**, the last king.

**Assyria:** **Tiglath-pileser III** (745–727), **Shalmaneser V** (727–722), **Sargon II** (722–705; named in [[Isa. 20:1]]), **Sennacherib** (705–681; [[Isa. 36–37]]), **Esarhaddon** (681–669; [[Isa. 37:38]]).

**Babylon:** **Merodach-baladan** (a Chaldean rebel against Assyria; [[Isa. 39:1]]), and a century later **Nebuchadnezzar**, who destroyed Jerusalem.

**Persia:** **Cyrus the Great** (559–530), named by Isaiah about 150 years before his birth ([[Isa. 44:28]]; [[Isa. 45:1]]).

## Who’s who among the nations

| Name in Isaiah | Who they were | Why they matter |
|---|---|---|
| **Ephraim**, Israel, Samaria | The northern kingdom (ten tribes) | Isaiah’s own brothers in the covenant, destroyed by Assyria in 722 BC |
| **Syria**, Aram, Damascus | Aramean kingdom north of Israel; king Rezin | Allied with Ephraim against Judah (Isa. 7) |
| **Assyria**, Asshur | The superpower, capital Nineveh | The Lord’s “rod” of discipline ([[Isa. 10:5]]), then judged for its pride |
| **Babylon**, Chaldeans | Mesopotamian power that succeeded Assyria | Destroyed Jerusalem; becomes the symbol of the proud world |
| **Egypt** | Ancient power on the Nile, ruled by Cushite pharaohs in Isaiah’s day | The tempting but useless ally ([[Isa. 30:1–7]]; [[Isa. 31:1]]) |
| **Cush**, Ethiopia | Kingdom south of Egypt (modern Sudan) | Its king Tirhakah marched against Sennacherib ([[Isa. 37:9]]) |
| **Philistia**, Palestina | Coastal cities (Gaza, Ashdod, and others) | Old enemies of Israel ([[Isa. 14:29–32]]) |
| **Moab** | East of the Dead Sea | Proud neighbor; Isaiah weeps over its fall ([[Isa. 15–16]]) |
| **Edom**, Idumea, Seir, Bozrah | South of the Dead Sea; descendants of Esau | A symbol of the world under judgment ([[Isa. 34]]; [[Isa. 63:1]]; [[D&C 1:36]]) |
| **Tyre** and **Sidon** | Phoenician port cities | Wealthy traders, a picture of commercial pride ([[Isa. 23]]) |
| **Tarshish** | A distant port, probably in the far west Mediterranean | “Ships of Tarshish” are the great ocean-going merchant ships |
| **Media**, **Elam** | Peoples east of Mesopotamia | Joined in Babylon’s overthrow ([[Isa. 13:17]]; [[Isa. 21:2]]) |
| **Persia** | Empire of Cyrus | Liberated the Jewish exiles |

## Everyday things Isaiah assumes you know

- **Vineyards.** Hillside plots cleared of stones, terraced, walled, and guarded from a watchtower, with a winepress cut into the rock ([[Isa. 5:1–2]]). Isaiah’s audience knew exactly how much work a vineyard was.
- **Threshing floors.** Flat, windy places where grain was beaten and tossed into the air so the chaff blew away ([[Isa. 41:15–16]]).
- **City gates.** Where elders sat to judge cases. “Justice in the gate” was the measure of a city ([[Isa. 29:21]]).
- **Watchmen.** Men on walls or towers who watched for enemies or messengers ([[Isa. 21:6–12]]; [[Isa. 52:8]]; [[Isa. 62:6]]).
- **Cisterns and pools.** Jerusalem’s water supply was a matter of life and death in a siege. Hezekiah carved a tunnel through bedrock to bring water inside the city walls ([[2 Kgs. 20:20]]), and an inscription carved by the workmen still survives.
- **High places and groves.** Local shrines on hilltops, often with a sacred pole or tree, where Israelites mixed the worship of Jehovah with fertility gods.
- **Ensigns.** Banners or poles raised on a hill so scattered people could see where to gather ([[Isa. 5:26]]; [[Isa. 11:12]]; [[Isa. 18:3]]).

## Archaeology that illuminates Isaiah

- **Sennacherib’s Prism** records the Assyrian king’s version of the 701 BC campaign. He boasts of taking forty-six walled cities of Judah and shutting Hezekiah in Jerusalem “like a bird in a cage.” He does *not* claim to have taken Jerusalem, which fits [[Isa. 37:33–37]].
- **The Lachish reliefs** in Sennacherib’s palace at Nineveh show the siege and capture of Lachish, the Judean fortress mentioned in [[Isa. 36:2]].
- **Hezekiah’s Tunnel** and its **Siloam Inscription** preserve the water tunnel of [[2 Kgs. 20:20]].
- **The Cyrus Cylinder** records Cyrus’s policy of returning displaced peoples and restoring their sanctuaries, the policy that let the Jews go home.
- **The Great Isaiah Scroll**, found among the Dead Sea Scrolls in 1947, is a complete copy of Isaiah made in the second century BC. It is about a thousand years older than the previously oldest Hebrew manuscripts, and it is remarkably close to the text behind our Bible. You can page through it online at the Israel Museum’s Digital Dead Sea Scrolls site.
