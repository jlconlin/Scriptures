Chapters 40–55 of Isaiah speak often of the Lord’s **servant** (*ʿebed*). Usually the servant is plainly the nation of Israel: “thou, Israel, art my servant, Jacob whom I have chosen” ([[Isa. 41:8]]). But four passages describe a servant who seems to be an individual, with a mission *to* Israel as well as to the nations, and who suffers for others. Since the end of the nineteenth century these passages have been called the **Servant Songs**.[@duhm] The name is modern; nothing in the text marks them off. But the songs do form a sequence, which reading them one at a time can hide.

## Where the songs are

Where each song begins is agreed, but not always where it ends, because the verses that follow a song respond to it.[@westermann][@blenkinsopp] The passages below include those verses, so nothing is left out.

## How the songs build

Read in order, the songs tell a story. The servant’s lot grows harder from song to song: in the first he is gentle and unbroken; in the second he is discouraged; in the third he is beaten and spat upon; in the fourth he dies. His mission grows at the same time: justice for the nations, then the gathering of Israel and light to the Gentiles, then a word for the weary, and finally bearing “the sin of many.” And the songs move from speaking *about* the servant to letting him speak for himself, and then to the confession of those who finally understand him.

<h2 id="the-songs">The four songs</h2>

<h3 id="song-1">The first song: the gentle servant</h3>

**[[Isa. 42:1–9]].** The Lord presents His chosen one, “mine elect, in whom my soul delighteth,” sent to “bring forth judgment to the Gentiles.” He is quiet (“a bruised reed shall he not break”) and unbroken: “He shall not fail nor be discouraged.” Later in the chapter Israel, the servant nation, is “blind” and “deaf” ([[Isa. 42:19]]); the servant of the song will not fail as Israel has.[@oswalt]

<h3 id="song-2">The second song: a light to the Gentiles</h3>

**[[Isa. 49:1–13]].** Now the servant speaks for himself, and for the first time he is discouraged: “I have laboured in vain” (verse 4). The Lord answers by enlarging his task beyond restoring Israel, to be “a light to the Gentiles” (verse 6). Here is the puzzle at the center of the debate: the servant is called “Israel” (verse 3), yet sent “to bring Jacob again to him” (verse 5).[@paul-40-66]

<h3 id="song-3">The third song: the servant who listens</h3>

**[[Isa. 50:4–11]].** The servant, wakened every morning to hear the Lord, now suffers for what he hears: “I gave my back to the smiters… I hid not my face from shame and spitting” (verse 6). He does not turn back, but sets his face like flint. The word “servant” appears only in verse 10, “the voice of his servant.”

<h3 id="song-4">The fourth song: the suffering servant</h3>

**[[Isa. 52:13–53:12]].** The longest song opens with the servant’s exaltation, in words Isaiah elsewhere uses of God (compare [[Isa. 6:1]]). Then a chorus confesses that they despised him, only to learn “he was wounded for our transgressions.” He dies and is buried, yet “he shall see his seed.” The chapter pages for [Isaiah 52](/isaiah/52/) and [Isaiah 53](/isaiah/53/) comment on it verse by verse.

<h2 id="who-is-the-servant">Who is the servant?</h2>

When the Ethiopian official read the fourth song, he asked Philip, “of whom speaketh the prophet this? of himself, or of some other man?” ([[Acts 8:34]]). Readers have given three main answers.

- **Israel as a people.** Isaiah calls Israel the servant many times ([[Isa. 41:8]]; [[Isa. 44:1]]; [[Isa. 49:3]]), and the nation did suffer in exile. On this reading, the “we” of chapter 53 are the nations, astonished to learn that the people they despised suffered for their sake. This has been the main Jewish interpretation since at least the Middle Ages, and a Jewish scholar was already arguing for it in the third century.[@origen-celsum]
- **An individual in Isaiah’s world**, such as the prophet himself, a persecuted prophet like Jeremiah (who called himself “like a lamb or an ox that is brought to the slaughter,” [[Jer. 11:19]]), or a suffering king. One proposal ties the songs to King Hezekiah’s near-fatal illness ([[Isa. 38]]). It is one theory among many, though it has the merit of placing the songs in Isaiah’s own lifetime.[@sc-knowhy-648]
- **The Messiah.** The ancient Aramaic paraphrase of Isaiah, the Targum, begins the fourth song with “my servant the Messiah.”[@chilton-targum] The New Testament identifies the servant with Jesus.

Each reading explains some features of the songs and struggles with others. The collective reading fits verse 3 of the second song (“Thou art my servant, O Israel”) but struggles with verse 5, where the servant is sent to Israel, and with the fourth song, where he is stricken “for the transgression of my people” ([[Isa. 53:8]]), is innocent, and dies and yet lives on.

Latter-day Saints read the songs first of all as prophecies of Jesus Christ, as the Book of Mormon does. But the Restoration is also comfortable with prophecy that is fulfilled more than once, and Nephi taught his people to “liken all scriptures unto us” ([[1 Ne. 19:23]]). Read that way, the servant is a pattern. Israel was called to be God’s servant and a light to the nations. Jesus Christ fulfilled that calling perfectly, as the one faithful Israelite who bore the sins of others. And those who follow Him share in the calling, as Paul and Barnabas did when they applied the second song to themselves ([[Acts 13:47]]). The Savior Himself applied the language of the fourth song to a latter-day servant who would be “marred” and healed ([[3 Ne. 21:10]]). Latter-day Saints have usually taken that servant to be Joseph Smith; others have read it as Christ, or as the Book of Mormon itself.[@sc-knowhy-215] The text does not settle which.

<h2 id="servants">From servant to servants</h2>

After the fourth song the word changes. In chapters 41–53 “servant” (*ʿebed*) appears twenty times, always singular, the last being “my righteous servant” ([[Isa. 53:11]]); from there to the end of the book it appears eleven times, always plural, beginning with “This is the heritage of the servants of the LORD” ([[Isa. 54:17]]).[@oshb-wlc] The note on Isaiah 54:17 on the [chapter page](/isaiah/54/) takes up how the servants are related to the servant.

<h2 id="fifth-song">A fifth song?</h2>

[[Isa. 61:1–3]] is sometimes added to the list: “The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me; because the LORD hath anointed me to preach good tidings unto the meek.” The speaker is not called a servant, but his anointing by the Spirit echoes the first song. Jesus read this passage in the synagogue at Nazareth and announced, “This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears” ([[Luke 4:16–21]]).
