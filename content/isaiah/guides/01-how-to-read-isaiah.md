If Isaiah has ever made your eyes glaze over, you’re in good company. Nephi’s own people found him hard: “Isaiah spake many things which were hard for many of my people to understand” ([[2 Ne. 25:1]]). Nephi didn’t respond by skipping Isaiah. He gave them **keys**. The Savior, for His part, commanded us to “search these things diligently; for great are the words of Isaiah” ([[3 Ne. 23:1]]).

This guide gathers the keys that help most. None of them are secret. They are habits of reading that, once learned, turn Isaiah from a fog into a landscape.

## Key 1: Nephi’s four keys

In [[2 Ne. 25:1–8]], Nephi explains why Isaiah is hard and what makes him plain. Read those verses slowly; they are a good introduction to Isaiah. Nephi names four things:

1. **“The manner of prophesying among the Jews”** (verse 1). Isaiah writes in the forms of ancient Hebrew prophecy: poetic style, imagery and figurative language, allusions to the law of Moses, and phrases with more than one meaning.[@ot-manual-e] Nephi’s people, raised far from Jerusalem, didn’t know those forms. Keys 2–5 below are about learning them.
2. **“The regions round about”** (verse 6). Nephi had lived in Jerusalem and knew the geography and the nations Isaiah names. We have to learn them, because Isaiah uses places both literally and as figures.[@ot-manual-e] See [Isaiah’s World](/isaiah/guides/isaiahs-world/).
3. **“The spirit of prophecy”** (verse 4). Isaiah’s words “are plain unto all those that are filled with the spirit of prophecy.” John defines that spirit: “the testimony of Jesus is the spirit of prophecy” ([[Rev. 19:10]]). Isaiah’s writings must be interpreted under the influence of that same spirit.[@ot-manual-e]
4. **Living in the last days** (verses 7–8). Nephi promised that “in the last days… shall they understand them.” Many of Isaiah’s prophecies concern our time: the gathering of Israel, the coming forth of a book, an ensign to the nations, the temple in the tops of the mountains.[@mcconkie-keys]

Elder Bruce R. McConkie gave ten keys of his own. He urged readers to know the plan of salvation and the destiny of the house of Israel, to use the Book of Mormon and latter-day revelation, to see how the New Testament reads Isaiah, to study the Old Testament setting, to learn the Jewish manner of prophesying, to seek the spirit of prophecy, and to devote themselves to hard, conscientious study.[@mcconkie-keys] His article is well worth reading in full.

## Key 2: Read it as poetry

Isaiah is written in a poetic style,[@ot-manual-e] and Hebrew poetry doesn’t rhyme sounds. It rhymes **ideas**. The basic unit is a pair of lines in which the second line echoes, sharpens, or contrasts the first. This is called *parallelism*, or “thought-rhythm.”[@ot-manual-g] Once you hear it, Isaiah becomes far easier.

**The second line restates the first:**

> Hear, O heavens, / and give ear, O earth ([[Isa. 1:2]])

These aren’t two separate commands to two audiences. They are one summons said twice, with the second line widening the frame.[@ot-manual-g]

**The second line intensifies the first:**

> though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow; / though they be red like crimson, they shall be as wool ([[Isa. 1:18]])

**The second line contrasts with the first:**

> The grass withereth, the flower fadeth: / but the word of our God shall stand for ever ([[Isa. 40:8]])

**The lines build step by step:**

> they shall mount up with wings as eagles; / they shall run, and not be weary; / and they shall walk, and not faint ([[Isa. 40:31]])

<div class="callout">

**A practical tip:** When a verse confuses you, find its partner line. Isaiah often explains a hard line with the one right next to it. In “he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities” ([[Isa. 53:5]]), *wounded* and *bruised* explain each other, and so do *transgressions* and *iniquities*.

**Chiasmus.** Sometimes ideas are arranged in a mirror pattern (A-B-C-C′-B′-A′), also called inverted parallelism.[@ot-manual-g] A short example is [[Isa. 6:10]]:

> Make the **heart** of this people fat,
> and make their **ears** heavy,
> and shut their **eyes**;
> lest they see with their **eyes**,
> and hear with their **ears**,
> and understand with their **heart**…


## Key 3: Know which “horizon” you are looking at

Isaiah moves between several time periods, often without warning, because some of his writings can apply to more than one situation or be fulfilled at more than one time:[@ot-manual-e]

| Horizon | Rough dates | Typical signals |
|---|---|---|
| **Isaiah’s own day** | 740–701 BC[@bd-isaiah] | Assyria, Ahaz, Hezekiah, Ephraim, Syria |
| **The Babylonian exile and return** | 586–536 BC[@bd-captivities] | Babylon, Cyrus, “comfort,” the way through the wilderness |
| **The Savior’s mortal ministry** | about 700 years after Isaiah[@mcconkie-keys] | the Servant, Immanuel, the Anointed One, the Man of Sorrows |
| **The last days and the Millennium** | our day and beyond | the ensign, gathering, Zion, “in that day,” new heavens and new earth |

One passage can speak to more than one horizon. In chapters 13–14, Babylon is both the nation and the wickedness of the world that the nation epitomized.[@ot-manual-e] Cyrus captured the nation,[@bd-cyrus] and the figure of Babylon also describes the downfall of “Babylon” the wicked world at the Second Coming ([[D&C 1:16]]; [[D&C 133:14]]). The Book of Mormon calls this *likening*; it is also called *dualism*.[@ot-manual-e] Nephi says “all things which have been given of God from the beginning of the world, unto man, are the typifying of him” ([[2 Ne. 11:4]]). A good question to ask of any Isaiah passage is: *Where else has this pattern happened, and where will it happen again?*

## Key 4: The “prophetic perfect”

Hebrew prophets sometimes describe future events in the past tense, as though they had already happened. The prophet has seen it, so it is as good as done. Isaiah 53 is full of past-tense verbs: “he **was** wounded… he **was** oppressed.” In “For unto us a child **is born**” ([[Isa. 9:6]]), the prophet sees as born the one he foretold in chapter 7.[@delitzsch, on Isa. 9:6] That was some seven hundred years before His birth.[@mcconkie-keys] Don’t let the tense throw you. Ask what the prophet saw, not when the verb says it happened.

## Key 5: Listen for the change of speaker

Isaiah’s text has no quotation marks. In a single chapter Isaiah may shift among the Lord speaking, Isaiah speaking, Zion answering, the nations mocking, and the Servant speaking about Himself. Isaiah 49 is a good example: the Servant speaks (verses 1–6), then the Lord (verses 7–13), then Zion complains (verse 14), and the Lord answers (verses 15–26). When a verse seems to contradict the one before it, ask: *Who is talking now?* The chapter pages on this site flag these shifts in the plain-words summaries.

## Key 6: Learn a few symbols

Isaiah uses a vocabulary of images that recur throughout the book. A few to start with:

- **The mountain of the Lord.** The temple, and more generally the places where God’s power and authority reside ([[Isa. 2:2–3]]).[@ot-manual-e]
- **The vineyard.** Israel, the Lord’s covenant people ([[Isa. 5:1–7]]).
- **Tall trees** (cedars of Lebanon, oaks of Bashan). The proud, who will be cut down ([[Isa. 2:13]]; [[Isa. 10:33–34]]).[@ot-manual-e]
- **An ensign.** A flag or standard around which people gather ([[Isa. 5:26]]; [[Isa. 11:12]]).[@gs-ensign]
- **Babylon.** The nation of Babylon and the wickedness of the world it epitomized ([[Isa. 13]]; [[D&C 133:14]]).[@ot-manual-e]
- **Zion.** The Lord’s pure and gathered people, and the place where they dwell ([[D&C 97:21]]).
- **The desert blossoming.** Latter-day revelation in D&C 133 opens Isaiah 35 to view ([[Isa. 35:1]]).[@mcconkie-keys]

## Key 7: Read Isaiah with the Book of Mormon open

The Book of Mormon quotes about a third of Isaiah and paraphrases a little more.[@mcconkie-keys] It also **interprets** him. Nephi explains Isaiah 48–49 in [[1 Ne. 22]]. Jacob explains Isaiah 49–52 in [[2 Ne. 6–10]]. Nephi comments on Isaiah 2–14 and 29 in [[2 Ne. 25–30]]. Abinadi explains Isaiah 53 in [[Mosiah 15]]. The Savior Himself quotes and explains Isaiah 52 and 54 in [[3 Ne. 16]] and [[3 Ne. 20–22]]. The Book of Mormon versions sometimes differ from the King James text in illuminating ways. See [Isaiah and the Restoration](/isaiah/guides/isaiah-and-the-restoration/).

## Key 8: Watch out for 1611 English

Some confusion comes from the King James English, not from Isaiah. A few words that have changed meaning:

| KJV word | Means |
|---|---|
| *crib* | manger, feeding trough ([[Isa. 1:3]])[@webster-1828] |
| *let* | hinder, prevent ([[Isa. 43:13]])[@bd-let] |
| *prevent* | go before, anticipate[@webster-1828] |
| *conversation* | conduct, way of life[@webster-1828] |
| *meat* | food of any kind[@webster-1828] |
| *suffer* | allow[@webster-1828] |
| *quick* | alive[@webster-1828] |
| *bowels* | the seat of pity and compassion ([[Isa. 63:15]])[@webster-1828] |
| *vex* | harass, trouble[@webster-1828] |

## Key 9: Liken, and look for Christ

Nephi names two purposes for reading Isaiah: to “liken all scriptures unto us, that it might be for our profit and learning” ([[1 Ne. 19:23]]), and to persuade his people to “believe in the Lord their Redeemer” ([[1 Ne. 19:23]]; [[2 Ne. 11:2–6]]). He said his soul delighted “in proving unto my people the truth of the coming of Christ” ([[2 Ne. 11:4]]). Every chapter page on this site includes a **Seeing Christ** section for that reason. See [Isaiah’s Witness of Christ](/isaiah/guides/isaiah-and-christ/).

## A word about “Second Isaiah”

If you read modern commentaries, you will quickly meet the idea that the book of Isaiah had more than one author. Many text-critical scholars hold that the disputed chapters, mainly 40–66, were written or edited after the Babylonian deportation of the sixth century BC, and chapters 56–66 are argued most strongly to date from after the exile.[@isaiah-in-bom, 423, 432–33] The main arguments concern differences in form and content and the problem of foreknowledge, above all the naming of Cyrus ([[Isa. 44:28]]; [[Isa. 45:1]]).[@isaiah-in-bom, 425–29]

Most Latter-day Saint writers read the book as Isaiah’s prophecy, for reasons like these:[@isaiah-in-bom, 423–24]

- Lehi’s family carried the **brass plates** out of Jerusalem around 600 BC, before the exile. Nephi quotes Isaiah 48–49 from those plates ([[1 Ne. 20–21]]), and Jacob quotes chapters 50–51 ([[2 Ne. 7–8]]). Those chapters were already on the plates before Babylon conquered Jerusalem.[@isaiah-in-bom, 423, 434]
- The **Savior** quoted Isaiah 52 and 54 and attributed them to Isaiah ([[3 Ne. 20:11]]; [[3 Ne. 22]]; [[3 Ne. 23:1–3]]). New Testament writers likewise attribute passages from chapters 40 and 53 to Isaiah ([[Matt. 3:3]]; [[John 12:38–41]]).[@isaiah-in-bom, 424–25]
- Latter-day Saints believe that God reveals the future to His prophets ([[Amos 3:7]]). Naming Cyrus in advance is exactly what Isaiah says the Lord does to prove He is God: “declaring the end from the beginning” ([[Isa. 46:9–10]]).[@isaiah-in-bom, 429–30]

Two cautions belong with these reasons. The New Testament references are helpful but “do not settle the question of authorship,” since they name the book as a whole without dating each part of it.[@isaiah-in-bom, 425] And the Book of Mormon requires only the chapters it quotes or uses to be older than 600 BC; it is possible that some parts of Isaiah were missing from the brass plates.[@isaiah-in-bom, 432]

That doesn’t mean the observations behind the scholarly theory are worthless: critical scholars may have correctly identified real differences in the text, and the question is how best to explain them.[@isaiah-in-bom, 428] A Latter-day Saint reader can explain them by Isaiah’s inspired view of a future he was shown.[@isaiah-in-bom, 429–30]

## Where to start

If you’re new to Isaiah, don’t start at chapter 1 and grind forward. Try this sampler first, then go back and read in order:

1. **Isaiah 53**: the Suffering Servant.
2. **Isaiah 40**: “Comfort ye my people.”
3. **Isaiah 6**: Isaiah’s call in the temple.
4. **Isaiah 11**: the stem of Jesse and the gathering. Moroni quoted it to Joseph Smith ([[JS—H 1:40]]).
5. **Isaiah 29**: the sealed book and a marvelous work.
6. **Isaiah 61**: the chapter Jesus read in Nazareth ([[Luke 4:16–21]]).
7. **Isaiah 2**: the mountain of the Lord’s house.

Then read [Isaiah’s World](/isaiah/guides/isaiahs-world/) and begin with [Isaiah 1](/isaiah/1/).
