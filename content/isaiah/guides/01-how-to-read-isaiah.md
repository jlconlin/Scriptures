Nephi’s own people found Isaiah hard: “Isaiah spake many things which were hard for many of my people to understand” ([[2 Ne. 25:1]]). Nephi didn’t respond by skipping Isaiah. He gave them **keys**. The Savior, for His part, commanded us to “search these things diligently; for great are the words of Isaiah” ([[3 Ne. 23:1]]).

This guide gathers the keys that help most: habits of reading that, once learned, turn Isaiah from a fog into a landscape.

## Key 1: Nephi’s four keys

In [[2 Ne. 25:1–8]], Nephi explains why Isaiah is hard and what makes him plain. He names four things:

1. **“The manner of prophesying among the Jews”** (verse 1). Isaiah writes in the forms of Hebrew prophecy: poetic style, imagery and figurative language, allusions to the law of Moses, and phrases with more than one meaning.[@ot-manual-e] Keys 2–5 below are about learning them.
2. **“The regions round about”** (verse 6). Isaiah uses places both literally and as figures.[@ot-manual-e] See [Two Kingdoms and Assyria](/old-testament/guides/two-kingdoms-and-assyria/#nations).
3. **“The spirit of prophecy”** (verse 4). Isaiah’s words “are plain unto all those that are filled with the spirit of prophecy,” and “the testimony of Jesus is the spirit of prophecy” ([[Rev. 19:10]]). Isaiah’s writings must be interpreted under the influence of that same spirit.[@ot-manual-e]
4. **Living in the last days** (verses 7–8). Nephi promised that “in the last days… shall they understand them.” Many of Isaiah’s prophecies concern our time: the gathering of Israel, the coming forth of a book, an ensign to the nations, the temple in the tops of the mountains.[@mcconkie-keys]

Elder Bruce R. McConkie added ten keys of his own, among them reading Isaiah with the Book of Mormon and latter-day revelation.[@mcconkie-keys]

## Key 2: Read it as poetry

Isaiah is written in a poetic style,[@ot-manual-e] and its basic unit is a pair of lines in which the second line echoes, sharpens, or contrasts the first; [Hebrew Poetry](/old-testament/guides/hebrew-poetry/#parallelism) explains the forms. When a verse confuses you, find its partner line. In “he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities” ([[Isa. 53:5]]), *wounded* and *bruised* explain each other, and so do *transgressions* and *iniquities*.

## Key 3: Know which “horizon” you are looking at

Isaiah moves between time periods, often without warning, because some of his writings can apply to more than one situation or be fulfilled at more than one time:[@ot-manual-e]

| Horizon | Rough dates | Typical signals |
|---|---|---|
| **Isaiah’s own day** | 740–701 BC[@bd-isaiah] | Assyria, Ahaz, Hezekiah, Ephraim, Syria |
| **The Babylonian exile and return** | 586–536 BC[@bd-captivities] | Babylon, Cyrus, “comfort,” the way through the wilderness |
| **The Savior’s mortal ministry** | about 700 years after Isaiah[@mcconkie-keys] | the Servant, Immanuel, the Anointed One, the Man of Sorrows |
| **The last days and the Millennium** | our day and beyond | the ensign, gathering, Zion, “in that day,” new heavens and new earth |

One passage can speak to more than one horizon. In chapters 13–14, Babylon is both the nation and the wickedness of the world that the nation epitomized.[@ot-manual-e] The Book of Mormon calls this *likening*; it is also called *dualism*.[@ot-manual-e] A good question to ask of any Isaiah passage is: *Where else has this pattern happened, and where will it happen again?*

## Key 4: The “prophetic perfect”

Hebrew prophets sometimes describe future events in the past tense, as though they had already happened. Isaiah 53 is full of past-tense verbs: “he **was** wounded… he **was** oppressed.” In “For unto us a child **is born**” ([[Isa. 9:6]]), the prophet sees as born the one he foretold in chapter 7.[@delitzsch, on Isa. 9:6] Don’t let the tense throw you. Ask what the prophet saw, not when the verb says it happened.

## Key 5: Listen for the change of speaker

Isaiah’s text has no quotation marks, and the speaker can change without warning. In Isaiah 49 the Servant speaks (verses 1–6), then the Lord (verses 7–13), then Zion complains (verse 14), and the Lord answers (verses 15–26). When a verse seems to contradict the one before it, ask: *Who is talking now?*

## Key 6: Learn a few images

Isaiah uses a vocabulary of images that recur throughout the book. A few to start with:

- **Arm of the Lord.** His power in action: “The LORD hath made bare his holy arm” ([[Isa. 52:10]]), where “made bare” means to “show strength.”[@isaiah-in-bom, p. 421]
- **Blind and deaf.** Spiritual blindness and insensitivity: people who see but do not perceive ([[Isa. 6:9]]; [[Isa. 42:19]]).[@isaiah-in-bom, p. 418]
- **Branch.** A shoot from a stump ([[Isa. 11:1]]); Elder McConkie taught that the Branch who grew out of the house of David is Christ.[@isaiah-in-bom, p. 13]
- **Burden.** Hebrew *massaʾ*, a load and also a prophetic utterance, or oracle,[@bdb, *massaʾ*] the word Isaiah uses for his oracles on the nations ([[Isa. 13:1]]).[@isaiah-in-bom, p. 72]
- **Ensign.** A flag or standard around which people gather ([[Isa. 5:26]]; [[Isa. 11:12]]); the Book of Mormon and the Church are symbolic ensigns to all nations.[@gs-ensign]
- **Hand stretched out still.** A refrain of continuing judgment ([[Isa. 9:12]]); the Book of Mormon pictures mercy instead with the Lord’s arm “lengthened out” toward all who will turn ([[2 Ne. 28:32]]).[@gee-hand-lord]
- **The mountain of the Lord.** The temple ([[Isa. 2:2–3]]),[@isaiah-in-bom, p. 409] and more generally the places where God’s power and authority reside.[@ot-manual-e]
- **Remnant.** The portion that remains after judgment,[@isaiah-in-bom, p. 415] summoned and gathered again in the day of the Gentiles.[@isaiah-in-bom, p. 36]
- **Stakes and cords.** The pegs and ropes of a tent ([[Isa. 54:2]]), the image behind the Church’s term “stake.”[@gs-stake]
- **Stone of stumbling.** The Lord is a “sanctuary” for some, but a “stone of stumbling” for others ([[Isa. 8:14]]).[@isaiah-in-bom, p. 35]
- **Tall trees** (cedars of Lebanon, oaks of Bashan). The proud, who will be cut down ([[Isa. 2:13]]; [[Isa. 10:33–34]]).[@ot-manual-e]
- **“That day.”** The “day of the Lord” ([[Isa. 2:12]]), often pointing to the last days.[@isaiah-in-bom, p. 61]
- **Vineyard.** Israel, planted and tended by the Lord ([[Isa. 5:7]]); compare Zenos’s allegory of the olive tree in [[Jacob 5]].
- **Winepress.** Treading grapes, a picture of judgment ([[Isa. 63:3]]); compare [[D&C 76:107]].
- **Zion.** The Lord’s pure and gathered people, and the place where they dwell ([[D&C 97:21]]).

## Key 7: Read Isaiah with the Book of Mormon open

The Book of Mormon quotes Isaiah at length and explains him, sometimes in a text that differs from the King James; see [Isaiah and the Restoration](/isaiah/guides/isaiah-and-the-restoration/).

## Key 8: Watch out for 1611 English

Some confusion comes from the King James English, not from Isaiah. A few words that have changed meaning:

| KJV word | Means |
|---|---|
| *crib* | manger, feeding trough ([[Isa. 1:3]])[@webster-1828] |
| *let* | hinder, prevent ([[Isa. 43:13]])[@bd-let] |
| *prevent* | go before, anticipate[@webster-1828] |
| *conversation* | conduct, way of life[@webster-1828] |
| *meat* | food of any kind[@webster-1828] |
| *suffer* | allow[@webster-1828] |
| *quick* | alive[@webster-1828] |
| *bowels* | the seat of pity and compassion ([[Isa. 63:15]])[@webster-1828] |
| *vex* | harass, trouble[@webster-1828] |

## Key 9: The LORD is Jehovah, and Jehovah is Christ

The most important key to Christ in Isaiah is one the Restoration makes plain. **The LORD of the Old Testament, Jehovah, is Jesus Christ in His premortal role.**[@bd-jehovah] In the King James Bible, “LORD” in small capitals stands for the divine name *YHWH* (Jehovah).[@bd-jehovah] When Isaiah says “the LORD,” he usually means the God who would come to earth as Jesus of Nazareth. The Savior taught this to the Nephites: “I am he that gave the law, and I am he who covenanted with my people Israel” ([[3 Ne. 15:5]]).

## The Great Isaiah Scroll

The Great Isaiah Scroll, found among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran in 1947, is an almost complete copy of Isaiah dating to about 125 BC, some thousand years older than the oldest Hebrew manuscripts of the Bible known before its discovery. Its text generally agrees with the traditional Hebrew text, called the Masoretic text, though it differs from it in many places.[@great-isaiah-scroll]

## A word about “Second Isaiah”

Many text-critical scholars hold that the disputed chapters, mainly 40–66, were written or edited after the Babylonian deportation of the sixth century BC,[@isaiah-in-bom, 423, 432–33] chiefly because of differences in form and content and because Isaiah names Cyrus ([[Isa. 44:28]]; [[Isa. 45:1]]).[@isaiah-in-bom, 425–29] Most Latter-day Saint writers read the book as Isaiah’s prophecy, for reasons like these:[@isaiah-in-bom, 423–24]

- Lehi’s family carried the **brass plates** out of Jerusalem around 600 BC, and Nephi and Jacob quote Isaiah 48–51 from them ([[1 Ne. 20–21]]; [[2 Ne. 7–8]]).[@isaiah-in-bom, 423, 434]
- The **Savior** attributed Isaiah 52 and 54 to Isaiah ([[3 Ne. 20:11]]; [[3 Ne. 23:1–3]]), and New Testament writers attribute passages from chapters 40 and 53 to him ([[Matt. 3:3]]; [[John 12:38–41]]).[@isaiah-in-bom, 424–25]
- Naming Cyrus in advance is what Isaiah says the Lord does to prove He is God: “declaring the end from the beginning” ([[Isa. 46:9–10]]).[@isaiah-in-bom, 429–30]

Two cautions belong with these reasons: the New Testament references “do not settle the question of authorship,”[@isaiah-in-bom, 425] and the Book of Mormon requires only the chapters it quotes or uses to be older than 600 BC.[@isaiah-in-bom, 432] And critical scholars may have correctly identified real differences in the text;[@isaiah-in-bom, 428] a Latter-day Saint reader can explain them by Isaiah’s inspired view of a future he was shown.[@isaiah-in-bom, 429–30]

## Where to start

If you’re new to Isaiah, try this sampler first, then go back and read in order:

1. **Isaiah 53**: the Suffering Servant.
2. **Isaiah 40**: “Comfort ye my people.”
3. **Isaiah 6**: Isaiah’s call in the temple.
4. **Isaiah 11**: the stem of Jesse and the gathering. Moroni quoted it to Joseph Smith ([[JS—H 1:40]]).
5. **Isaiah 29**: the sealed book and a marvelous work.
6. **Isaiah 61**: the chapter Jesus read in Nazareth ([[Luke 4:16–21]]).
7. **Isaiah 2**: the mountain of the Lord’s house.

Then read [Two Kingdoms and Assyria](/old-testament/guides/two-kingdoms-and-assyria/) and begin with [Isaiah 1](/isaiah/1/).
