At midnight on the threshing floor Boaz wakes, finds a woman lying at his feet, and asks, “Who art thou?” She answers with two relationships. “I am Ruth thine handmaid: spread therefore thy skirt over thine handmaid; for thou art a near kinsman” ([[Ruth 3:9]]). She says what she is to him, a servant, and what he is to her. “Near kinsman” is *gōʾēl*, the redeemer.[@strathearn-ruth]

Both relationships change before the book is over. By the end of the next morning's business at the city gate she is no longer his servant but his wife, blessed by the elders to be like Rachel and Leah. He was kin all along; what changes is that he is no longer only the kinsman with the right to redeem, but the kinsman who has paid.

This essay follows those two changes, because I think they are one change seen from two sides. In Ruth, redemption is the making of kin, and it runs in both directions. The redeemer has to be kin before he can redeem. The stranger he redeems becomes kin by being redeemed. That is my reading of the book, and I will argue it from the text. I think it is also the shape of what Jesus Christ does: He took part of our flesh and blood so that He could redeem us as our kinsman, and those He redeems He makes His family.

The book has a second kinsman, and he shows what the first one is. He is nearer to the family than Boaz, and he has the means to redeem. What he lacks is the willingness to pay for it out of his own inheritance. Beside him, Boaz shows that kinship gives a man the right to redeem but not the will, and that the will is what redemption costs.

## Only a kinsman

The law behind the story is in Leviticus 25. A man who fell into poverty and sold his land, or sold himself, could be bought back, and the law says by whom: “one of his brethren may redeem him: Either his uncle, or his uncle's son, may redeem him, or any that is nigh of kin unto him of his family may redeem him” ([[Lev. 25:48–49]]). The one who did it was the *gōʾēl*. Redemption of this kind “could not be done by anyone for anyone. It is based on familial relationship.”[@lane-redemption-gaal] Nor was it free: “this was deliverance at a price, and the gō’el paid that price.”[@muhlestein-ruth]

So two conditions are set before the story begins. The redeemer must be kin, and he must pay. Leviticus has its own theme page, [One of His Brethren May Redeem Him](/leviticus/themes/one-of-his-brethren/#jesus-christ), which joins the first condition to the Savior. What Ruth adds is a story in which the conditions are met, or not met, by real people, and a third condition comes to light.

The narrator introduces Boaz as kin before anything else is said about him: “a kinsman of her husband's, a mighty man of wealth, of the family of Elimelech” ([[Ruth 2:1]]). When Ruth comes home from his field, Naomi gives him the title. “The man is near of kin unto us, one of our next kinsmen” ([[Ruth 2:20]]), and “next kinsmen” is *gōʾēl*.[@scripture-helps-ruth-samuel] The note on [[Ruth 2:20]] says what such a kinsman could do.

In the same chapter Ruth gives herself the opposite name. Falling on her face before Boaz, she asks, “Why have I found grace in thine eyes, that thou shouldest take knowledge of me, seeing I am a stranger?” ([[Ruth 2:10]]). Her word is *nokrîyâ*. It is not the protected stranger of the law, the *gēr*; it “emphasizes a foreigner without the legal protection of the gērîm.”[@strathearn-ruth] Her question is even built around the word. “Take knowledge of me” is the verb *nākar*, to recognize, and the words for a foreigner come from the same root.[@oshb-wlc][@strongs-concordance] She asks, in effect, why he should know her when she is one whom no one knows.

A few verses on she goes lower. She calls herself “thine handmaid,” his *šipḥâ*, the lowest rank of servant, and then says she is not even that: “though I be not like unto one of thine handmaidens” ([[Ruth 2:13]]).[@strathearn-ruth]

So in chapter 2 the two stand far apart. He is a man of standing in Israel, with the right to redeem. She is, by her own word, a foreigner with no standing there, and her tie to him runs through a husband who is dead: Boaz is near of kin “unto us,” Naomi says, and at the gate Ruth is still “the wife of the dead” ([[Ruth 4:5]]).

What closes that distance first is not his act but hers. Ruth had already bound herself to Naomi's people and Naomi's God: “thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God” ([[Ruth 1:16]]). One Latter-day Saint study reads the vow as the reason she could be redeemed at all. Her covenants “gave her access to blessings from the Lord and a right to a redeemer. Without this covenant, Ruth was not eligible for redemption.”[@muhlestein-ruth] That fits what is known of kinship in Israel, whose duties “could be extended to those outside the tribe, clan, or family through covenant.”[@spackman-atonement] Boaz recognizes the vow when he blesses her as a woman who has come to trust under the LORD's “wings” ([[Ruth 2:12]]).

So Ruth comes to her redeemer with a claim, but as an outsider. Her marriage had tied her to Elimelech's house, but only through a husband now dead, and on that reading her covenant gave her the right to a redeemer. What the redemption will do is bring the foreign widow into the house of Israel itself. The rest of the story is that.

## Thine handmaid

At the threshing floor Ruth asks Boaz to act, and when he asks who she is, she uses a different word for servant than she used in the field. At 2:13 the word she used of herself was *šipḥâ*; at 3:9 it is *ʾāmâ*.[@oshb-wlc] Both words can name a slave. One lexicon draws a line between them: a *šipḥâ* is “a girl who is not free but is yet untouched, whose duty was primarily to serve the woman of the house,” while an *ʾāmâ* is “a woman who is not free, and who could be a man's secondary wife.”[@strathearn-ruth] An older lexicon treats both, in Ruth's mouth, simply as the humble way a woman speaks of herself.[@bdb] So the change should not be pressed far. But the word she chooses at midnight is the word for a servant who could be married, and she chooses it in the sentence in which, on a common reading, she is “essentially asking Boaz to marry her.”[@scripture-helps-ruth-samuel]

She asks in Boaz's own image. His blessing in the field had put her under the LORD's “wings,” *kānāp*, and now she asks him to spread his *kānāp* over her. She is asking him “to be the Lord's wings.”[@muhlestein-ruth] What she asks for is redemption, and she gives the reason in a clause: “for thou art a near kinsman.”

Boaz answers as a kinsman, and then names the difficulty. “And now it is true that I am thy near kinsman: howbeit there is a kinsman nearer than I” ([[Ruth 3:12]]). Kinship gives the right in an order, and the nearer man stands first. Boaz does not step past him. He swears instead, “as the LORD liveth,” that if the other “will not do the part of a kinsman to thee, then will I do the part of a kinsman to thee” ([[Ruth 3:13]]). Naomi, when she hears it, is sure of him: “the man will not be in rest, until he have finished the thing this day” ([[Ruth 3:18]]).

## Able, and unwilling

The matter is decided in the morning, at the gate, before ten elders. The nearer kinsman comes by, and Boaz calls to him, “Ho, such a one! turn aside, sit down here” ([[Ruth 4:1]]). The Hebrew means “a certain unnamed one,”[@bdb] or, in the Church's footnote, “Mr. So & So.”[@lds-scriptures] The narrator leaves the name out, in one older reading because he “had either not heard the name, or did not think it necessary to give it.”[@keil-ruth]

Boaz offers him the field first. “If thou wilt redeem it, redeem it… And he said, I will redeem it” ([[Ruth 4:4]]). At this point the man meets every condition the law names. He is kin, nearer than Boaz, and he is able to pay for the land.

Then Boaz tells him the rest. “What day thou buyest the field of the hand of Naomi, thou must buy it also of Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, to raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance” ([[Ruth 4:5]]). And the man withdraws: “I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I mar mine own inheritance: redeem thou my right to thyself; for I cannot redeem it” ([[Ruth 4:6]]).

His “cannot” is most naturally read as a matter of arithmetic. The price of the field would come out of his own estate, but a son by Ruth would count as the dead man's heir and take the land with him: “he did not want the land he purchased to be inherited by Ruth's future sons instead of members of his own family.”[@scripture-helps-ruth-samuel] His means “would not go to the children he already had,” and so, “not wanting to siphon these means away from his own children's inheritance, the kinsman refuses,” and the shoe he hands to Boaz shows “his unwillingness to perform the duty of the redeemer.”[@muhlestein-ruth] He could pay. He would not pay for another man's name.

He broke no law in refusing. The law of Deuteronomy laid the duty on brothers, neither man seems to have qualified, and neither the purchase nor the marriage was “required of Boaz by law”; the nearer kinsman simply “formally recused himself.”[@strathearn-ruth] In the ceremony of the shoe he “incurs no disgrace.”[@gaskill-shoe] The Church's own reading is sterner. Its footnote to “redeem” in 4:6 sends the reader to the Topical Guide's “Selfishness,”[@lds-scriptures] and a seminary manual quotes a Latter-day Saint commentator who says that “he said selfishly, ‘I cannot redeem it, lest I mar mine own inheritance.’”[@ot-seminary-teacher-80]

The point stands on either reading. The nearer kinsman had the right and the means, and the redemption still did not happen.

That is what the scene teaches about a redeemer. Kinship gives the right to redeem. Means make it possible. Neither one makes it happen. Redemption happens when the one who has the right is willing to pay with what is his own, for someone else's gain. Ruth needed “a righteous man who was both able and willing,”[@muhlestein-ruth] and the nearer kinsman lacked the second.

The book then does something I take to be telling. The man would not raise up the name of the dead, and the book does not give his own. The silence has been noticed before: “The writer has not even preserved the name of that kinsman who was willing to redeem the property but not to marry the widow.”[@ot-seminary-teacher-80] Boaz, who paid from his own estate for another man's name, is named in the line of David at the close of the book, “and Boaz begat Obed” ([[Ruth 4:21]]), and Matthew names him again in the line of Christ ([[Matt. 1:5]]). I do not claim the narrator meant the silence as a verdict; the older explanation given above, that he did not know the name or did not think it worth giving, is as likely as any. But in the story as we have it, the man who kept his inheritance whole has no name, and the man who paid from his has a name that lasts.

## That the name of the dead be not cut off

Boaz pays at once, and calls the town to witness. “Ye are witnesses this day, that I have bought all that was Elimelech's, and all that was Chilion's and Mahlon's, of the hand of Naomi. Moreover Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of Mahlon, have I purchased to be my wife, to raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance, that the name of the dead be not cut off from among his brethren” ([[Ruth 4:9–10]]). He was “willing to sacrifice his estate in order to support Ruth, Naomi, and their heritage.”[@muhlestein-ruth] He had “generously interpreted any legal responsibilities he might have had.”[@strathearn-ruth]

Notice what the sentence calls her. “Ruth the Moabitess” has been her name since she came into Bethlehem ([[Ruth 1:22]]; [[Ruth 2:2]], [[Ruth 2:21]]); “up until Ruth 4:10, the author repeatedly emphasizes Ruth's Moabite identity.”[@strathearn-ruth] Verse 10 is the last time. In the same sentence the word gives way to another: “to be my wife.” The book does not say how her welcome fits the law that kept Moabites out of the congregation ([[Deut. 23:3]]); the note on [[Ruth 1:4]] takes that up. What it shows is the order of things. She is called a Moabite until the sentence that redeems her, and not after.

The elders answer with a blessing. “The LORD make the woman that is come into thine house like Rachel and like Leah, which two did build the house of Israel” ([[Ruth 4:11]]). The woman who called herself a foreigner is blessed to be one of the mothers of the house. “So Boaz took Ruth, and she was his wife” ([[Ruth 4:13]]).

This is the second direction. Boaz was kin before he redeemed, and Ruth became kin by being redeemed: not by her first marriage, which had left her a Moabite widow in Bethlehem, but as Boaz's wife and a mother in Israel. Her covenant had given her a claim on a kinsman; his redemption brought her into the house.

The name that was in danger is not lost, either. The elders bid Boaz “be famous in Beth-lehem” ([[Ruth 4:11]]), and the women pray “that his name may be famous in Israel” ([[Ruth 4:14]]); in both verses the Hebrew has the word “name.”[@oshb-wlc] Then the neighbors give the boy a name, Obed, “the father of Jesse, the father of David” ([[Ruth 4:17]]). The danger was that a dead man's line would end with him. Instead a son is born into the house he left, the town asks that the house's name be famous, and the son's line runs to the king.

## Better than seven sons

The women of Bethlehem have the last word on Ruth. “Blessed be the LORD, which hath not left thee this day without a kinsman,” they tell Naomi, “for thy daughter in law, which loveth thee, which is better to thee than seven sons, hath born him” ([[Ruth 4:14–15]]). The kinsman here is *gōʾēl* again, and the stranger is now worth more to Naomi than the largest family a mother could be given. (The note on [[Ruth 4:14]] gives what seven sons meant.)

Isaiah has a passage about two people who feared being shut out of Israel for good:

> Neither let the son of the stranger, that hath joined himself to the LORD, speak, saying, The LORD hath utterly separated me from his people: neither let the eunuch say, Behold, I am a dry tree. For thus saith the LORD unto the eunuchs that keep my sabbaths, and choose the things that please me, and take hold of my covenant; Even unto them will I give in mine house and within my walls a place and a name better than of sons and of daughters: I will give them an everlasting name, that shall not be cut off. ([[Isa. 56:3–5]])

The stranger fears separation from God's people. The eunuch fears that he will leave no one behind him, “a dry tree.” The promise of a name “even better than son or daughter” means, in one Latter-day Saint reading, “the true inclusion of the eunuch into the household of God.”[@belnap-outcasts] The strangers' own promise follows, that the LORD will bring them to His holy mountain and make them joyful in His house ([[Isa. 56:6–7]]). Isaiah's word for the stranger, *nēkār*, is of the same family as Ruth's *nokrîyâ*.[@strongs-concordance]

I know of no one who has joined this passage to Ruth, and I do not suggest that Isaiah had her in mind. What I see is that Ruth's last chapter acts out in one family what Isaiah promises to both of his outsiders. Ruth is the stranger who joined herself to the LORD ([[Ruth 1:16]]; [[Ruth 2:12]]). The house she married into is the dry tree: its men had died, and no son was left to carry the dead man's name. And the words of the answers match. Boaz redeems “that the name of the dead be not cut off” ([[Ruth 4:10]]), and Isaiah promises “an everlasting name, that shall not be cut off”; in Hebrew it is the same verb in the same form.[@oshb-wlc] The women call Ruth “better to thee than seven sons,” and Isaiah promises a name “better than of sons.” (A name not cut off is covenant language, and Isaiah uses it of Israel's own seed as well, [[Isa. 48:19]]; the note on [[Isa. 56:5]] says what “cut off” meant. The match is of meaning, a house without a son and a name kept, as much as of words.)

## The Kinsman who was able and willing

Each condition the book lays down for a redeemer, Jesus Christ meets, and He meets the third at the very point where the nearer kinsman failed.

He is kin. Only kin could redeem, and the Redeemer took our flesh, which I read as His meeting that condition: “Forasmuch then as the children are partakers of flesh and blood, he also himself likewise took part of the same,” for “in all things it behoved him to be made like unto his brethren” ([[Heb. 2:14, 17]]). The role Hebrews gives Him as “brother” “stands within same conceptual frame work to the older Israelite-Jewish kinsman-redeemer concept.”[@bowen-brethren-hebrews-2] Abinadi said the same thing in a sentence: “God himself shall come down among the children of men, and shall redeem his people” ([[Mosiah 15:1]]). Leviticus's theme page sets out this step; here it is enough that the first condition, which Boaz met by birth, the Savior met by coming down.

He is able. Every kinsman in Ruth is able in the way the law meant, with silver enough for a field. No kinsman is able for the debt of sin. “There is not any man that can sacrifice his own blood which will atone for the sins of another,” Amulek says; it must be “an infinite atonement,” and it is “the Son of God, yea, infinite and eternal” ([[Alma 34:11–14]]).

And He is willing. “I lay down my life, that I might take it again. No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself” ([[John 10:17–18]]). The seminary manual that quotes “selfishly” of the nearer kinsman calls Boaz “a type of the Savior, who redeemed all of mankind without thought for His own benefit.”[@ot-seminary-teacher-80] The *Liahona* for June 2026 puts the type in the language of kinship: as Boaz redeemed “his kinsman,” Christ “fulfilled the law to offer redemption to His kin through His great atoning sacrifice.”[@liahona-2026-redemption-ruth] Lehi's assurance to his son Jacob, “I know that thou art redeemed, because of the righteousness of thy Redeemer” ([[2 Ne. 2:3]]), has been read against Ruth's unnamed kinsman. Human kinsman-redeemers “are not always reliable and faithful in carrying out covenantal obligations,” but “God is righteous.”[@spackman-atonement]

There is one more thing, and it is the step in this essay I hold most clearly as my own. The nearer kinsman refused because his inheritance would pass to a son who carried another man's name. At that exact point the Savior does the opposite. He is the one God “hath appointed heir of all things” ([[Heb. 1:2]]), and He makes those He redeems “heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ” ([[Rom. 8:17]]). Abinadi asks of Him, “who shall be his seed?”, and answers that those who believed the Lord would redeem His people “are his seed, or they are the heirs of the kingdom of God” ([[Mosiah 15:10–11]]). The question follows from Hebrews: if the Son “will be heir of all things (1:2) including God's name (1:4), what are the implications for ‘many sons and daughters’?”[@bowen-brethren-hebrews-2] Read beside Ruth 4:6, the answer I see is this. The one thing the nearer kinsman would not do, let what he paid from his own estate go to an heir counted to another man's name, is the thing the Redeemer does on purpose: He shares His inheritance with those who are not heirs in their own right. Sharing His inheritance does not mar it. It is what He redeems us into.

## Brought into His house

The other direction is the stranger's, and scripture describes it in Ruth's terms. Paul reminds Gentile converts that they were once “aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of promise,” and are “made nigh by the blood of Christ”: “Now therefore ye are no more strangers and foreigners, but fellowcitizens with the saints, and of the household of God” ([[Eph. 2:12–13, 19]]).

King Benjamin gives the order in which it happens, and I read it as the order of Ruth's story. First the covenant: “because of the covenant which ye have made ye shall be called the children of Christ, his sons, and his daughters” ([[Mosiah 5:7]]). Then the name: “take upon you the name of Christ” ([[Mosiah 5:8]]), a name, he says, “that never should be blotted out, except it be through transgression” ([[Mosiah 5:11]]). There is one difference. In Ruth the name that is kept is the dead man's, and Ruth's own change is the loss of a label; in Mosiah the name is Christ's, given to those He makes His family. The passage holds both lines: “As we become his covenant people, Christ gives us his name,” and a covenant is “the creation of family relationships.”[@lane-grand-design]

So the two movements of Ruth are the two movements of the gospel. In Ruth they meet in one morning at the gate: Boaz, already kin, pays, and Ruth becomes kin. In the gospel the Redeemer first came down into our family, taking part of our flesh and blood, so that by His blood He could bring us into His. Hebrews puts the kinship in one verse: “For both he that sanctifieth and they who are sanctified are all of one: for which cause he is not ashamed to call them brethren” ([[Heb. 2:11]]).

## Kin, in both directions

A disciple begins where Ruth began. Before Christ, Paul says, we were strangers to the covenants, with no claim of our own on the Redeemer. The covenant gives that claim, as Ruth's vow gave hers, and His redemption makes the stranger family. Elder Caussé calls Paul's words a promise “made to everyone who becomes a member of the Church.”[@causse-no-more-strangers] Whatever a person has called herself or himself before God, stranger or servant or less than a servant, the name the Redeemer gives is son or daughter.

And a disciple is sometimes in the place of the kinsman. Boaz redeemed “to raise up the name of the dead,” and Latter-day Saints act for their own dead in the temple. That this is Boaz's work in Ruth is my connection. What has been said of the temple work is what it does to those who do it: they become like Christ “in vicariously working for the redemption of others,” and “as we become instruments in his hands, we more fully take on Christ's name and nature as a kinsman-redeemer.”[@lane-grand-design] At the gate the choice was whether to spend what was one's own so that another's name would not be cut off. Work for the dead asks the same of the living: time and means given for people who cannot repay them, so that their names are kept.

Ruth's words at midnight were a claim and a request: thou art a near kinsman. Anyone who comes to Christ can say the same words to Him. He answers as Boaz answered in the morning. He pays, and the stranger becomes family.
