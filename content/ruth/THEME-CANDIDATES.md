# Ruth: theme candidates

Topics that run across the book and might become theme pages (`STANDARDS.md` §8). **None is written until the author chooses it.** The candidates for the author are Fable's and Codex's lists (`.cache/batch/ruth/reports/themes-fable.md` and `themes-codex.md`), shown to the author combined; only the author's choice is recorded here.

## Author's selection (2026-11-04)

**One page, combining two proposals: “Thou Art a Near Kinsman”** (working title, from [[Ruth 3:9]]). The two lists' first two candidates, “That the Name of the Dead Be Not Cut Off” and “Who Art Thou? / A Stranger Brought Near,” were shown to the author. The author asked for the first to center on Boaz as the type of Christ (“I like the theme of Boaz as a Christ figure. It sounds like you were going in that direction with #1, but didn't quite get there”), and then for the two to be one page (“While Boaz is the christ figure, Ruth turns from stranger to family”). The page may be longer than usual if the argument needs it (author, 2026-11-04: “We can make this single theme page longer than usual if we need to expand”); the argument, not the allowance, sets its length.

**The insight.** In Ruth redemption is the making of kin, in both directions: the redeemer must be kin before he can redeem, and the stranger becomes kin by being redeemed. Christ became our kin so that He could redeem us ([[Heb. 2:11, 14–17]]), and the stranger is made “of the household of God” by His blood ([[Eph. 2:12–19]]; [[Mosiah 5:7]]).

**The chapter notes already give the type** (Boaz as *gōʾēl* and Redeemer, in the `christ` sections of chapters 2–4). The page must not repeat it; it shows how the type works, from the book's own conditions for a redeemer, and what it makes of the stranger.

**Passages.**
- *Boaz, the redeemer:* 2:1, 20 (kin, a man of *ḥayil*, “one of our next kinsmen”); 3:9–13 (asked, “thou art a near kinsman”; the nearer kinsman's prior right; the oath); 3:18 (“will not be in rest”); 4:1–10 (the nameless kinsman, “Ho, such a one,” able but unwilling, “lest I mar mine own inheritance”; Boaz pays, before witnesses, “to raise up the name of the dead … that the name of the dead be not cut off”). Lev. 25:25, 47–49 for who could redeem; Muhlestein (ch. 4 `christ`) for “both able and willing” and a covenant creating the kinsman's tie.
- *Ruth, the stranger:* “Ruth the Moabitess” (1:4, 22; 2:2, 6, 21; 4:5, 10); “a stranger” (*nokrîyâ*) and “handmaid” (*šipḥâ*), 2:10, 13; “Ruth thine handmaid” (*ʾāmâ*), 3:9; “Who art thou?” at 3:9 and 3:16; “Moabitess” last at 4:10, the sentence that makes her a wife; “like Rachel and like Leah” (4:11); “better to thee than seven sons” (4:15).
- *To Christ:* Heb. 2:11, 14–17 (the Redeemer made kin); Alma 34:10–14 (only He could pay); Isa. 53:8–10 with Mosiah 15:10–13 (cut off, yet “he shall see his seed”); Isa. 56:3–5 (the stranger joined to the Lord, a name “better than of sons” that “shall not be cut off,” the verb of Ruth 4:10); Eph. 2:12–19; Mosiah 5:7–12.
- *For the reader:* 2 Ne. 26:33; Mosiah 5:7–12; the redemption of the dead (D&C 128:18; Christofferson, “Redemption,” `christofferson-redemption`).

**Supporting, not arcs of their own:** the name thread through Naomi (1:20–21) and the genealogy (4:17–22); “Who art thou?” asked twice. **Order:** the story's, with Boaz's acts and Ruth's change interleaved, not one half each.

**Must not claim:** that the law required the purchase or the marriage of Boaz (Strathearn: not required); that the nearer kinsman stands for the law, or is “selfish,” in the page's words (no Church source found); that the shoe, the gate or the field are types; how Ruth's conversion was formalized; that Deut. 23:3 was set aside; any meaning of the names Ruth, Orpah, Mahlon or Chilion; that “better than seven sons” to Isaiah 56:5 is anyone's connection but the page's.

**Not chosen:** “Go Not Empty” and the other candidates in the two lists (the notes already carry them).
