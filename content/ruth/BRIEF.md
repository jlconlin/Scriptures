## What the author has decided

Nothing for Ruth beyond choosing it. The standing rules are in `STANDARDS.md` §1 and `AUTHORING.md`. How three land on this book:

- **Newest trumps oldest.** *Scripture Helps: Old Testament* (2025), “Ruth; 1 Samuel 1–7” (`scripture-helps-ruth-samuel`), has a section for every chapter: the book and its theme of redemption [4–5], the Moabites (1:1) [6], gleaning (2:1–3) [7], the ephah (2:17) [9], the “next kinsmen” and *gōʾēl* (2:18–20) [10–12], the threshing floor (3:1–2) [13], why Ruth lay at Boaz's feet (3:1–9) [14], why the nearer kinsman declined (4:1–8) [15]. Its endnotes lean on Muhlestein (`muhlestein-ruth`). The seminary manual (`ot-seminary-intro-ruth`, 2014) is newer than the Student Manual (`ot-manual-judges-13-21`, 23-14 to 23-20, 1980s); where they differ, follow *Scripture Helps*, then the seminary manual.
- **Don't replicate the Gospel Library.** *Scripture Helps* is beside 1:1, 2:1–3, 2:17, 2:18–20, 3:1–9 and 4:1–8; the footnotes give the names' meanings (Elimelech, Naomi, Mara, Boaz) and “Ho, such a one.” A note there goes where *Scripture Helps* stops (the Hebrew, a study its endnote names, what the site's other books already show), or it is not written.
- **Quote the Church's modern material sparingly.** *Scripture Helps* quotes Elder Christofferson on “Redeemer” (`christofferson-redemption`); the seminary manual quotes President Monson's “A model of ideal womanhood is Ruth” (`monson-models`). Each at most once on the site, from the talk itself, and only where the note needs the words. *Come, Follow Me* (`cfm-ot-2026-23`) asks how Boaz redeemed Ruth and how Ruth and Boaz were like Jesus Christ; it is what the Church asks a reader to see, not commentary.

## What a chapter is for here

Ruth is 85 verses in four chapters (22, 23, 18, 22): a short story told with care, “one of the most loved stories of the Old Testament” whose readers “often do not recognize a deeper symbolism in the text” (`muhlestein-ruth` [6]). The reader knows the story and asks grown-up questions:

- **Ruth 1:** *When did this happen, and who wrote it? Who were the Moabites, and could an Israelite marry one, given Deut. 23:3? Was Elimelech wrong to leave? What do the names mean? Why does Naomi send her daughters-in-law home, and what are the sons “in my womb” (1:11–13)? Was Orpah wrong to go? Is Ruth's vow a conversion? Is Naomi accusing God when she calls herself Mara?*
- **Ruth 2:** *What was gleaning, and was Boaz required to let her? Was it chance (“her hap”)? What is “a mighty man of wealth”? Why is he so kind? What are the “wings” of 2:12? How much is an ephah? What is the “kindness to the living and to the dead,” and what is a “next kinsman”?*
- **Ruth 3:** *What was Naomi's plan, and was it proper? Why was Boaz sleeping on the threshing floor? What does “uncover his feet” mean, and what happened that night? What is she asking with “spread therefore thy skirt”? What is her “kindness … in the latter end”? Why must no one know she came?*
- **Ruth 4:** *Why the gate and ten elders? What land, and why must the buyer also take Ruth? What does “mar mine own inheritance” mean? Is the shoe Deut. 25:9? Is this a levirate marriage when Boaz is not a brother? Why is the son born “to Naomi”? Why end with a genealogy, and why is a Moabite in David's line and Christ's?*

A chapter answers those from the text first. (The `christ` section then applies the chapter to the reader, in statements: `STANDARDS.md` §1.)

**Read the story as a story.** The narrator works by repeated words: Naomi goes out “full” and comes home “empty” (1:21), and ends with a child in her bosom (4:16); the LORD “visited his people in giving them bread” (1:6) and “gave her conception” (4:13), the two verses where, in Strathearn's words, “God is specifically mentioned” acting [50]; Boaz blesses Ruth under the LORD's “wings” (2:12) and she asks him to spread his “wing” (3:9); Boaz is a “mighty man of wealth” (*ḥayil*, 2:1) and Ruth “a virtuous woman” (*ḥayil*, 3:11; Strathearn [43]). Say what the repetition shows where a source shows it; don't build a scheme of the writer's own.

**Say what the story means in its own terms before what it foreshadows.** The Church's sources read the book as redemption and point to the Redeemer descended from Ruth (*Scripture Helps* [5], [12]; the seminary manual [10]). The chapters do too, in `christ` and at the verses named below. But each note first says what happened among Naomi, Ruth and Boaz, under the law of Israel, and what the LORD's part in it was; the type comes after, and is a source's.

**Four chapters, many shared things.** Each is explained once, at the place this brief names; the other chapters point to it. Chapters will be short; `setting` may fall under the checker's word count; ignore the warning. **Don't pad.**

## Divisions

The book gives no divisions of its own, and none of the sources read gives a two-part scheme: the seminary manual outlines it chapter by chapter [11–14], the Guide to the Scriptures the same (`gs-ruth`). Welch and Radday's study of the book's structure (*Beth Mikra*, in Hebrew) is not readable. **Proposed (kept from the coordinator's draft):** two divisions, the story's own two movements. Chapters 1–2 bring Naomi home empty and end with her recognizing a redeemer (2:20); chapters 3–4 are the redemption itself, at night on the threshing floor and in the morning at the gate. The titles are the King James' words (1:16; 4:15).

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Quotations were copied from the page named, with its paragraph number; the rest is the drafter's direction of where to look. Nothing goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **`when`.** The book gives none beyond “in the days when the judges ruled” (1:1), ten years in Moab (1:4), and the barley and wheat harvests (1:22; 2:23). The seminary manual places nothing; Strathearn “sometime between 1200 and 1000 BC” [13]; the *Old Testament Minute* “sometime between 1200 and 1020 BC” (`ot-minute-ruth-1` [4]). **Proposed:** `when` is from the text (“In the days when the judges ruled”; “At the beginning of barley harvest”; “At the end of the harvests”; “The next morning, at the gate”); no B.C. year. Chapter 1's `setting` may point to the period guide in a clause.
- **Names of God.** Copy any phrase with LORD or “the Almighty” from `data/kjv/ruth.json`. The LORD is named by the characters in blessings and oaths (1:8–9, 17; 2:4, 12, 20; 3:10, 13; 4:11–12, 14); the narrator names Him acting only at 1:6 and 4:13. That the LORD is the premortal Christ is said where it answers a question, in a clause, pointing to `[[Ex. 3:14]]`.
- **Hebrew only where it answers a reader's question** (`STANDARDS.md` §3). Words that pay: *ḥesed* (1:8; 2:20; 3:10, the only three: `occurrences.mjs ruth --hebrew 2617`); “rest” (1:9; 3:1; Strathearn [27], [30]); *nokrîyâ*, “stranger” (2:10; Strathearn [32]); *kānāp*, “wings”/“skirt” (2:12; 3:9; BDB 3671); *gōʾēl* (BDB 1350: “act as kinsman, do the part of next of kin,” and at 3:13 “in taking a kinsman's widow”); *moda‘* (2:1); *ḥayil* (2:1; 3:11); *margǝlōt*, “place of the feet” (3:4, 7, 8, 14; BDB 4772, which notes a ketiv and qere at 3:14); *gālâ*, “uncover” (3:4, 7), the same verb as “advertise thee,” literally “uncover thine ear,” at 4:4 (a lead: check before saying so); **Muhlestein's word-study at 3:13** (“the writer does not use the word for lying down … but rather for lodging,” [51]) is half right: 3:13 opens with *lînî*, “tarry” (H3885), but ends with *šikbî*, “lie down” (H7901). Cite him only for what holds. No more than about a quarter of a chapter's notes are Language notes. 
- **The Joseph Smith Translation.** None of the Church's footnotes in Ruth is a JST reading (`fn1.txt`–`fn4.txt`). No note uses the JST of Ruth.
- **Scripture reads Ruth.** Matthew names “Booz of Rachab; and Booz begat Obed of Ruth” ([[Matt. 1:5]]); Luke has Booz and Obed ([[Luke 3:32]]); 1 Chronicles 2:11–15 repeats the line. The site already sets Ruth beside [[Isa. 56:3]] and [[Neh. 13:1]]. The Book of Mormon: Muhlestein ends on Alma 13:2, that such things were done so “the people might know in what manner to look forward to [God's] Son for redemption” ([65]); KnoWhy #436 joins the *gōʾēl* to 2 Ne. 2. Where scripture quotes or names Ruth, say so; an echo someone identifies is that author's. **No comparison panel.**
- **Other books on the site and the volume guides.** Besides the table, the guides: *The Conquest, the Judges, and One Kingdom* (`#rulers`, where its row already summarizes Ruth; `#books`), *A Timeline of the Old Testament* (`#judges`), *Women and Marriage* (`#valued`, `#levirate-marriage`, `#the-women`), *The Land* (`#neighbors`, `#places`, `#inheritance`), *Law and Covenant* (`#feasts`, for the harvests). Point in a clause; don't retell.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports an arc a reader would not see chapter by chapter (empty to full; the LORD's *ḥesed* done by people; “rest” from 1:9 to 3:1 to 4:13, which Muhlestein joins [61]); `propose-themes` and Codex propose the list at the end of the book. No theme page is written until the author chooses it.

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter; point with a `[[reference]]` rather than re-explain; a note's first sentence gives the answer; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; don't join two verses yourself and call it the text's meaning; application only in `christ`, as statements; **no romance novel** (no feelings, glances or motives the text does not give); **no moral verdict in the writer's own words** on Elimelech's leaving, Orpah's return, Naomi's complaint, Naomi's plan, or the nearer kinsman; **no Jewish reception history** (the Feast of Weeks reading, the Targum, the rabbis on Orpah) unless it answers the reader's question.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that four chapters say the same thing. Quotations were copied from the pages named on 2026-11-03; re-read the page before using one. **Muhlestein and Strathearn translate verses themselves** (“author's translation”): quote their renderings as theirs.

- **Date, writer and purpose (1:1; 4:7; 4:18–22).** The text sets the story “in the days when the judges ruled” (1:1) and looks back on a custom “in former time in Israel” (4:7). The Bible Dictionary: the book “appears to be intended to connect the history of David with the earlier times, and also to form a contrast, in its peaceful and pastoral simplicity,” to Judges (`bd-ruth` [1]). The seminary manual: “The author of the book of Ruth is unknown”; because David's genealogy is included it “may have been written after David's or Solomon's time, likely following the Babylonian exile,” and it “seems to provide valuable balance” to those who would separate from all non-Israelites: “such faithfulness is more important than ethnicity” (`ot-seminary-intro-ruth` [7–8]). Strathearn gives three views (the monarchy, the Persian period, an early story redacted later) [13]; Muhlestein: “we do not know who the author of Ruth is nor when he wrote the book” [8]. **Proposed:** chapter 1's `setting` says that the events are in the judges' days, that the writer and date are not known, and gives the Bible Dictionary's two purposes (David; the contrast with Judges) and the seminary manual's “may have” as the Church's own suggestion of a later telling. Chapter 4 at 4:7 notes that the narrator explains an old custom to readers who no longer knew it; at 4:18 that the line runs to David. **No chapter argues the date, builds a reading on the book as fiction, or treats Ruth, Naomi and Boaz as other than real** (Muhlestein: the writer's skill does not “take away from … the reality of Ruth's, Naomi's, and Boaz's lives” [8]). Francisco's reading of the names as an allegory of the latter-day gathering (`francisco-allegory-ruth`) is his, at most once in an `explore` bullet.
- **The Moabite law and Ruth's welcome (Deut. 23:3; Ruth 1:4, 16; 2:10; 4:10).** The text never cites the law; it calls her “Ruth the Moabitess” again and again (Strathearn counts 1:4, 6, 22; 2:2, 6, 21; 4:5, 10 [32]), and she calls herself a “stranger,” *nokrîyâ* (2:10). Strathearn: the book focuses on “the notion that blessings of the Abrahamic covenant could be extended to the Moabites” and portrays “a social reality that is not envisioned in Deuteronomy” [15–16]; Deuteronomy 23 on the site already gives that and the rabbis' reading of the masculine wording. The Bible Dictionary: “the conversion of a non-Israelite into the fold of Israel” (`bd-ruth` [2]); *Scripture Helps*: she “was fully accepted as an Israelite” [5]. **Proposed:** 1:4 says the sons married Moabite women, that the law of Deut. 23:3 kept Moabites out of “the congregation of the LORD,” and that the book does not explain how the two fit; it gives Strathearn's reading and points to `[[Deut. 23:3]]` for the rest. 1:16 carries the conversion (the Bible Dictionary; Strathearn's caution that “conversion” in our sense is “probably anachronistic” [33]; the manual's “Both of these passages indicate that Ruth was converted,” 23-15). 4:10 says Boaz takes her before the elders as his wife. **No note says the law did not apply, was set aside, or applied only to men, in the writer's voice**; the rabbis' reading is reported only as Strathearn reports it, on the Deuteronomy page.
- **Was Elimelech wrong to go to Moab? (1:1–5).** Strathearn: “There is no evidence that the author negatively viewed Elimelech's migration” [16]. **Proposed:** say what the text says and that it gives no cause for the deaths; no note blames the family (Francisco's broken covenants are his).
- **Orpah (1:14–15).** The text: she “kissed her mother in law” and returned “unto her people, and unto her gods” at Naomi's urging. Muhlestein: “the narrative is not written in a way that portrays Orpah in a bad light”; “Orpah’s goodness is contrasted with Ruth’s greatness” [36]; Welch: “I find Orpah's decision to return home to be an honorable one” [7]. **Proposed:** Orpah does what Naomi asked; Ruth does more; the contrast is the text's, not a condemnation.
- **Naomi's complaint (1:20–21).** Strathearn: “Job-like anguish” [28]; the manual: “not an accusation, only Naomi's way of saying that she had endured much tragedy” (23-16); the footnote at 1:21 gives “OR humbled me.” **Proposed:** the note lets her words stand, explains Mara from the text, and shows from the book's end that she was not left empty (4:14–17; the *Old Testament Minute* on the empty-full theme, `ot-minute-ruth-1` [22]); no note scolds her or excuses her.
- ***Ḥesed* as the book's key word (1:8; 2:20; 3:10).** Belnap: the term “is used to describe the acts of Ruth and God explicitly and the works of Boaz implicitly” (`belnap-hesed` [18]); Strathearn: God's *ḥesed* “is often accomplished through the acts of other people” [38], and though God is named acting in only two verses, “his ḥesed pervades the story” [50]; Welch: *ḥesed* “exceeds the bounds of law or custom” [5]; McClellan: Boaz married Ruth “because of her multiple acts of ḥesed” (`mcclellan-go-ye-learn` [18]). **Proposed:** 1:8 explains the word once (the KJV's “deal kindly”; what Naomi asks the LORD to do), pointing to `[[Gen. 24:12]]` for the word's range; 2:20 says whose *ḥesed* Naomi means (the KJV's “his” is ambiguous; Belnap gives it to God, Muhlestein [46] translates it; check the Hebrew and say it may be read either way only if a source does); 3:10 says what her “latter” kindness was, with Strathearn and Belnap [42], [19]. The book's three uses are not called a structure unless a source says so.
- **The threshing floor (3:1–14).** The most sensitive passage; handle it plainly and chastely. The text: Naomi seeks “rest” for Ruth (3:1; footnote: “Implying marriage”); Ruth uncovers “his feet” and lies down (3:4–7); at midnight he finds her (3:8); she asks him to spread his skirt over her (3:9); he calls her “a virtuous woman,” promises, and sends her away “before one could know another” (3:10–14). **The sources:** *Scripture Helps*: Naomi “encouraged Ruth to seek marriage from him,” and Ruth “was essentially asking Boaz to marry her” [14]; Strathearn: “While scholars debate what actually happened here,” the intent was that Boaz would be “the human conduit of God's ḥesed” [41]; Muhlestein: the words “for lying down, uncovering, and feet are words often used as sexual euphemisms,” and if the writer raised the thought, “it seems most likely that our writer only did so in order that he might crush the idea … nothing of the kind happened” [51]; the *Old Testament Minute*: lying at his feet “suggests the position of a humble supplicant,” and Boaz sent her early “To protect Ruth's reputation” (`ot-minute-ruth-3` [6], [11]); BDB: *margǝlōt* is “place of the feet.” The manual relays Keil: objectionable “According to our customs,” but not “by the customs of the people of Israel at that time” (23-19 [46]). **Proposed:** 3:4 says what Naomi told her to do and what “uncover his feet” means (the place at his feet, BDB), and that Naomi's purpose was marriage (*Scripture Helps*, the footnote); 3:9 says the request was a proposal of marriage in the words of 2:12; 3:14 says Boaz protected her name. Muhlestein's point about euphemism may be given once, at 3:4 or 3:7, in his words and with his conclusion; the note does not elaborate on it, quote other possibilities, or speculate. **No note suggests anything improper happened, and none treats the scene coyly or romantically; the text's “virtuous woman” (3:11) is the verdict.** Ray's reading of the threshing floor as a type of the temple (`ray-house-lord` [39]) is his, at most in `christ` or `explore`.
- **Levirate marriage and the redeemer combined (1:11–13; 2:20; 3:12–13; 4:5–10).** Boaz is not a brother, and Deut. 25:5 speaks of brothers who “dwell together.” The Church's sources call it levirate: the Bible Dictionary, the custom of marrying the husband's brother “or sometimes a near heir,” which “forms a major aspect of the story of Ruth (Ruth 4:1–12)” (`bd-levirate-marriage`); the nearer kinsman had the right “according to the law in Deut. 25:5–10” (`bd-ruth` [1]); *Scripture Helps*: “The book of Ruth uniquely associates the kinsman redeemer with the levirate law of marriage,” and Boaz redeemed them “by purchasing Elimelech's land but also by following levirate customs to marry Ruth” [11]. Muhlestein: “Apparently, levirate duty could apply to relatives beyond immediate brothers,” and “It is from the book of Ruth we learn that levirate marriage and the right of redemption were connected” [22], [26]. Strathearn: Boaz acts “with a combination of legal customs that are usually discussed separately,” and “Technically, neither of these actions were required of Boaz by law”; “neither Boaz nor the ‘nearer gōʾēl’ qualified as levirate candidates under these precise stipulations” [45]. Gaskill argues it is not levirate marriage at all (`gaskill-shoe` [12]–[18]). **Proposed:** 4:5 says what the text joins (the land and “Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, to raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance”), that the law names a brother, and that the Church's sources read Boaz as taking up the levirate duty as a near kinsman (the Bible Dictionary, *Scripture Helps*), with Strathearn's “not required by law” as what makes it *ḥesed*. Gaskill's dissent is given at 4:7, where his argument is about the shoe, and the Deuteronomy note already states it: agree. 1:11–13 says in a clause that Naomi's words assume the custom and points to 4:5. The writer does not choose between Gaskill and the Bible Dictionary; *Scripture Helps* leads.
- **The shoe (4:7–8) and Deut. 25:9.** The text explains it itself: “the manner in former time in Israel concerning redeeming and concerning changing, for to confirm all things” (4:7). In Deut. 25:9 the widow takes the shoe off the brother who refuses and spits in his face; in Ruth the kinsman draws off his own shoe and no shame is named. The *Old Testament Minute*: a ritual asserting “the giver was offering their property rights to the recipient” (`ot-minute-ruth-4` [8]); the manual (quoting *Great People of the Bible*): “A man renouncing property rights removed a sandal” (23-20 [47]); Gaskill: “the shoe served as the perfect symbol of the right of possession” [26]; Muhlestein suggests a shoe on means readiness for a duty [20]. **Proposed:** 4:7 explains it from the text, then the readings above; points to `[[Deut. 25:9]]`, which already sets both side by side. Gaskill's temple applications [33] are his, at most in `explore`.
- **Why the nearer kinsman declines (4:6).** *Scripture Helps*: he “did not want the land he purchased to be inherited by Ruth's future sons instead of members of his own family” [15]; Muhlestein: the child “would eventually take Naomi's land inheritance away from his family” [60]. **Proposed:** 4:6 gives *Scripture Helps*' reason (beside it: go where it stops, with what land Naomi had, 4:3, from Strathearn [29] and the *Old Testament Minute*'s two possibilities, `ot-minute-ruth-4` [6]). The footnote's “TG Selfishness” is the Church's; **the note does not call him selfish in its own words**, nor “Mr. So-and-so” a slur.
- **Names (1:2–4, 20; 4:17).** The footnotes give Elimelech “My God is king,” Naomi “Pleasant,” Mara “bitter,” Boaz “In him is strength”; the Bible Dictionary gives Obed “Servant” (`bd-obed`). The *Old Testament Minute*: Mahlon “sick,” Chilion “pining,” Ruth “friend,” Orpah “gazelle,” and “Not all these names are significant to the story” (`ot-minute-ruth-1` [10]); Francisco builds an allegory on other meanings. **Proposed:** the text itself explains only Naomi and Mara (1:20); 1:20 says so. Mahlon and Chilion get a Language note at 1:2 only if BDB gives the meanings; Ruth's and Orpah's names are said to be uncertain, and **no reading is built on them** in the writer's words.
- **Ruth in the line of David and of Christ; Boaz as a type of the Redeemer (4:13–22).** First in the story's own terms: the women bless the LORD, “which hath not left thee this day without a kinsman” (*gōʾēl*, 4:14), and the child is “a restorer of thy life” (4:15); Obed is “the father of Jesse, the father of David” (4:17). Then the Church's reading: “Ruth and Boaz were ancestors of David and Jesus Christ” (*Scripture Helps* [1]); “Like Naomi and Ruth, we cannot save ourselves but must rely on our Redeemer Jesus Christ” ([12]; the seminary manual's “a Redeemer from Bethlehem” [10]); *Come, Follow Me*: “How were both Ruth and Boaz like Jesus Christ?” [8]. Muhlestein: “Numerous elements of the story serve as a type of Christ” [7], Ruth herself “a poignant symbol of the Savior” [63]; Welch: Boaz as *gōʾēl* is “for Christian readers, a powerful type of Christ our Redeemer, Bridegroom, and generous Friend” [3]. The *Old Testament Minute*'s “symbol of the Atonement” in 3:9 (`ot-minute-ruth-3` [8]) rests on *kāpar*, a different root from *kānāp*: hers, if used at all. **Proposed:** 4:14 and 4:17–18 carry the line to David and to `[[Matt. 1:5]]` (the four women of Matthew 1 are at `[[Gen. 38:29]]`: point); the type of the Redeemer is stated in each chapter's `christ` section and in the note at 2:20 or 4:14, with the source's own words, after the story's meaning. **No chapter makes the threshing floor, the six measures, the shoe or the wing a type of Christ in the writer's words.**

## Settled without the author

Decided by the standard, not asked of the author (Psalms' brief has the rule):

- **No Church, BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* source answers a reader's real question, and an older commentary does:** give its answer as “one older reading,” within the limit of a third of the notes. If the question isn't one a reader would ask, there is no note.
- **A scholar's reading that an RSC, BYU or *Interpreter* publication prints** (Hubbard, Tigay, Ostriker in Strathearn; Westbrook, Burrows in Muhlestein; Alter in Francisco) is cited as that publication's author's, or as quoted in it, beside the Church's, never above it.
- **Two Church sources read a verse differently** (the date: the Bible Dictionary and the seminary manual; Naomi's complaint: the manual and Strathearn): give both, the newer and the Church's own first, without choosing; where *Scripture Helps* speaks, it leads.
- **A source quoted inside a Church manual** whose work can't be read (Rasmussen; *Great People of the Bible*; Gesenius in Rasmussen) is cited as the manual; Keil, whom it relays, may be read on Bible Hub and cited as himself.
- **Figures that differ** (the ephah in *Scripture Helps* [9], the Bible Dictionary, Strathearn [38], the *Old Testament Minute*): a note gives *Scripture Helps*' figure or none.
- **The Church's footnote points somewhere the note does not go:** leave it.
