## What a chapter is for here

Proverbs is 915 verses in 31 chapters, of three kinds: connected discourses and poems (1–9; 22:17–24:34; 30; 31), and some 600 independent two-line sayings (10:1–22:16 and 25–29). The reader's questions: *Did Solomon write this? Who were Agur and Lemuel, and “the men of Hezekiah”? Why does part of it read like an Egyptian text? Who is Wisdom, the woman who calls in the streets and was with the Lord “before his works of old” (8:22)? Is she Christ? What does “the fear of the LORD” mean, if not being afraid? Who is the “strange woman,” and is the book suspicious of women? Is 22:6 a promise, and what if a child departs anyway? Do the righteous really always prosper? Why does one verse say to answer a fool and the next not to (26:4–5)? Does 13:24 command spanking? What does “virtuous woman” mean in 31:10? Why are some proverbs repeated word for word?* (The `christ` section then applies the chapter to the reader, in statements: `STANDARDS.md` §1.)

**Chapters 1–9 and the longer poems are written like any book.** A discourse has an argument (the father's appeal, the warning, the reward), and `thread` follows it. Where Wisdom speaks (1:20–33; 8; 9), say who is speaking and to whom before what she stands for.

**Chapters 10–29: a collection, not an argument.** Delitzsch (`delitzsch-proverbs`, ch. 1, the introduction [118]; one older reading) says of 10:1–22:16 that the proverbs are grouped “without any comprehensive plan, but only according to their more or fewer conspicuous common characteristics,” and that none “stand[s] in necessary connection with one that precedes or that follows; each is in itself a small perfected and finished whole.” The Bible Dictionary calls 10–24 “a collection of proverbs and sentences about the right and wrong ways of living” (`bd-proverbs`). So the standard's “find each chapter's thread” (§3) cannot mean an argument the chapter does not have. **Proposed, for every chapter in 10:1–22:16 and 25–29:**

- **`sections`** group the verses into three to six runs that the text itself joins: a shared subject (the tongue, the sluggard, the king, wealth and poverty), a shared word or form (the run of “The LORD …” sayings in 16:1–9; the “better … than” sayings; the comparisons “As … so” of 25–26), or a shared pair (wise son and foolish son, 10:1; 15:20). The `heading` names what the verses share, in plain words (“The tongue,” “Sayings about the king”). Where a run has nothing in common, the heading says so plainly (“Sayings on several subjects”), and the run is kept short rather than forced under a theme. The `plain` paraphrase still covers every verse, briefly, one sentence or clause a saying; it does not join two sayings into one thought.
- **`thread`** says honestly what kind of chapter this is, once (a string of separate sayings), and then what does recur in it: the one or two subjects that return most often, the contrast the chapter keeps drawing (most often the righteous and the wicked, the diligent and the slothful), and what those sayings add up to about the two ways of 1–9. It may name a cluster the text marks (16:1–9; 25:2–7; 26:1–12 on the fool; 26:13–16 on the sluggard; 27:23–27 on the flock). It does not invent a movement from the first verse to the last. Two short paragraphs are a full thread. `setting` says where the chapter sits in its collection (10:1 and 25:1 are headings) and may fall under the word count; ignore the warning.
- **`notes`** pick the sayings a careful reader stops at, six to ten in a chapter of some thirty sayings; most sayings get no note. The tests, in order: (1) a saying the New Testament, the Book of Mormon or a latter-day prophet quotes or answers (3:11–12; 25:21–22; 26:11; 11:31; 22:6); (2) a King James word or image that is obscure now (“a jewel of gold in a swine's snout,” 11:22; “landmark,” 22:28; “surety,” “reins”); (3) a hard saying (the rod; bribes, 17:8; 18:16; 21:14; the lot, 16:33; “The LORD hath made all things for himself: yea, even the wicked for the day of evil,” 16:4); (4) a saying that seems to promise too much or to contradict another (“Contested,” the proverbs as generalizations; 26:4–5); (5) a saying about the LORD's rule over human plans (16:1, 9; 19:21; 20:24; 21:1, 30–31). **No note on a saying whose meaning is plain in English**, and no note that only restates a proverb in other words.
- **Repeated sayings** (14:12 and 16:25; 18:8 and 26:22; 20:16 and 27:13; 21:9 and 25:24; others: `occurrences.mjs` finds them) are noted once, at the first; the second says in its section's paraphrase that the saying has come before, with a `[[reference]]`.

**Graphic or crude images** (the drunkard, 23:29–35; the adulteress's bed, 7:16–18): describe rather than quote at length (`STANDARDS.md` §3).

## Divisions

The book carries its own headings: “The proverbs of Solomon” (1:1; 10:1), “the words of the wise” (22:17; “These things also belong to the wise,” 24:23), “These are also proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out” (25:1), “The words of Agur” (30:1) and “The words of king Lemuel” (31:1). The sources read divide them differently: the Bible Dictionary 1–9, 10–24, 25–29, 30–31 (`bd-proverbs`); the Guide to the Scriptures the same (`gs-proverb`); *Scripture Helps* 1–9, 10–29, 30–31 [14–16]; Davis (`ot-minute-proverbs` [8]–[18]) the ten collections at the headings, with 10:1–22:16 as “the actual proverbs of Solomon” [19]; Meservy (`meservy-proverbs` [5]) “At least five different collections.” **Proposed:** five divisions at the book's own headings. Division ranges are whole chapters, so the words of the wise, which begin at 22:17, open the third division only at chapter 23: chapter 22's `thread` and its last section say where they begin, and chapter 23's `setting` says the collection began at 22:17.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, which scripture takes up which line) is the drafter's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **`when`.** The text dates nothing but two headings: Solomon (1:1; 10:1; 25:1) and Hezekiah's men (25:1). **Proposed:** `when` names the collection, not a year (“The proverbs of Solomon,” “Copied out under Hezekiah,” “The words of Agur”); 1–9 say “A father's instruction, under Solomon's name.” No B.C. year in `when`. Chapter 1's `setting` may say, as Allis-Pike does, that the book “was not likely compiled in its present form until, at the earliest, the sixth century BC” (`allis-pike-words-wise` [13]), as her reading.
- **No Context link** (`noPeriod` in `book.yaml`, at the author's word above). Solomon's reign is the volume timeline's (`/old-testament/guides/israels-story/#united-kingdom`), and the intro links it; a chapter that needs the reign points there in a clause. If the author later wants 25–29 to open the period guide *Two Kingdoms and Assyria* (Hezekiah's reign), that is a one-line change to `book.yaml`.
- **Names of God.** Copy any phrase with LORD, Lord or GOD from `data/kjv/proverbs.json`. Proverbs uses the LORD's name throughout but, as Davis says, “does not reference the great acts of God for Israel such as the Exodus, nor does it mention specific covenants” (`ot-minute-proverbs` [5]); a note doesn't supply them. The LORD of Proverbs is the premortal Christ: said where it answers a question, in a clause, pointing to `[[Ex. 3:14]]`.
- **Hebrew only where it answers a reader's question** (`STANDARDS.md` §3; Psalms' brief, “Hebrew”). Words that pay: *mashal*, “proverb” (1:1; the Bible Dictionary: “a similitude or parable”); *chokmah*, wisdom as skill (1:2); “dark sayings,” the footnote's “riddles” (1:6); *yir'at YHWH* (1:7; the footnote: “OR reverence of the Lord”); *zarah* and *nokriyyah*, the “strange” and the “foreign” woman (2:16; the footnote at 23:33: “OR foreign women”); *qanah*, “possessed” at 8:22, which Davis says “can mean both ‘to acquire’ and ‘to create’” (`ot-minute-proverbs-1-9` [40]); *'amon*, “one brought up” (8:30); the *ketiv* and *qere* of 22:20, “excellent things” (`hebrew.mjs proverbs 22:20` prints both; what modern translations read there, and why, from the translations themselves); *chayil* (31:10, already in the women guide: link). Not: the Hebrew behind an ordinary English word, or counting “wisdom” (Yarn's count, `yarn-wisdom`, is a curiosity). No more than about a quarter of a chapter's notes are Language notes.
- **Parallelism is the reading, not a note.** Most sayings in 10–29 are two lines in contrast or in agreement (`parry-antithetical`; the volume guide). The `plain` paraphrase reads the two lines together, as parallelism asks; a Literary Structure note is written only where the form changes the meaning (`STANDARDS.md` §7), as when a second line narrows the first or when 26:4–5 set two sayings against each other on purpose.
- **The Joseph Smith Translation.** The Church's footnotes have one JST reading in Proverbs, at 18:22 (“Whoso findeth a good wife hath obtained favor of the Lord”). McConkie calls it “The most significant change made in Proverbs” (`mcconkie-poetic-writings` [9]); it is beside the verse. A note only if a reader would ask why the change was made, and then from McConkie. Proverbs 8 has no JST change; no note says the JST confirms or corrects a reading of Wisdom.
- **Scripture reads Proverbs.** The New Testament: the chastening of the Lord (3:11–12 in [[Heb. 12:5–6]]); grace to the lowly (3:34 in [[James 4:6]], [[1 Pet. 5:5]]); love covering sins (10:12 in [[1 Pet. 4:8]]); the righteous scarcely saved (11:31 in [[1 Pet. 4:18]]); coals of fire (25:21–22 in [[Rom. 12:20]]); the dog to its vomit (26:11 in [[2 Pet. 2:22]]); the lowest seat (25:6–7 beside [[Luke 14:8–11]]); Christ “the wisdom of God” ([[1 Cor. 1:24]], [[1 Cor. 1:30]]; [[Col. 2:3]]). The Book of Mormon: Alma's counsel to his sons (Alma 36–39) beside the father's in 1–9 (`allis-pike-words-wise`; `sc-knowhy-744`); wisdom as “she” in [[Mosiah 8:20]] (Allis-Pike [69]); the tree of life ([[1 Ne. 11:25]], *Scripture Helps*' endnote 12). Latter-day scripture: “seek ye out of the best books words of wisdom” ([[D&C 88:118]], Davis [4]); the footnote at 8:23 points to [[D&C 93:29]]. Where scripture says what a passage meant, say so as scripture's reading; an echo someone identifies is that author's. **No comparison panel, and nothing is written for a Book of Mormon chapter** (`AUTHORING.md`).
- **Other books on the site and the volume guides.** Besides the table, `occurrences.mjs proverbs "<phrase>" --site all` finds what the site already says (Psalms 1, 37, 73, 111, 112, 127; Deuteronomy 6, 19, 21; Isaiah 55; Amos 8). The guides: *Hebrew Poetry* (`#parallelism`, `#acrostics`), *Women and Marriage* (`#valued`, `#the-women`), *A Timeline of the Old Testament* (`#united-kingdom`, `#two-kingdoms`), *How the Old Testament Came to Us* (`#hebrew-bible`, for the Septuagint). Point from the chapter in a clause; don't retell.
- **The `christ` section in 10–29.** It says where the chapter's principle meets Jesus Christ from what scripture and the Church's sources say: a saying the Savior took up or lived (the soft answer, *Come, Follow Me* [18], sets Prov. 15:1–4, 18 beside Mark 12 and John 8); Christ as “the wisdom of God” (1 Cor. 1:24); wisdom given by God to those who ask ([[James 1:5]]). Then one to three plain sentences of application. **It does not read a saying as a type of Christ**, and it does not turn the righteous man of a proverb into the Savior.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports an arc that joins chapters in a way a reader would not see chapter by chapter (Wisdom the woman in 1–9 and the woman of strength in 31 are the obvious one; check it is not only a shared word); `propose-themes` and Codex propose the list at the end of the book. No theme page is written until the author chooses it.

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter; don't re-explain what another chapter explains, point with a `[[reference]]`; a Witness of Christ does not open by saying what the chapter lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer, since the site shows it alone as the note's card; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; “In plain words” adds nothing to the verse; don't join two sayings yourself and call it the text's meaning; application only in `christ`, as statements resting on what it cites, never questions or exhortation; **no self-help commentary**: a note says what the proverb meant and how scripture and the Church read it, not how to apply it to a modern workplace.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that 31 chapters say the same thing. Quotations were copied from the pages named on 2026-10-24; re-read the page before using one.

- **Who wrote Proverbs, and when (1:1; 10:1; 22:17; 24:23; 25:1; 30:1; 31:1).** The Church's sources: *Scripture Helps*, “While many of the proverbs are attributed to Solomon, the book of Proverbs can be seen as a library of Israelite wisdom literature that was collected over several centuries” [5]; the Guide, a book of sayings “some of which were written by Solomon” (`gs-proverb` [2]); the Student Manual (2-3), that Solomon “spoke or compiled three thousand proverbs” (1 Kgs. 4:32) and “Some of his wisdom was undoubtedly preserved by later writers and editors.” The Latter-day Saint studies: Davis, the book is not “composed entirely of proverbs, nor do they all come from Solomon. The actual proverbs of Solomon are found in Proverbs 10:1–22:16” [19], whose 375 lines equal the numerical value of the name Solomon in Hebrew (after Clifford, “good evidence that this section was deliberately crafted”); Allis-Pike, 1–9 are “not generally thought to be personal to him or his children. No son or child is named” [7], and the book was likely compiled “at the earliest, the sixth century BC” [13]; McConkie, “an anthology of epigrams, wise sayings, and fatherly advice gathered from both Hebrew and foreign sources over many generations” [8]. **Proposed:** chapter 1's `setting` says the book's own headings name Solomon, the wise, Hezekiah's men, Agur and Lemuel, and gives *Scripture Helps*' “library … collected over several centuries” as the Church's framing; 1:1 says Solomon is the book's name and patron as the headings show, with 1 Kgs. 4:32. The 375-line count is Davis's (after Clifford), said once at 10:1 as his. **No chapter argues a date or assigns verses to sources beyond what a heading says.** Agur and Lemuel: *Scripture Helps* says of Lemuel only that he “is thought to have been a non-Israelite king” and that “It is not known whether Lemuel was an actual person or a literary creation” [27]; nothing read identifies Agur, and Brenton's Septuagint does not read the name at 30:1 (“These things says the man to them that trust in God”; `lxx-rahlfs`, `biblehub.com/sep/proverbs/30.htm`). Say what is known, and that the rest is not.
- **The words of the wise and the *Instruction of Amenemope* (22:17–24:22).** Davis: the section “is thought to have been influenced by an earlier Egyptian text called ‘The Instructions of Amenemope’” [20]; Von Feldt (`von-feldt-instructional-wisdom`): Hebrew writers “were influenced at times by Egyptian wisdom but … did not incorporate it wholesale. Even in the case of Proverbs 22:17–24:22, which clearly resembles The Instruction of Amenemopet, the author freely adapts the Egyptian material in order to express his own unique beliefs,” and dates the Egyptian text “approximately 1186 to 1069 BC”; KnoWhy #539 (`sc-knowhy-539` [33]–[34]): it “parallels some of the concepts and language found in the biblical book of Proverbs, and indeed may have been the source or inspiration for some of its axioms,” quoting Shupak on scribal schools at Judah's court; McConkie, “foreign sources” [8]. **Proposed:** one note at 22:17 (and chapter 22's `setting` in a sentence): the resemblance as these authors state it, and what the Hebrew author does with it (he sets the counsel under “That thy trust may be in the LORD,” 22:19, which a note may point out from the text). 22:20's “excellent things” is a Language note only if the writer reads what modern translations print there and why (the *ketiv*/*qere* is in `hebrew.mjs`). No note lists parallels line by line from memory or from a source not read, and none says Proverbs copied Egypt or that the Egyptian text is inspired. Faith framing: *Scripture Helps*' “library of Israelite wisdom” and D&C 88:118 (“out of the best books”), which Davis brings to Proverbs [4]: inspired compilers could gather wisdom where it was found.
- **Wisdom personified, and whether Proverbs 8 points to Christ (1:20–33; 3:19–20; 8; 9).** What the Church's sources say: *Scripture Helps*, wisdom “is personified as a woman—often referred to as Lady Wisdom. This was a literary device used to make the concept of wisdom more vivid and memorable” [21]; the Student Manual (2-18), Wisdom “enthroned and contrasted with the seductive, evil, and death-giving woman of Proverbs 7.” **Neither, nor the Bible Dictionary, the footnotes, the Guide or *Come, Follow Me*, identifies the Wisdom of Proverbs 8 with Christ, and there is no JST change in the chapter.** The footnote at 8:23 (“beginning”) points to D&C 93:29, “Man was also in the beginning with God.” The Latter-day Saint scholars: Davis, “The apostle John in John 1 seems to appeal to the image of wisdom in describing Jesus Christ as the Word” [42], after Clifford, and sets beside it that Wisdom “was in the beginning with God and was used by God in Creation”; Von Feldt sets Wisdom's public call beside Christ's invitation to all (2 Ne. 26:23–33) and Wisdom as guide beside the Holy Ghost on Nephi's path (2 Ne. 31–32), each as her comparison. The New Testament calls Christ “the power of God, and the wisdom of God” ([[1 Cor. 1:24]]) and says in Him “are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” ([[Col. 2:3]]). Other Latter-day Saint writers read Lady Wisdom as a figure of a divine feminine (the *Interpreter* article “‘Zion’ and ‘Jerusalem’ as Lady Wisdom,” Peterson's “Nephi and His Asherah”; Christensen on Barker, `christensen-barker`). **Proposed:** the notes at 8:22 and 8:30 say first what the poem says (Wisdom speaks as a person who was with the Lord before the earth and rejoiced in His works); then that the New Testament gives Christ the name “the wisdom of God” and calls Him the Creator ([[John 1:1–3]]; [[Col. 1:16]]), and that John's prologue “seems to appeal to the image of wisdom” (Davis's reading, his word “seems” kept). Chapter 8's `christ` section sets Wisdom at the creation beside the Word by whom all things were made, worded as a comparison, and applies it (Wisdom's “Whoso findeth me findeth life,” 8:35, beside [[John 17:3]]). **No chapter says Wisdom is Christ, that Proverbs 8 prophesies Him, or that Wisdom was created** (the Arian quarrel over 8:22 is reception history: not raised). The divine-feminine readings are not used in chapters: no Church source read makes them, and the Church's statements on a Mother in Heaven are not about Proverbs. If a reader's question at 8:22 requires it, one sentence may say that some Latter-day Saint writers read Wisdom so, as theirs, without arguing it. Lady Wisdom is “she” throughout; don't replace the poem's woman with a doctrine.
- **“The fear of the LORD” (1:7; 9:10; and some fifteen other verses).** The footnote at 1:7: “OR reverence of the Lord”; *Scripture Helps* quotes Bednar (“Unlike worldly fear that creates alarm and anxiety, godly fear is a source of peace, assurance, and confidence”) [18]–[20], already printed at `[[Ps. 111:10]]`; *Come, Follow Me* offers “reverence, love, or obedience” [28]; Allis-Pike [22], that what grows from awe of the Lord is “spiritual even when focused on the temporal”; the Bible Dictionary, that “throughout it is taken for granted that ‘the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom.’” **Proposed:** the note at 1:7 says what the phrase means from these, links Ps. 111:10 for Bednar, and says what the rest of the book does with it: it is the frame of the whole (1:7 and 9:10 bracket 1–9; 31:30 closes the book). The other verses point. No note says the fear is only metaphor for respect; *Scripture Helps*' Bednar includes “anticipation of the Final Judgment.”
- **Proverbs as generalizations, not promises (22:6; 10:3, 27; 13:21; 16:7; and every saying that says the righteous prosper).** Davis: the book “is built on the assumption that proper action will lead to proper outcomes” [4]; *Come, Follow Me*: its message “is that if you seek wisdom—especially God’s wisdom—life will go well. But Proverbs is followed by the book of Ecclesiastes, which seems to say, ‘It’s not that simple’” [4], and “Life is always better—if not always perfect—when we trust and follow the Lord Jesus Christ” [5]. Matson (`matson-training-child`) on 22:6: the verse “can bring equal heartache and undue anxiety to the parent whose child has chosen to exercise his or her agency to wander” [6]; “God respects the agency of all his children” [7]; the outcome “does not always follow the ideal pattern of Proverbs 22:6” [26]; he quotes Elder Christofferson (April 2022) against “a cosmic vending machine” [21] (read the talk's own page before quoting it). The canon's own counterweights: Job, Ecclesiastes, [[Ps. 73]], and the pair 26:4–5. **Proposed:** the full note is at 22:6: what the saying says, that a proverb states how things generally go and is not a covenant promise, Matson's reading of agency, and the Book of Mormon's parents whose children strayed (from Matson). Chapter 10's `setting` says in one sentence that the sayings describe the usual course of a life and points to 22:6. The prosperity sayings are paraphrased as what they say; the notes don't add “usually” to the text, and don't say the text is wrong. Sayings that observe without approving (the bribe that “prospereth,” 17:8; the gift that “maketh room,” 18:16; 21:14) are read as observations, which the text's other sayings judge (17:23); say so from the text.
- **The “strange woman” and the women of 1–9 (2:16–19; 5; 6:24–35; 7; 9:13–18).** The words: “strange” and “stranger” (2:16) are *zarah* and *nokriyyah* (`hebrew.mjs`); the footnote at 23:33 gives “OR foreign women.” The Student Manual (2-10): the term “refers not only to foreigners (non-Israelites) and idolaters but also to unchaste women. It is often synonymous with harlot”; *Scripture Helps*: “the adulterous ‘strange woman,’ who seeks to lure the foolish from the path of righteousness” [21]; the text: she “forsaketh the guide of her youth, and forgetteth the covenant of her God” (2:17), so she is married. Davis: faithfulness to Wisdom “is represented by faithfulness to one's wife” [6] of the overview, and a caution: “we do have to be cautious that the images of the strange woman as a seducing temptress do not add to a general assumption that women should be treated as dangerous and that any interaction with them is hazardous” (1–9, [31]). Allis-Pike: “The wisdom of mothers may, to a real extent, account for the personification of wisdom as Woman Wisdom” [68], and the mother's law and the father's instruction are set “on similar, perhaps equal, footing” [66]. **Proposed:** the note at 2:16 gives the two words and the covenant of 2:17 (she is another man's wife who has left her covenant, not simply a foreigner); the book addresses a son, so the danger is drawn as a woman, and the young man is held responsible (6:32, “he that doeth it destroyeth his own soul”). Davis's caution goes once, at 5:3 or 7:10. The other women of 1–9 are named where they fall (the mother's law, 1:8; the wife of youth, 5:18; Wisdom herself), so that no chapter leaves the impression the book is against women. Corianton and Isabel (Alma 39) beside chapter 7 are Allis-Pike's comparison [70]–[74]: once, at 7:10 or in `explore`.
- **The woman of strength (31:10–31).** *Scripture Helps*: the poem is “about a woman who symbolizes the outcome of living according to the wisdom found throughout Proverbs,” and “a more accurate rendering would be ‘capable woman’ or ‘woman of strength’” [28]; *Come, Follow Me*: “a woman of great spiritual strength, capability, and influence” [22]; KnoWhy #639 (`sc-knowhy-639`): her “economic enterprises,” with Carol Meyers on women's work in the household; the women guide (`#valued`) already explains *chayil* and quotes *Scripture Helps*: link, don't repeat. **Proposed:** chapter 31 reads the poem as what *Scripture Helps* says it is, the book's last picture of wisdom lived (the book that began with Wisdom calling in the streets ends with a woman who “openeth her mouth with wisdom,” 31:26, and “feareth the LORD,” 31:30: the frame is the text's, and the note says only that much). Her work is said as the text gives it (fields bought, merchandise, the poor, the household), not softened into domestic sentiment and not made a modern career; the poem praises her for what she does and for her fear of the Lord, and 31:30 sets “beauty” aside. **No note makes her a checklist for Latter-day Saint women** or a measure anyone falls short of, and none reads a modern role into or out of the text. The acrostic: link `#acrostics`; say in a sentence that it runs aleph to tav (*Scripture Helps*' endnote 18), so the poem is “a complete” praise only if a source read says so.
- **The rod (13:24; 19:18; 22:15; 23:13–14; 29:15).** The text speaks of a real rod (*shevet*) and of beating (23:13–14), and the note says so plainly. The Church's newest word: *Scripture Helps*, “These scriptures are best understood as figurative expressions emphasizing the importance of loving and consistent discipline. They should not be used as a justification for physical abuse. Modern revelation teaches that discipline should be done in love and righteousness and never by force or violence” [26], citing the Proclamation (`family-proclamation`) and [[D&C 121:41–45]]; its endnote 15 points to Newell. Newell and others (`newell-rearing-children`): President Hinckley, “I have never accepted the principle of ‘spare the rod and spoil the child.’ … Children don’t need beating. They need love and encouragement” [51]; Brigham Young that kind words subdue a child “a great deal better than the rod, or, in other words, than physical punishment” [50]; the rod as the word of God, “One might choose to read this as …” [53], which is their suggestion. The Student Manual (2-24) quotes Brigham Young and George A. Smith (the remark “in nine cases out of ten referred to mental rather than physical correction”). **Proposed:** the full note is at 13:24: what the text says, in its world; then the Church's reading in *Scripture Helps*' words (it is the Church's statement of its position on a sensitive question, `STANDARDS.md` §1, so the two sentences may be quoted); then Hinckley's words as a prophet's teaching. The rod as the word of God is Newell's suggested reading, said as theirs, never as what the Hebrew means. 22:15 is beside *Scripture Helps*: point to 13:24. **No note defends physical punishment, and none says the verse never meant a rod.** Discipline from the Lord (3:11–12; Heb. 12) is explained at 3:11 and kept apart from the rod.
- **Agur's question, “what is his son's name” (30:4).** No source read comments on it. **Proposed:** the note at 30:4 (if a reader would ask, and they will) says what the questions point to in the text (the Creator who “gathered the wind in his fists”; Job 38 asks the same kind of question) and that no Church source read reads the son as Christ; if the writer finds one, it is given as its author's, and the Christ reading otherwise stays out of the note. The `christ` section may set 30:4 beside [[John 3:13]] as a comparison only.
- **Is Proverbs “worldly”?** McConkie: Proverbs and Ecclesiastes “fall far short of the spiritual power contained in some of their companion volumes” [6], and “Neither of these books espouses gospel principles” [8]. The Bible Dictionary is gentler and is the Church's own: “There is much in it that does not rise above the plane of worldly wisdom,” but “The least spiritual of the Proverbs are valuable as reminding us that the voice of divine inspiration does not disdain to utter homely truths.” Allis-Pike [22]: “while Proverbs may appear more temporal, it is also innately spiritual.” **Proposed:** follow the Bible Dictionary; McConkie's judgment is not repeated.
- **The Septuagint.** Read for this brief: Brenton's translation on Bible Hub (`lxx-rahlfs`) prints Proverbs in the Hebrew's chapter order, so it does not show where the Greek places 30–31; its 24:22 adds five lines (24:22A–E), and its 30:1 has no name Agur. **No source read here describes the Septuagint's different order for 24–31**: no note says the Greek orders the book differently until a writer reads that in a source (*How the Old Testament Came to Us*, `#hebrew-bible`, or a commentary) and cites it. A Septuagint reading goes into a note only where it answers a reader's question about the verse.

## Settled without the author

Decided by the standard, not asked of the author (Psalms' brief has the rule):

- **No Church, BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* source answers a reader's real question, and an older commentary does:** give its answer as “one older reading,” within the limit of a third of the notes. The Latter-day Saint studies cluster on 1–9 (Davis's *Old Testament Minute* covers only the overview and 1–9; Allis-Pike, Von Feldt and the KnoWhys are on 1–9) and on 22:6, 13:24 and 31. **Chapters 10–21 and 23–30 will lean on the text, the scriptures that quote it, the Church's footnotes and the older commentaries**, and need fewer notes; a short chapter resting on the text is better than one filled with the lexicon.
- **A scholar's reading that a Religious Studies Center, BYU or *Interpreter* publication prints** (Clifford in Davis, Shupak in KnoWhy #539, Murphy and Longman in Allis-Pike, Meyers in KnoWhy #639) is cited as that publication's author's, as one reading, beside the Church's, never above it.
- **Two faithful sources read a verse differently** (the rod: *Scripture Helps* and the Student Manual; the strange woman's scope): give both, the newer first, without choosing beyond what the standard settles.
- **A saying that seems to contradict another** (26:4–5): say what each says and what the text implies by placing them together; a reason only from a source read.
- **A source quoted inside a Church manual** whose own work can't be read (the *New Bible Commentary* in the Student Manual; the study Bibles *Scripture Helps* cites) is cited as the manual, for what the manual prints, as one view.
- **The Church's footnote points somewhere the note does not go:** leave it.
