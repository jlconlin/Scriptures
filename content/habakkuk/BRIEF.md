## What a chapter is for here

Habakkuk is 56 verses in three chapters, and it is unlike the other prophets: “Whereas most of the others contain the words of the Lord addressed to the people, in the Book of Habakkuk the prophet, as the representative of the people, addresses and challenges the Lord” (Sperry, as the Student Manual prints him, 22-1). The reader's questions follow from that: *Who was Habakkuk, and when? How can a prophet complain to God, and is it wrong to? Who are the Chaldeans, and why would the Lord use a people worse than Judah? What is the “burden,” the watchtower, the vision written on “tables” so “he may run that readeth it”? What does “the just shall live by his faith” mean here, and why did Paul build so much on it? Who is being taunted in the five woes? Why is chapter 3 a psalm, with “Shigionoth” and “Selah”? Where are Teman and Paran, and what is God doing in the storm, the sea and the standing sun? How can a man rejoice when the fig tree does not blossom?* A chapter answers those from the text first.

**Read the book as one conversation.** The prophet complains (1:2–4); the Lord answers (1:5–11); the prophet complains again (1:12–17) and waits on his tower (2:1); the Lord answers (2:2–20); the prophet prays and sings (3). Each chapter's `thread` says where its verses fall in that exchange, and who is speaking, since the King James text does not mark the change of speaker. The book's movement, from “how long shall I cry” (1:2) to “Yet I will rejoice in the LORD” (3:18), is the thread of the whole; Ball calls it the prophet coming to “embrace” the will of God, a play on his name (below).

**Don't moralize about Habakkuk.** Ball calls his attitude “somewhat proud and whining”; Rasmussen, “near fault-finding with the Lord.” The Lord does not rebuke him (Rasmussen: the answer “is not really reproof!”), and the Church's newest help calls his questions “bold and heartfelt” and says they “reflected his concern for his people” (*Scripture Helps*). Describe what he asks and let the Lord's answer judge it.

## Divisions

**Proposed: two divisions** (`book.yaml`). Chapters 1–2 (`habquestion`): the exchange, the prophet's two complaints and the Lord's two answers, ending with the five woes on the Chaldean and “the LORD is in his holy temple” (2:20). Chapter 3 (`habprayer`): “A prayer of Habakkuk the prophet,” the psalm with its own title and its musical subscription. The book marks the break itself with a second heading (3:1). Keil divides it so: “one single prophecy arranged in two parts,” the conversation in chapters 1 and 2 and the prayer in chapter 3; *Scripture Helps* reads it the same way (the exchange, then “Habakkuk's prayer and psalm of praise”). The Student Manual and the seminary guide count “two questions” and two answers inside the first part; that belongs in chapter 1's and chapter 2's threads, not in the divisions.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (which later scripture takes up which line, what a Hebrew word can mean) is the coordinator's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **When.** The book names no king. The Bible Dictionary: “uncertain—possibly in the reign of Josiah or of Jehoiakim (about 600 B.C.)”; *Scripture Helps*: “likely during the reigns of Josiah or Jehoiakim in the late 600s and early 500s BC”; the Guide: “possibly during the reign of Jehoiachin.” Welch: “ca. 609–598 B.C.”; KnoWhy #441: “ca. 622–605 (alt. 609–598) bc”; the Student Manual (22-2): after Carchemish (605) and “before the first deportation of the Jews in 597 B.C.”; Keil: before the Chaldeans came, since 1:5 announces them as future. **Proposed: every chapter's `when` is “Habakkuk prophesied in Judah about 600 B.C., probably under Josiah or Jehoiakim, as Babylon rose; the book names no king,”** with a ledger row from the Bible Dictionary or *Scripture Helps*. The range is given once, in chapter 1's setting, with the one clue in the text (“I will work a work in your days,” 1:5). Don't date it to a year.
- **Who.** “Habakkuk the prophet” (1:1; 3:1), with no father and no town; the Bible Dictionary: “Nothing beyond this is known about him.” The name, from *ḥābaq*, “to clasp or embrace”: “one who embraces or one who is embraced” (Ball; Keil). Ball's reading, that the prophet came to “embrace” the will of God, is devotional: once, as his. **The legends** (Habakkuk carrying food to Daniel in Bel and the Dragon; his tribe and tomb) are not used; Keil calls them “quite worthless.” That he was a temple singer (Keil, from 3:19; the Student Manual's “Possibly”) is one sentence at 3:19, as a possibility.
- **His contemporaries.** He was in Jerusalem with Jeremiah, Zephaniah and Lehi: link `[[Jer. 1:4]]` (the site's note already says so) and [Jeremiah, Lehi, and the Restoration](/jeremiah/guides/jeremiah-lehi-and-the-restoration/); don't retell Lehi's call. Welch sets 2:2 (“Write the vision”) beside the book Lehi read (`[[1 Ne. 1:11]]`): a sentence at 2:2, as his. That Habakkuk was on the plates of brass is a guess (KnoWhy #410, “may refer to”): not used.
- **The Chaldeans** (1:6). Explained once, at 1:6: `bd-chaldea` (“the portion of Babylonia that lay south and east of Babylon,” the name later used for the whole land); Rasmussen, Nabopolassar's “Neo-Babylonians.” Babylon's rise, Carchemish (605) and Judah's kings are in `[[Jer. 25:1]]`, `[[Jer. 36:1]]`, [Babylon and the Exile](/old-testament/guides/babylon-and-the-exile/#rulers) and [Jeremiah's World](/jeremiah/guides/jeremiahs-world/): link, don't retell. The images of 1:8–17 (horses swifter than leopards; the fisherman who sacrifices “unto their net”; the footnotes “OR hook,” “OR net”) are explained where they stand.
- **“Burden”** (1:1). The Hebrew *massāʾ* (`hebrew.mjs habakkuk 1:1`: H4853). If the site has explained “burden” as an oracle in Isaiah or Nahum, link it; otherwise one sentence at 1:1 from the lexicon page. Not a Language note.
- **Names of God.** Copy every phrase from `data/kjv/habakkuk.json` (“mine Holy One,” 1:12; “the Holy One,” 3:3; “The LORD God,” 3:19). That the LORD of the Old Testament is the premortal Christ is said once, in chapter 3's `christ` section, where the footnote at 3:13 invites it.
- **Verse numbers.** The Hebrew and King James numbering agree in all three chapters (`hebrew.mjs` prints no differences).
- **The Septuagint** matters only at 2:3–4 (Brenton: “though he should tarry, wait for him”; “the just shall live by my faith”), which Hebrews 10:37–38 follows: explained at 2:4.
- **Other books on the site.** Link; don't say who borrowed from whom unless a source read does. 1:2–4 and `[[Jer. 12:1]]` (the Guide names Jeremiah 12 and D&C 121 as like it; the site's note treats the question); 1:2 and `[[D&C 121:1–6]]` and `[[Mark 4:37–38]]` (*Come, Follow Me*); 1:5 and `[[Acts 13:40–41]]` (the footnote: Paul quotes it at Pisidian Antioch); 2:3 and `[[Heb. 10:37]]`; 2:14 and `[[Isa. 11:9]]` (below); 2:18 and `[[Isa. 44:9–20]]`; 2:20 and `[[Zeph. 1:7]]`, `[[Ps. 11:4]]`; 3:3 and `[[Deut. 33:2]]` (the site's Deuteronomy 33 `explore` already points to Hab. 3:3–6); 3:6 and `[[Isa. 54:10]]` (the site's note quotes “the everlasting mountains”); 3:11 and `[[Josh. 10:12–13]]`, `[[Hel. 12:15]]`; 3:15 and `[[Josh. 3:16]]`; 3:19 and `[[Deut. 32:13]]`, and `[[Ps. 18:33]]` (a lead).
- **Mormon 4:5.** The Bible Dictionary's entry on Habakkuk sends the reader to `[[Morm. 4:5]]`: “it is by the wicked that the wicked are punished.” This is the Restoration's clearest word on the book's question. Use it once, at 1:12–13 or 2:8, and the other chapters point there.
- **Liberty Jail.** Joseph Smith's “O God, where art thou?” (`[[D&C 121:1–6]]`) is the parallel every source draws (the footnote at 1:2; Van Dyke and Ogden; Brown; Rasmussen; *Come, Follow Me*), and the site already uses it in Isaiah (`[[Isa. 6:11]]`, `[[Isa. 42:14]]`, `[[Isa. 45:15]]`). **Proposed:** one note, at 1:2, linking those; the Lord's answer (`[[D&C 121:7–8]]`) beside 2:3. No other note reaches for Liberty Jail.
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** None in Habakkuk (the footnotes show none).

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that three chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-23.

- **The dialogue: may a prophet question God? (1:2–4, 12–17; 2:1).** The sources agree the book is a conversation (*Scripture Helps*, “an exchange between Habakkuk and the Lord”; the seminary guide, “two questions he asked the Lord, the Lord's answers, and a prayer”). They differ on tone: Ball (“proud and whining”) and Rasmussen (“near fault-finding”) judge the prophet; *Scripture Helps* calls his questions “bold and heartfelt.” Rasmussen's sentence on 2:1 is the useful one: Habakkuk expects to be “reproved,” and the answer “is not really reproof!” **Proposed:** follow *Scripture Helps* and the text. He asks out of concern for his people and the law (“the law is slacked,” 1:4; footnote: “HEB grows cold”), the Lord answers twice, and he waits on his watch (2:1; the prophet as watchman, compare `[[Ezek. 3:17]]`, a lead).
- **Theodicy: why use the Chaldeans? (1:5–17; 2:5–20).** The book's question: how can God, “of purer eyes than to behold evil” (1:13), let the wicked devour “the man that is more righteous than he”? The Bible Dictionary: “This raises the problem as to why the pure and holy God should use for the working out of His purposes such a sinful nation. Hab. 2 supplies a solution to the problem: the Chaldeans themselves will come under judgment.” Van Dyke and Ogden: Habakkuk “prophesied against Babylon in the same way that Isaiah prophesied against Assyria (see Isaiah 10).” Draper reads the question as showing how widely Judah thought itself wronged, beside Laman and Lemuel's “a righteous people” (`[[1 Ne. 17:22]]`): a sentence, as his. Ball's note calls it “a theodicy,” with Job and D&C 121–22. **Proposed:** give the book's own answer, without adding a philosophy: the Chaldean's triumph is temporary and he will be judged (the woes); the just live by faithfulness while they wait (2:3–4); the Lord is in His temple (2:20). `[[Morm. 4:5]]` (above) is the Restoration's word; chapter 1's `explore` may point to `[[Isa. 10:5–15]]`. **Don't** call Judah “more righteous” as a fact: it is the prophet's complaint, and the Lord's answer begins from Judah's wickedness (1:2–4).
- **“The just shall live by his faith” (2:4).** The heart of the book, and the verse where the Hebrew pays. The Hebrew is *beʾĕmûnātô* (`hebrew.mjs habakkuk 2:4`: H530, *ʾĕmûnâ*); the Church's footnote: “HEB faithfulness, steadfastness.” *Scripture Helps*: the word “can also mean ‘firmness,’ ‘steadfastness,’ or ‘faithfulness,’” and the verse contrasts “the proud Babylonians, whose souls were ‘lifted up’ and ‘not upright,’” with the righteous; it was “a call for the people of Judah to trust Him and remain faithful while waiting for His promises to be fulfilled.” Reynolds (RSC, 2020): *ʾĕmûnâ* “denoted not just ‘faith’ but durative and perdurative ‘faithfulness’ or ‘steadfastness’”; Bowen (*Interpreter*, 2023) gives the NRSVue's “the righteous live by their faithfulness.” *Scripture Helps*' endnote 35 points to the same word in `[[Ex. 17:12]]`, Moses's hands “steady” till sunset: a strong illustration, worth one sentence, from the endnote and the verse. Sperry (in the Student Manual, 22-5): “more accurately faithfulness,” and “he implies permanency.” **Proposed: one note at 2:4, a Language note, that gives the Hebrew, the footnote, *Scripture Helps*' reading and Exodus 17:12, and says what the contrast in the verse is (the proud “lifted up” against the faithful who endure).** Faith and faithfulness are one thing here, trust that holds over time; the note does not set them against each other.
- **2:4 in the New Testament and the Restoration.** Paul quotes it (`[[Rom. 1:17]]`; `[[Gal. 3:11]]`), and Hebrews (`[[Heb. 10:38]]`), as “The just shall live by faith” (*Scripture Helps*: “to emphasize the eternal importance of faith”). Hebrews follows the Septuagint of 2:3–4, which makes the vision a person who “will surely come” and reads “by my faith,” God's faithfulness (Brenton). Strathearn reads Paul's *ek pisteōs* as “Christ's faith,” noting that “the Septuagint has interpreted Habakkuk 2:4 messianically”: a sentence, as hers. J. Ludlow sets Galatians 3:11 beside Leviticus 18:5 (the site's Leviticus 18 links it). **Joseph Smith used the verse** on 29 September 1839, as James Mulholland reported it (*The Words of Joseph Smith*): “it is a false idea that the saints will escape all the judgements whilst the wicked suffer … still many of the Saints will escape—for the just shall live by faith.” It speaks to Habakkuk's own question; quote it once in chapter 2. **Proposed:** a second note at 2:4 (or chapter 2's `christ`): the word that told Judah to hold on through Babylon became, in Paul, the gospel's summary of how anyone is made righteous. Luther's use is reception history and is left out. President Uchtdorf's sentence may close it.
- **The vision written on tables (2:2–3).** “That he may run that readeth it”: legible at a glance, or a herald who runs with it (Welch: “to carry forth with speed”; KnoWhy #17). **Proposed:** give both, Welch's as his; don't pick. 2:3 (“though it tarry, wait for it”) is the verse *Come, Follow Me* builds on; the note says what the appointed time is in context (the Chaldean's fall) and links `[[Heb. 10:37]]`.
- **The five woes (2:6–20).** A taunt song in the mouths of the nations Babylon took: “Shall not all these take up a parable against him” (2:6). Sperry (Student Manual): “five woes upon the corrupt traits in the enemy's character”; Welch: woes “upon extortioners, exploiters, debauchery, and idolatry.” A short note for each; the stone and beam that cry out (2:11; footnote “OR a rafter out of the woodwork will witness it”); the cup of the Lord's right hand (2:16: link [The Cup of the Lord's Fury](/jeremiah/themes/cup-of-fury/), don't explain the cup again); the idols (2:18–19). **Describe 2:15–16 plainly and briefly** (`STANDARDS.md` §3). 2:20 closes them; Van Dyke and Ogden paraphrase it “Be still; I'm aware of your concerns; know that I am God”: as theirs. Rasmussen's note that the verse “is inscribed in the Idaho Falls Temple” is used only if a Church page confirms it.
- **2:14 and Isaiah 11:9.** The same promise, with Habakkuk's “of the glory.” The site's note on `[[Isa. 11:9]]` reads it of the Millennium (`[[D&C 101:32–34]]`). At 2:14 the point is where it stands: in the middle of woes on an empire built “with blood” (2:12–13), the Lord says the earth will be full of His glory, not Babylon's. Rasmussen calls it “the millennial promise.” One note; link Isaiah's, don't repeat it.
- **The psalm of chapter 3: Shigionoth and Selah.** “Shigionoth” (3:1): the footnote, “A type of poetry”; BDB (H7692), “perhaps a wild passionate song with rapid changes of rhythm”; the site's note on `[[Ps. 7:1]]` (“Shiggaion”) already names Hab. 3:1: link. Not the Student Manual's stringed instrument. “Selah” (3:3, 9, 13): the Bible Dictionary, “A musical term that occurs 71 times in the Psalms and three times in Habakkuk. Its meaning is uncertain”; the site's Psalm 3 note explains it: link. **Proposed:** chapter 3's setting says once that the chapter is a psalm, with a title, pauses and a choir-leader's subscription (3:19), and may have been sung in the temple (as a possibility); no note on Selah.
- **The theophany (3:3–15).** God comes from the south in storm and light. Teman is Edomite (the site's note on `[[Jer. 49:7]]`); Paran, “The desert between Judea and Sinai” (`bd-paran`). The footnote at 3:3: “This alludes to historic occasions when the Lord miraculously delivered the people,” with `[[Deut. 33:2]]`. Huntington reads 3:10 as the Red Sea, 3:11 as Joshua's sun and moon, 3:12–13 as the conquest (he cites them as “Habakkuk 2:12–13,” a slip), told to strengthen faith in “God's power in their own affairs.” The Student Manual: “The burden of Habakkuk's prayer is for Jehovah to return and sustain Israel as in days of old.” **Proposed:** chapter 3's thread says the psalm recalls the Exodus and the conquest to ask for them again (“revive thy work in the midst of the years … in wrath remember mercy,” 3:2); notes explain each allusion from the footnotes (3:7 with `[[Judg. 3:10]]`, `[[Num. 31]]`; 3:11; 3:15). Canaanite storm-god parallels are not needed. For “lifted up his hands on high” (3:10), read Calabro first.
- **“Thine anointed” (3:13).** “Thou wentest forth for the salvation of thy people, even for salvation with thine anointed.” The footnote gives “TG Jesus Christ, Prophecies about.” The anointed in the psalm is the king, or Israel (a lead from the lexicon and Keil); the Church's footnote reads it of Christ. **Proposed:** the note says what *māšîaḥ* (anointed) is, that the verse is read in the Church's footnotes as pointing to Christ, and keeps the two apart. The word “salvation” here is *yēšaʿ* (`hebrew.mjs habakkuk 3:13`), related to the name Jesus (*Yeshua*): one sentence, only if a source read says so.
- **“Although the fig tree shall not blossom” (3:17–19).** The book's answer: everything the farm gives may fail, “Yet I will rejoice in the LORD.” Rasmussen sets it beside `[[Job 13:15]]`; Ball calls it the prophet's “change of heart”; Van Dyke and Ogden: “Everything in the telestial world is prone to collapse except the Lord.” **Proposed:** the note says what the losses would mean in Judah (famine; the siege brought it: link the site's note on `[[Deut. 28:53]]`), and that the joy is “in the God of my salvation,” not in the harvest. The hinds' feet and high places (3:19): the footnotes' `[[Deut. 32:13]]` and `[[Deut. 33:29]]`. Chapter 3's `christ` section applies it.
- **“In wrath remember mercy” (3:2).** The verb is *rāḥam*, “have compassion” (H7355). One note; link the site's notes on `[[Lam. 3:22]]` and `[[Lam. 3:25]]`.
