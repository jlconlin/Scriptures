Three times in his speeches Moses tells Israel that the LORD was angry with him “for your sakes” and would not let him cross the Jordan ([[Deut. 1:37]]; [[Deut. 3:26]]; [[Deut. 4:21]]). The usual way to read those verses is as a footnote to Numbers 20: Moses struck the rock, and this was the price. I want to read them differently, as Deuteronomy's own teaching about what the Lord does with a righteous request He will not grant. The book sets the refusal among prayers it says the LORD heard, binds it to the people Moses served, answers it with a word that twice before had meant “enough of this, now the next thing,” and follows it at once with a sight, a charge, and a life kept for work beyond the river. That work brought Moses to a mountain where he spoke with Jesus of the death Jesus was about to die, before the Son's own plea in Gethsemane was refused. Put Pisgah and Gethsemane side by side and a pattern appears. A righteous prayer refused is one of the ways God answers, not a sign that He has not heard, and the One who said “enough” to Moses later knelt under the same answer.

## Heard, and not heard

Begin with the prayers Deuteronomy says were heard. After the golden calf Moses “fell down before the LORD, as at the first, forty days and forty nights” ([[Deut. 9:18]]) and prayed, “O Lord GOD, destroy not thy people and thine inheritance, which thou hast redeemed through thy greatness” ([[Deut. 9:26]]). He reports the outcome twice, in the same words: “the LORD hearkened unto me at that time also” ([[Deut. 9:19]]; [[Deut. 10:10]]). Exodus tells the scene from outside, “the LORD repented of the evil which he thought to do unto his people” ([[Ex. 32:14]]), and Numbers records another of his prayers heard, at Kadesh: “I have pardoned according to thy word” ([[Num. 14:20]]). This is the Moses later scripture remembers, the man who “stood before him in the breach, to turn away his wrath” ([[Ps. 106:23]]), one of those who “called upon the LORD, and he answered them” ([[Ps. 99:6]]).

Now the verb. “Hearkened” in 9:19 and 10:10 and “would not hear me” in 3:26 are the same Hebrew verb, *shamaʿ*, in the same construction, literally “hear unto me,” twice affirmed and once negated.[@oshb-wlc] With a person as its object the construction means to hear and answer.[@bdb] So the book that reports the refusal is the book that twice reports, in the identical phrase, that God answered this man. Deuteronomy even tells the refusal first, in chapter 3, and the intercessions afterward, in chapters 9 and 10, though in time Horeb came long before Pisgah. A reader who takes the book in order meets the refused prayer before hearing a word about the sin at Meribah, and then meets, twice, the prayers that were heard. Whatever 3:26 is, Deuteronomy has made it impossible to read as the prayer of a man God had stopped listening to.

## Half a yes

Here is the prayer itself:

> And I besought the LORD at that time, saying, O Lord GOD, thou hast begun to shew thy servant thy greatness, and thy mighty hand: for what God is there in heaven or in earth, that can do according to thy works, and according to thy might? I pray thee, let me go over, and see the good land that is beyond Jordan, that goodly mountain, and Lebanon. ([[Deut. 3:23–25]])

“Besought” is a form of *chanan*, to be gracious; the form means to seek or implore favor, and here it is addressed to God.[@bdb] Moses is not claiming a right. He argues from what he has seen: the LORD has “begun to shew” His greatness, so let the servant see the work finished (the note “What Moses had seen begun” on [chapter 3](/deuteronomy/3/) follows that argument). And the request has two verbs. He asks to go over, and he asks to see.

The answer takes up the first verb and turns it. “But the LORD was wroth with me” ([[Deut. 3:26]]), and the Hebrew for “was wroth,” *vayitʿabber*, is built on the same root letters, ʿ-b-r, as “let me go over” in verse 25 and “thou shalt not go over” in verse 27.[@oshb-wlc] The Cambridge commentary reads the verb as literally “to exceed bounds”;[@cambridge-deuteronomy] the lexicon files it as a verb of its own, “infuriate oneself,” and finds no other instance of it in the five books of Moses.[@bdb] I do not claim the text intends a pun. I do say that a reader of the Hebrew meets the same root on both sides of the refusal, in the request, in the anger, and in the denial, and that the effect is to make the anger answer the asking.

But only the first verb is refused. The second is granted, and granted in the very word Moses used. “Get thee up into the top of Pisgah, and lift up thine eyes westward, and northward, and southward, and eastward, and behold it with thine eyes: for thou shalt not go over this Jordan” ([[Deut. 3:27]]). “Behold” here is the *raʾah* of “let me… see” two verses earlier,[@oshb-wlc] and the last words the LORD speaks to Moses in the book are built on the same pair: “I have caused thee to see it with thine eyes, but thou shalt not go over thither” ([[Deut. 34:4]]). Half the prayer was refused and half was granted, and the text says so in the words the prayer was made of. That is the first thing to notice about this refusal. It is not a flat no.

## Whose fault

Why was the other half refused? Scripture gives more than one answer and does not reconcile them, and I will not pretend to.

Moses' own account is “for your sakes,” all three times, though the Hebrew phrase is different each time.[@oshb-wlc] Numbers, and the LORD's own words at the end of Deuteronomy, point instead to what Moses did at Meribah: “Because ye believed me not, to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel” ([[Num. 20:12]]); “ye rebelled against my commandment in the desert of Zin, in the strife of the congregation” ([[Num. 27:14]]); “ye sanctified me not in the midst of the children of Israel” ([[Deut. 32:51]]). Told to speak to the rock, he struck it twice and asked the people, “Must we fetch you water out of this rock?”[@scripture-helps-num] A psalm holds both accounts in one breath: “it went ill with Moses for their sakes: Because they provoked his spirit, so that he spake unadvisedly with his lips” ([[Ps. 106:32–33]]). And latter-day revelation adds a third, about why Moses was taken from Israel at all: the people “hardened their hearts and could not endure” the Lord's presence, “Therefore, he took Moses out of their midst, and the Holy Priesthood also” ([[D&C 84:24–25]]).

The Church's own helps leave this open. “The scriptures do not fully explain the reasons for this consequence.”[@scripture-helps-num] The Student Manual notes that two passages “indicate that Moses did sin,” while “for your sakes” “could imply that there were reasons other than the error of Moses.”[@ot-manual-numbers-13-36] Allred, in the *Old Testament Minute*, offers with a “perhaps” that the cause lies in the only setting Deuteronomy itself gives for the LORD's anger, the sending of the spies ([[Deut. 1:22–23]]).[@ot-minute-deut-3]

What I take from the disagreement is the thing all three accounts share. Each binds the refusal to the people. “For your sakes” says so outright. The sentence from Meribah lays the fault on Moses, yet frames it as a failure “in the eyes of the children of Israel,” “in the strife of the congregation.” The revelation's account is entirely about what the people could not endure. Whatever Moses did, he did it as their mediator, in their quarrel, and he is kept out of the land with them in view. That is the second thing to notice. The mediator's lot was tied to the people's. (The note “Why Moses could not cross” on [chapter 3](/deuteronomy/3/) sets the passages out in full.)

## Enough

Then comes the word that closes the matter: “Let it suffice thee; speak no more unto me of this matter” ([[Deut. 3:26]]). In Hebrew it is *rav lakh*, “enough for you,” an exclamation the lexicon hears with an “implication of excess,” the same words Korah's company hurled at Moses, “ye assume too much”[@bdb] (“Ye take too much upon you,” [[Num. 16:3]]). There is an edge to it. There is also, as Allred hears it, kindness: the Lord “kindly acknowledged Moses's yearning.”[@ot-minute-deut-3]

But Deuteronomy has used the phrase twice before, and the book's own usage is the best guide to its meaning here. Both times it is said to all Israel, in the plural, and both times it means “you have done this long enough”:[@bdb] “Ye have dwelt long enough in this mount: Turn you, and take your journey” ([[Deut. 1:6–7]]); “Ye have compassed this mountain long enough: turn you northward” ([[Deut. 2:3]]). In both places the word does not end Israel's story. It ends one stage and starts the next in the same sentence. Keil hears 3:26 the same way, “as in Deuteronomy 1:6.”[@keil-deuteronomy]

Read in the company of those two verses, “Let it suffice thee” sounds less like a door shutting than like the word that has moved Israel forward twice already, and in 3:26 too the next thing comes in the next breath: “Get thee up into the top of Pisgah… But charge Joshua, and encourage him, and strengthen him” ([[Deut. 3:27–28]]). I cannot say what the Lord meant by the word. I can say that inside this book, “enough” has never yet meant only “stop.” (The note “Enough for you” on [chapter 3](/deuteronomy/3/) points to the same verses.)

## What he was given

So what did Moses receive? Three things, and none of them is a consolation prize.

The first is the land seen whole. “Lift up thine eyes… and behold it with thine eyes” ([[Deut. 3:27]]) is fulfilled on Nebo, where “the LORD shewed him all the land of Gilead, unto Dan” ([[Deut. 34:1]]). The second is a successor charged: Joshua “shall go over before this people” ([[Deut. 3:28]]), a man whose name means “Jehovah is salvation”[@scripture-helps-num] (the Witness of Christ on [chapter 3](/deuteronomy/3/) follows where that name leads).

The third is a life kept. The text says “Moses the servant of the LORD died there in the land of Moab” and that “no man knoweth of his sepulchre unto this day” ([[Deut. 34:5–6]]). The Joseph Smith Translation reads instead that “the Lord took him unto his Fathers,”[@scripture-helps-deut] and the Book of Mormon says “the Lord took Moses unto himself” ([[Alma 45:19]]). Because Moses came to the Mount of Transfiguration before the Resurrection, “Moses was a translated being and had not died as reported in Deut. 34”; he needed “a body of flesh and bones at the time of the Transfiguration.”[@bd-moses] Moses and Elijah were preserved from death “because they had a mission to perform, and it had to be performed before the crucifixion of the Son of God.”[@ot-manual-deuteronomy-17-33] His translation, as the Student Manual says, was “hardly a punishment.”[@ot-manual-numbers-13-36] (The notes “Died, or translated?” and “Who buried Moses” on [chapter 34](/deuteronomy/34/) give the detail; the keys Moses later brought to Kirtland, [[D&C 110:11]], are a further work on the far side of the same refusal.)

Put the three together and the refusal looks different. Moses was denied forty more years in Canaan and given, in their place, the whole land in one sight, a man to lead the people in, and a body kept for an errand no mortal could have run. The refusal was the shape his next assignment took.

## The mountain

That assignment brought him to a mountain in the Savior's company. Jesus “went up into a mountain to pray. And as he prayed, the fashion of his countenance was altered… And, behold, there talked with him two men, which were Moses and Elias: Who appeared in glory, and spake of his decease which he should accomplish at Jerusalem” ([[Luke 9:28–31]]). The Greek for “decease” is *exodos*, departure, “the close of one's career”;[@thayer] the Joseph Smith Translation has them speak “of his death, and also his resurrection.”[@nt-student-manual-6] And the Student Manual, citing Talmage, adds the sentence this whole essay turns on: “Apparently Jesus himself was strengthened and encouraged by Moses and Elijah so as to be prepared for the infinite sufferings and agony ahead of him.”[@nt-student-manual-6]

Consider who is standing there. The man whose one prayer for himself was refused “for your sakes” has been kept alive to stand with the Son and speak of the Son's death, and, if Talmage is right, to strengthen Him for an agony in which the Son's own prayer for Himself will be refused, so that He can suffer “for all” ([[D&C 19:16]]). The same thing is about to happen to the Son that happened to the servant, and the servant is there.

## The garden

In Gethsemane Jesus “fell on his face, and prayed, saying, O my Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me: nevertheless not as I will, but as thou wilt” ([[Matt. 26:39]]). Mark's version is blunter: “Abba, Father, all things are possible unto thee; take away this cup from me” ([[Mark 14:36]]). He prayed a second time, “if this cup may not pass away from me, except I drink it, thy will be done” ([[Matt. 26:42]]), and “the third time, saying the same words” ([[Matt. 26:44]]). Luke adds, “there appeared an angel unto him from heaven, strengthening him” ([[Luke 22:43]]). His own account of it, given to Joseph Smith: “would that I might not drink the bitter cup, and shrink—Nevertheless, glory be to the Father, and I partook” ([[D&C 19:18–19]]).

The answer was a refusal. Elder Oaks says it as plainly as it can be said: “The Father's answer was to deny the plea of his Only Begotten Son. The Atonement had to be worked out by that lamb without blemish. But though the Son's request was denied, his prayer was answered.”[@oaks-1994-faith] Elder Holland: He pled that the cup pass, “But, of course, it could not pass.”[@holland-none-with-him] “He waited upon His Father, saying, ‘Thy will be done,’”[@hales-waiting] and “because of His love for His Father and for us, He endured.”[@christofferson-abide-2016]

Hebrews describes these prayers,[@lds-scriptures] and describes them with a word that should stop us: He “offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears unto him that was able to save him from death, and was heard in that he feared” ([[Heb. 5:7]]). The Greek verb in the passive means “to be heard, to have one's request granted,” and Thayer gives that sense for this very verse.[@thayer] Yet the cup did not pass, and Hebrews never says what was granted. Set that beside Deuteronomy. Of Moses the LORD “would not hear me” ([[Deut. 3:26]]); of the Son, He “was heard.” Moses did not cross, and the cup did not pass. One prayer is told as unheard and the other as heard, and in neither was the thing asked for given. The apostles say what that means: a prayer can be heard and answered without its request being granted,[@oaks-1994-faith] “even if … not answered the way I asked.”[@holland-hidden-fire-2024]

One step further, and it is mine. In Latter-day Saint teaching “Jehovah is the premortal Jesus Christ,”[@bd-jehovah] and “usually, when the word Lord appears in the Old Testament, it means ‘Jehovah’”;[@gs-jehovah] the risen Christ Himself said, “I am he that gave the law” ([[3 Ne. 15:5]]). Then the One who told Moses, “speak no more unto me of this matter,” is the One who prayed three times “saying the same words” and ended, “thy will be done.” He asked of Moses nothing He would not Himself bear. No Church source draws that line between the two scenes, and I draw it as a reading, not a doctrine; but once drawn it is hard to unsee. (The cross and the psalms of lament belong to another essay, Psalms' [The Savior Enters the Unanswered Prayer](/psalms/themes/the-unanswered-prayer/).)

## Sufficient

Paul had a refused prayer of his own, and he told it in words that echo Moses'. “For this thing I besought the Lord thrice, that it might depart from me. And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness” ([[2 Cor. 12:8–9]]). Keil calls “Let it suffice thee” “substantially equivalent to” that answer,[@keil-deuteronomy] and the parallel holds at every point: a servant asks three times, the thing is refused, “the Lord declined to heal him,”[@oaks-2006-heals] and something else is given, strength enough[@nt-student-manual-42] to say “when I am weak, then am I strong” ([[2 Cor. 12:10]]).

That is where this arc reaches a disciple. A righteous request can be refused. “Sometimes our righteous desires are not granted in this life.”[@hales-2019-answers] “Some challenges in life will not be resolved here on earth.”[@nt-student-manual-42] But the refusal is not silence, and it is not a verdict on the one who asked. Elder Holland, speaking of prayers for his own health and his wife's, testified that both “were heard and answered by a divinely compassionate Heavenly Father, even if the prayers for Pat were not answered the way I asked.”[@holland-hidden-fire-2024] “The object of prayer is not to change the will of God.”[@bd-prayer]

A disciple who asks for a good thing, having lived so as to expect it, and is told “enough,” stands where Moses stood on the plains of Moab, where Paul stood with his thorn, and where the Savior knelt in the garden. Each was heard. None received what he asked. Each was given something else, and in Moses' case the something else turned out to be the work of his life. “Sometimes we are ‘healed’ by being given strength or understanding or patience to bear the burdens placed upon us,”[@oaks-2006-heals] and “in either case the Redeemer will support you.”[@nt-student-manual-42] The Lord said the rest to a prisoner in Liberty Jail:[@lds-scriptures] “all these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good” ([[D&C 122:7]]).
