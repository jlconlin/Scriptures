Three times in Deuteronomy Moses tells Israel that the Lord was angry with him “for your sakes” and would not let him cross the Jordan. The usual way to read those verses is as a footnote to the book of Numbers. Moses struck the rock at Meribah when he had been told to speak to it, and being kept out of the land was the price. That reading is not wrong, but it is not what Deuteronomy itself does with the story.

Deuteronomy does something more interesting. It sets the refused prayer among prayers it says God granted. It ties the refusal to the people Moses served rather than to Moses alone. It answers him with a word the book had already used twice, and used both times to mean “enough of this; now the next thing.” And it follows the refusal, in the very next sentence, with a sight, a charge, and a life kept for work beyond the river. That work eventually brought Moses to a mountain where he spoke with Jesus about the death Jesus was about to die, not long before Jesus prayed a prayer of His own that the Father refused.

Set Pisgah beside Gethsemane and a pattern comes into view. A righteous prayer refused is one of the ways God answers, not a sign that He has not heard. And the One who told Moses “enough” later knelt under the same answer Himself. That is the argument of this essay.

## Heard, and not heard

Deuteronomy is unusual among the books of Moses because almost all of it is Moses talking. He stands on the plains of Moab, with Israel about to cross the Jordan without him, and tells the story of the last forty years in his own words. Twice in that telling he remembers a prayer that God granted. The occasion was the golden calf. He lay before the Lord for forty days asking that the people not be destroyed ([[Deut. 9:18]]; [[Deut. 9:26]]), and the Lord relented. Moses reports it in chapter 9 and again in chapter 10, in the same words both times: “the LORD hearkened unto me at that time also” ([[Deut. 9:19]]; [[Deut. 10:10]]).

There is one other prayer in Deuteronomy that Moses remembers, and it is the only one he prayed for himself. He asked to go over the river and see the land ([[Deut. 3:25]]). This time the Lord would not hear him. That is Moses' own phrase for it, and it is a hard one: “the LORD was wroth with me for your sakes, and would not hear me” ([[Deut. 3:26]]).

It matters that these are the same words. In Hebrew, “hearkened unto me” and “would not hear me” are one verb in one construction,[@oshb-wlc] and when its object is a person the verb does not mean simply to listen. It means to hear and to grant.[@bdb] So Moses is applying one measure to all three prayers. By that measure God granted two of them and refused one.

This is worth dwelling on, because of what it rules out. The natural way to read the refusal at the Jordan is as the story of a man whose standing with God had slipped, the price of what he did at Meribah. Deuteronomy will not let a reader do that. The same book, in the same voice, with the same verb, says that God answered this man. It even puts the refusal first, in chapter 3, well before the prayers that were heard in chapters 9 and 10. Someone reading the book in order meets the no before the two yeses, and meets all three before a word is said about Meribah. Whatever the refusal is, it is not deafness, and it is not a verdict on Moses.

## Half a yes

Here is the prayer as Moses tells it:

> And I besought the LORD at that time, saying, O Lord GOD, thou hast begun to shew thy servant thy greatness, and thy mighty hand: for what God is there in heaven or in earth, that can do according to thy works, and according to thy might? I pray thee, let me go over, and see the good land that is beyond Jordan, that goodly mountain, and Lebanon. ([[Deut. 3:23–25]])

Notice first how he asks. “Besought” translates a Hebrew form that means to seek favor, to plead for grace.[@bdb] Moses is not claiming something owed to him. He is asking for a kindness, and he builds his case on what he has already seen. The Lord has “begun to shew” him His greatness, he says; let the servant see the work finished. (The note “What Moses had seen begun” on [chapter 3](/deuteronomy/3/) follows that argument through.)

Notice next that he asks for two things. He asks to go over, and he asks to see. They are separate verbs in the Hebrew as in the English, and the answer treats them separately.

The first is refused. “Thou shalt not go over this Jordan,” the Lord says ([[Deut. 3:27]]), and at the end of the book, on the top of Nebo, He says it again: “thou shalt not go over thither” ([[Deut. 34:4]]).

The second is granted, and granted in the same word Moses used. Where Moses asked to “see,” the Lord tells him to climb Pisgah and “behold it with thine eyes” ([[Deut. 3:27]]); “see” and “behold” are one Hebrew verb.[@oshb-wlc] And the last words the Lord speaks to Moses in the book pair the two halves of the prayer exactly: “I have caused thee to see it with thine eyes, but thou shalt not go over thither” ([[Deut. 34:4]]).

So the refusal was never a flat no. Half of what Moses asked was refused, and half was given, and the text says so in the very words he had prayed with. That is the first thing to notice about this refusal, and it changes the way the rest should be read.

## For your sakes

Why was the other half refused? Scripture gives more than one answer, and it does not reconcile them. It would be dishonest for this essay to pretend otherwise, so let me lay the answers out.

Moses' own answer, every time he gives it, is “for your sakes” ([[Deut. 1:37]]; [[Deut. 3:26]]; [[Deut. 4:21]]). The Hebrew phrase is a little different in each place,[@oshb-wlc] but the sense is the same. The people are the reason.

Numbers gives a different answer, and so does the Lord Himself at the end of Deuteronomy. At Meribah Moses was told to speak to the rock. Instead he struck it twice and asked the people, “Must we fetch you water out of this rock?”[@scripture-helps-num] The Lord's verdict was that Moses and Aaron had not believed Him enough “to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel” ([[Num. 20:12]]), and in Deuteronomy 32, just before Moses climbs the mountain to die, the Lord repeats it: “ye sanctified me not in the midst of the children of Israel” ([[Deut. 32:51]]). On this account the fault is Moses' own.

A psalm manages to hold both answers in a single breath. “It went ill with Moses for their sakes,” it says, “because they provoked his spirit, so that he spake unadvisedly with his lips” ([[Ps. 106:32–33]]). The people provoked him, and he spoke rashly. Both things are true at once.

Latter-day revelation adds a third account, which is really about something larger. The children of Israel “hardened their hearts and could not endure” the Lord's presence, and so “he took Moses out of their midst, and the Holy Priesthood also” ([[D&C 84:24–25]]). Here Moses is taken from Israel because of what Israel could not bear.

The Church's own study helps leave the question open, and so will I. “The scriptures do not fully explain the reasons for this consequence,” *Scripture Helps* says.[@scripture-helps-num] The Student Manual notes that two passages “indicate that Moses did sin,” while “for your sakes” “could imply that there were reasons other than the error of Moses.”[@ot-manual-numbers-13-36] One Latter-day Saint commentator, writing with a “perhaps,” traces it back to the sending of the spies, the one event Deuteronomy itself names as the setting for the Lord's anger.[@ot-minute-deut-3]

What I take from all this is not a verdict but the thing every account has in common. Each of them ties the refusal to the people. “For your sakes” says it outright. The Meribah account, which blames Moses, still describes his failure as something that happened “in the eyes of the children of Israel” and “in the strife of the congregation.” The revelation's account is entirely about what the people could not endure. Whatever Moses did, he did it as their mediator, in the middle of their quarrel, and when he is kept out of the land they are the reason given. The mediator's fate was bound to the people's. That is the second thing to notice. (The note “Why Moses could not cross” on [chapter 3](/deuteronomy/3/) sets out all of the passages in full.)

## Enough

Then comes the sentence that closes the matter: “Let it suffice thee; speak no more unto me of this matter” ([[Deut. 3:26]]).

In Hebrew the first clause is two short words, *rav lakh*, literally “much for you,” or “enough for you.” It is an exclamation, and the lexicon hears a note of excess in it: you have gone far enough.[@bdb] The same two words, in the plural, are what Korah's rebels threw at Moses in the wilderness, “Ye take too much upon you” ([[Num. 16:3]]). So there is an edge to the word. The Lord is telling Moses to stop asking.

But that is not all the word carries, and the best guide to what else it carries is Deuteronomy itself. The book has used the phrase twice before, and both times in a particular way. At Horeb the Lord said, “Ye have dwelt long enough in this mount,” and in the next verse, “Turn you, and take your journey” ([[Deut. 1:6–7]]). At Seir He said, “Ye have compassed this mountain long enough,” and then, “turn you northward” ([[Deut. 2:3]]). In both places “enough” ends one stage of Israel's journey and begins the next in the same breath. It does not close the story. It moves the story on. Keil, reading 3:26, hears exactly this: the phrase means what it meant in chapter 1.[@keil-deuteronomy]

Read in the company of those two verses, “Let it suffice thee” sounds less like a door shutting than like the word that had twice already moved Israel forward. And in chapter 3, too, the next thing follows at once. The sentence after “speak no more” begins, “Get thee up into the top of Pisgah,” and the one after that, “But charge Joshua, and encourage him, and strengthen him” ([[Deut. 3:27–28]]). I cannot say what the Lord meant by the word. I can say that inside this book “enough” has never yet meant only “stop.” One Latter-day Saint reader hears kindness in it: the Lord “kindly acknowledged Moses's yearning.”[@ot-minute-deut-3] (The note “Enough for you” on [chapter 3](/deuteronomy/3/) points to the same verses.)

## What he was given

So what did Moses actually receive in place of the thing he asked for? Three things, and none of them is a consolation prize.

The first is the land, seen whole. The command to “lift up thine eyes” and “behold it” ([[Deut. 3:27]]) is kept on the day he dies, when “the LORD shewed him all the land” from Gilead in the north to the southern desert ([[Deut. 34:1]]). No Israelite who crossed the river ever saw it that way.

The second is a successor. Moses is told to charge Joshua, because Joshua “shall go over before this people” ([[Deut. 3:28]]). Joshua's name means “Jehovah is salvation,”[@scripture-helps-num] and the Witness of Christ on [chapter 3](/deuteronomy/3/) follows that name where it leads.

The third is a life kept. On its face the text says that Moses “died there in the land of Moab,” and that “no man knoweth of his sepulchre unto this day” ([[Deut. 34:5–6]]). But the Joseph Smith Translation reads instead that “the Lord took him unto his Fathers,”[@scripture-helps-deut] and the Book of Mormon says plainly that “the Lord took Moses unto himself” ([[Alma 45:19]]). The Church teaches that Moses was translated, and the reason is the Mount of Transfiguration. Because Moses appeared there before any resurrection had happened, he needed “a body of flesh and bones,” and so “Moses was a translated being and had not died as reported in Deut. 34.”[@bd-moses] Moses and Elijah were preserved from death “because they had a mission to perform, and it had to be performed before the crucifixion of the Son of God.”[@ot-manual-deuteronomy-17-33] As the Student Manual observes, being translated was “hardly a punishment.”[@ot-manual-numbers-13-36] (The notes “Died, or translated?” and “Who buried Moses” on [chapter 34](/deuteronomy/34/) give the detail. The keys of gathering that Moses later brought to the Kirtland Temple, [[D&C 110:11]], are one more piece of work on the far side of the same refusal.)

Put the three together and the refusal looks very different from a punishment. Moses was denied a few more years in Canaan. In their place he was given the whole land in one sight, a man to lead the people into it, and a body kept for an errand that no mortal could have run. The refusal was the shape his next assignment took.

## The mountain

That assignment brought him, centuries later, to a mountain in the Savior's company. Luke tells it this way. Jesus “went up into a mountain to pray,” and as He prayed “the fashion of his countenance was altered,” and “there talked with him two men, which were Moses and Elias: Who appeared in glory, and spake of his decease which he should accomplish at Jerusalem” ([[Luke 9:28–31]]).

Two details in that account matter here. The first is what they talked about. The word translated “decease” is the Greek *exodos*, a departure, the close of a life,[@thayer] and the Joseph Smith Translation makes it explicit: they spoke “of his death, and also his resurrection.”[@nt-student-manual-6] Moses, of all people, came to speak with Jesus about an exodus.

The second detail is what the conversation did. The New Testament Student Manual, quoting Elder McConkie, who in turn cites Talmage, puts it carefully: “Apparently Jesus himself was strengthened and encouraged by Moses and Elijah so as to be prepared for the infinite sufferings and agony ahead of him.”[@nt-student-manual-6]

Now consider who is standing there. The man whose one prayer for himself was refused “for your sakes” has been kept alive, through all those centuries, to stand with the Son of God and speak of the Son's death. And if Talmage is right, he is there to strengthen Jesus for an ordeal in which Jesus' own prayer for Himself will be refused, so that He can suffer “for all” ([[D&C 19:16]]). The same thing is about to happen to the Son that happened to the servant, and the servant is on the mountain when it begins.

## The garden

In Gethsemane Jesus “fell on his face, and prayed, saying, O my Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me: nevertheless not as I will, but as thou wilt” ([[Matt. 26:39]]). Mark's version is blunter still: “Abba, Father, all things are possible unto thee; take away this cup from me” ([[Mark 14:36]]). He prayed it a second time, and then “the third time, saying the same words” ([[Matt. 26:44]]). Luke adds that “there appeared an angel unto him from heaven, strengthening him” ([[Luke 22:43]]). And in a revelation to Joseph Smith the Savior gave His own account of that hour: He “would that I might not drink the bitter cup, and shrink—Nevertheless, glory be to the Father, and I partook” ([[D&C 19:18–19]]).

The Father's answer was no. Elder Oaks has said this as plainly as it can be said: “The Father's answer was to deny the plea of his Only Begotten Son. The Atonement had to be worked out by that lamb without blemish. But though the Son's request was denied, his prayer was answered.”[@oaks-1994-faith] Elder Holland says the same in fewer words. Jesus pled that the cup might pass, “But, of course, it could not pass.”[@holland-none-with-him] What He received instead was an angel, and the strength to wait upon His Father and say, “Thy will be done.”[@hales-waiting]

The Epistle to the Hebrews describes these prayers,[@lds-scriptures] and it describes them with a word that should stop us. Jesus “offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears unto him that was able to save him from death, and was heard in that he feared” ([[Heb. 5:7]]). Was heard. The Greek verb, in this form, means to have one's request granted, and the lexicon applies that sense to this very verse.[@thayer] Yet the cup did not pass, and Hebrews never says what was granted.

Hold that beside Deuteronomy. Of Moses, the Lord “would not hear me.” Of the Son, He “was heard.” Moses did not cross the river, and the cup did not pass. One prayer is described as unheard and the other as heard, and in neither case was the thing asked for given. Scripture, it turns out, does not use “heard” to mean “granted,” and the apostles have said so: a prayer can be heard and answered without its request being given,[@oaks-1994-faith] “even if … not answered the way I asked.”[@holland-hidden-fire-2024]

There is one step further to take, and it is my own. Latter-day Saints understand that “Jehovah is the premortal Jesus Christ,”[@bd-jehovah] and that when “Lord” appears in the Old Testament it usually means Jehovah.[@gs-jehovah] The risen Christ said as much Himself: “I am he that gave the law” ([[3 Ne. 15:5]]). If that is so, then the One who told Moses to “speak no more unto me of this matter” is the One who prayed three times, “saying the same words,” and ended with “thy will be done.” He asked of Moses nothing He would not Himself bear. No Church source draws that line between the two scenes, and I offer it as a reading rather than a doctrine. But once it is drawn, it is hard to unsee. (The cross, and the psalms of lament behind it, belong to another essay, Psalms' [The Savior Enters the Unanswered Prayer](/psalms/themes/the-unanswered-prayer/).)

## The disciple

Paul had a refused prayer of his own, and he told it in words that sound like Moses'. “For this thing I besought the Lord thrice, that it might depart from me,” he wrote of his thorn in the flesh. “And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness” ([[2 Cor. 12:8–9]]). Keil, commenting on Deuteronomy, calls “Let it suffice thee” substantially the same answer.[@keil-deuteronomy] The parallel holds at every point. A faithful servant asks three times; the thing is refused, for “the Lord declined to heal him”;[@oaks-2006-heals] and something else is given in its place, in Paul's case strength enough to say, “when I am weak, then am I strong” ([[2 Cor. 12:10]]).

This is where the arc reaches us. A righteous request can be refused. That is not a hard saying invented here; it is what the apostles teach. “Sometimes our righteous desires are not granted in this life.”[@hales-2019-answers] “Some challenges in life will not be resolved here on earth.”[@nt-student-manual-42] And “the object of prayer is not to change the will of God.”[@bd-prayer]

But a refusal is not silence, and it is not a verdict on the one who asked. Elder Holland, speaking of the prayers offered for his own health and for his wife's, testified that both “were heard and answered by a divinely compassionate Heavenly Father, even if the prayers for Pat were not answered the way I asked.”[@holland-hidden-fire-2024]

So a disciple who asks for a good thing, having lived so as to expect it, and is told “enough,” stands in a particular company. Moses stood there on the plains of Moab. Paul stood there with his thorn. The Savior knelt there in the garden. Each of them was heard. None of them received what he asked. And each was given something else, which in Moses' case turned out to be the work of the rest of his life. “Sometimes we are ‘healed’ by being given strength or understanding or patience to bear the burdens placed upon us,”[@oaks-2006-heals] and whichever it is, “the Redeemer will support you.”[@nt-student-manual-42] The Lord said the rest to a prisoner in Liberty Jail:[@lds-scriptures] “all these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good” ([[D&C 122:7]]).
