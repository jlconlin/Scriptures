Three times in his speeches Moses tells Israel that the LORD was angry with him “for your sakes” and would not let him cross the Jordan ([[Deut. 1:37]]; [[Deut. 3:26]]; [[Deut. 4:21]]). It is easy to read those verses as a footnote to Numbers 20, the price of striking the rock. This page reads them as Deuteronomy's teaching on what the Lord does with a righteous request He will not grant. The book sets the refusal among prayers the LORD heard, binds it to the people Moses served, answers it with a word that twice before had meant “enough of this; now the next thing,” and follows it at once with a sight, a charge, and a life kept for a work beyond the river. That work brought Moses to a mountain where he spoke with Jesus of His death, before the Son's own plea in Gethsemane was refused. Read together, Pisgah and Gethsemane show that a righteous prayer refused is one of the ways God answers, not a sign that He has not heard; and the One who said “enough” to Moses later knelt under the same answer.

<h2 id="heard">“The LORD hearkened unto me”</h2>

After the golden calf Moses “fell down before the LORD, as at the first, forty days and forty nights” ([[Deut. 9:18]]) and prayed, “O Lord GOD, destroy not thy people and thine inheritance, which thou hast redeemed through thy greatness, which thou hast brought forth out of Egypt with a mighty hand” ([[Deut. 9:26]]). He reports the outcome twice in the same words: “the LORD hearkened unto me at that time also” ([[Deut. 9:19]]; [[Deut. 10:10]]). Exodus tells the scene: “the LORD repented of the evil which he thought to do unto his people” ([[Ex. 32:14]]); and Numbers tells another prayer of his that was heard, at Kadesh: “I have pardoned according to thy word” ([[Num. 14:20]]). Later scripture remembers Moses as the man who “stood before him in the breach, to turn away his wrath” ([[Ps. 106:23]]), one of those who “called upon the LORD, and he answered them” ([[Ps. 99:6]]). The office itself is traced in Exodus's [Moses the Mediator](/exodus/themes/moses-the-mediator/#offer).

What matters here is the verb. “Hearkened” in 9:19 and 10:10 and “would not hear me” in 3:26 are one Hebrew verb, *shamaʿ*, in one construction, “hear unto me” (*shamaʿ ʾelay*): twice affirmed, once negated.[@oshb-wlc] With a person as its object, the construction means to “hear and answer.”[@bdb]

Deuteronomy tells the refusal (chapter 3) before the intercessions (chapters 9–10), though in time the intercessions at Horeb came first. Either way, a reader of the whole book cannot take 3:26 as the prayer of a man God does not listen to. The same book, with the same verb, says twice that the LORD heard him. Whatever the refusal is, it is not deafness.

<h2 id="asked">“Let me go over, and see”</h2>

Then Moses asks for himself:

> And I besought the LORD at that time, saying, O Lord GOD, thou hast begun to shew thy servant thy greatness, and thy mighty hand: for what God is there in heaven or in earth, that can do according to thy works, and according to thy might? I pray thee, let me go over, and see the good land that is beyond Jordan, that goodly mountain, and Lebanon. ([[Deut. 3:23–25]])

“Besought” is a form of *chanan*, “to be gracious,” meaning “seek or implore favour,” here addressed to God.[@bdb] Moses asks for favor, and he argues from what he has seen: the LORD has “begun to shew” His greatness, so let His servant see it finished (the note “What Moses had seen begun” on [chapter 3](/deuteronomy/3/) gives the reading). The request has two verbs: go over, and see.

The answer opens, “But the LORD was wroth with me” ([[Deut. 3:26]]). The Hebrew for “was wroth,” *vayitʿabber*, shares the root letters ʿ-b-r as “let me go over” (*ʾeʿebrah*) in verse 25 and “thou shalt not go over” (*taʿavor*) in verse 27.[@oshb-wlc] On one older reading the verb means literally “to exceed bounds”;[@cambridge-deuteronomy] it is also filed as a verb of its own, “infuriate oneself,” with no other instance in the five books of Moses: its other uses are in Psalms and Proverbs.[@bdb]

The page takes the shared root as its own observation, not as a pun the text intends: a reader of the Hebrew meets ʿ-b-r on both sides of the refusal, in the request, in the anger, and in the denial. And the denial divides Moses' two verbs. “Go over” is refused, here and again at the end ([[Deut. 34:4]]). “See” is granted, in the same Hebrew verb as the request: “behold it with thine eyes” ([[Deut. 3:27]]) is the *raʾah* of “let me… see” ([[Deut. 3:25]]),[@oshb-wlc] and the last word to Moses is “I have caused thee to see it with thine eyes, but thou shalt not go over thither” ([[Deut. 34:4]]). The refusal is not a flat no. Half of what Moses asked is given, named in the words he asked it with.

<h2 id="sakes">“For your sakes”</h2>

Why was the other half refused? Scripture gives more than one account and does not reconcile them.

Moses' own speeches say “for your sakes” all three times ([[Deut. 1:37]]; [[Deut. 3:26]]; [[Deut. 4:21]]), though the Hebrew phrase differs each time.[@oshb-wlc] Numbers, and the LORD's own words in Deuteronomy 32, point to Moses' own act at Meribah: “Because ye believed me not, to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel” ([[Num. 20:12]]); “ye rebelled against my commandment in the desert of Zin, in the strife of the congregation” ([[Num. 27:14]]); “ye sanctified me not in the midst of the children of Israel” ([[Deut. 32:51]]). Instead of speaking to the rock Moses struck it twice, and he asked the people, “Must we fetch you water out of this rock?”[@scripture-helps-num] A psalm holds both: “it went ill with Moses for their sakes: Because they provoked his spirit, so that he spake unadvisedly with his lips” ([[Ps. 106:32–33]]). Latter-day revelation adds a third account, of why Moses was taken from Israel at all: the people “hardened their hearts and could not endure” the Lord's presence; “Therefore, he took Moses out of their midst, and the Holy Priesthood also” ([[D&C 84:24–25]]).

The reasons remain uncertain. “The scriptures do not fully explain the reasons for this consequence.”[@scripture-helps-num] Two passages “indicate that Moses did sin,” while “for your sakes” “could imply that there were reasons other than the error of Moses.”[@ot-manual-numbers-13-36] A further proposal, offered with a “perhaps,” finds the cause in Deuteronomy's own context, the sending of the spies ([[Deut. 1:22–23]]).[@ot-minute-deut-3]

This page does not choose among them. What it draws from them is what they share: every account binds the refusal to the people. “For your sakes” says so outright. The sentence from Meribah, which lays the fault on Moses, still frames it as a failure “in the eyes of the children of Israel” and “in the strife of the congregation.” The revelation's account is about what the people could not endure. Whatever Moses did, he did as their mediator, in their quarrel, and he is kept out with them in view. The mediator's lot was tied to the people's. (The note “Why Moses could not cross” on [chapter 3](/deuteronomy/3/) sets out the passages in detail.)

<h2 id="enough">“Let it suffice thee”</h2>

Then comes the word that closes the request: “Let it suffice thee; speak no more unto me of this matter” ([[Deut. 3:26]]). “Let it suffice thee” is *rav lakh*, “enough for you,” an exclamation with an “implication of excess,” like Korah's “ye assume too much”[@bdb] (“Ye take too much upon you,” [[Num. 16:3]]). The edge is real. Yet it can also be heard as kindness: the Lord “kindly acknowledged Moses's yearning.”[@ot-minute-deut-3]

Deuteronomy has used the phrase twice already, in the plural, to all Israel, where it means “you have done it enough”:[@bdb] “Ye have dwelt long enough in this mount: Turn you, and take your journey” ([[Deut. 1:6–7]]); “Ye have compassed this mountain long enough: turn you northward” ([[Deut. 2:3]]). Both times the word ends one thing and the next words begin another. One older reading hears 3:26 the same way, “as in Deuteronomy 1:6.”[@keil-deuteronomy] (The note “Enough for you” on [chapter 3](/deuteronomy/3/) points to the same verses.)

The page reads 3:26 the way the book has taught its reader to hear the word, and here too the next thing comes in the very next breath: “Get thee up into the top of Pisgah… But charge Joshua, and encourage him, and strengthen him” ([[Deut. 3:27–28]]). The page does not claim to know what the Lord meant by the word. It says that within this book, “enough” has never yet meant only “stop.”

<h2 id="given">“I have caused thee to see it”</h2>

What Moses received was three things. First, the land seen whole: “lift up thine eyes westward, and northward, and southward, and eastward, and behold it with thine eyes” ([[Deut. 3:27]]), fulfilled when “the LORD shewed him all the land of Gilead, unto Dan” ([[Deut. 34:1]]). Second, a successor charged: Joshua “shall go over before this people” ([[Deut. 3:28]]), a man whose name means “Jehovah is salvation”[@scripture-helps-num] (the Witness of Christ on [chapter 3](/deuteronomy/3/) follows the name).

Third, a life kept. The text says “Moses the servant of the LORD died there in the land of Moab” and that “no man knoweth of his sepulchre unto this day” ([[Deut. 34:5–6]]). The Joseph Smith Translation reads instead that “the Lord took him unto his Fathers,”[@scripture-helps-deut] and the Book of Mormon, that “the Lord took Moses unto himself” ([[Alma 45:19]]). Because Moses came to the Mount of Transfiguration before the Resurrection, “Moses was a translated being and had not died as reported in Deut. 34”; he needed “a body of flesh and bones at the time of the Transfiguration.”[@bd-moses] Moses and Elijah were preserved from death “because they had a mission to perform, and it had to be performed before the crucifixion of the Son of God.”[@ot-manual-deuteronomy-17-33] Moses' translation was “hardly a punishment.”[@ot-manual-numbers-13-36] (The notes “Died, or translated?” and “Who buried Moses” on [chapter 34](/deuteronomy/34/) give the detail; the keys Moses later brought to Kirtland, [[D&C 110:11]], are another work on the far side of the same refusal.)

The page concludes from this that what Moses was given in place of Canaan was neither nothing nor a consolation prize. The refusal was the shape his next assignment took.

<h2 id="mount">“Spake of his decease”</h2>

That assignment brought him to a mountain. Jesus “went up into a mountain to pray. And as he prayed, the fashion of his countenance was altered… And, behold, there talked with him two men, which were Moses and Elias: Who appeared in glory, and spake of his decease which he should accomplish at Jerusalem” ([[Luke 9:28–31]]). The Greek for “decease” is *exodos*, “departure,” “the close of one's career”;[@thayer] the Joseph Smith Translation has them speak “of his death, and also his resurrection.”[@nt-student-manual-6] “Apparently Jesus himself was strengthened and encouraged by Moses and Elijah so as to be prepared for the infinite sufferings and agony ahead of him.”[@nt-student-manual-6]

Read together, Pisgah and this mountain show the same thing happening to the servant and to the Son. The man whose own plea was refused “for your sakes” stood with the Savior to speak of His death and perhaps to strengthen Him for an agony in which the Savior's own plea would be refused, so that He could suffer “for all” ([[D&C 19:16]]). The man refused Canaan was the man the Lord preserved, and he stood on the mount in the Savior's company.

<h2 id="cup">“Not as I will”</h2>

In Gethsemane Jesus “fell on his face, and prayed, saying, O my Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me: nevertheless not as I will, but as thou wilt” ([[Matt. 26:39]]). In Mark the plea reads, “Abba, Father, all things are possible unto thee; take away this cup from me” ([[Mark 14:36]]). He prayed a second time, “if this cup may not pass away from me, except I drink it, thy will be done” ([[Matt. 26:42]]), and “the third time, saying the same words” ([[Matt. 26:44]]). Luke adds, “there appeared an angel unto him from heaven, strengthening him” ([[Luke 22:43]]). His own account, in latter-day revelation: “would that I might not drink the bitter cup, and shrink—Nevertheless, glory be to the Father, and I partook” ([[D&C 19:18–19]]).

The answer was a refusal. “The Father's answer was to deny the plea of his Only Begotten Son. The Atonement had to be worked out by that lamb without blemish. But though the Son's request was denied, his prayer was answered.”[@oaks-1994-faith] He pled that the cup pass, “But, of course, it could not pass.”[@holland-none-with-him] “He waited upon His Father, saying, ‘Thy will be done.’”[@hales-waiting] And “because of His love for His Father and for us, He endured.”[@christofferson-abide-2016]

Hebrews speaks of these prayers:[@lds-scriptures] He “offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears unto him that was able to save him from death, and was heard in that he feared” ([[Heb. 5:7]]). The Greek verb, in the passive, means “to be heard, to have one's request granted,” and that sense is given for this verse.[@thayer] Yet the cup did not pass, and Hebrews does not name what was granted.

The page sets two King James verses against each other here. Of Moses the LORD “would not hear me” ([[Deut. 3:26]]); of the Son, He “was heard” ([[Heb. 5:7]]). Moses did not cross, and the cup did not pass; one prayer is told as unheard and the other as heard. That a prayer can be heard and answered without the thing asked being given is said plainly: “though the Son's request was denied, his prayer was answered,”[@oaks-1994-faith] and prayers can be “heard and answered by a divinely compassionate Heavenly Father,” “even if … not answered the way I asked.”[@holland-hidden-fire-2024]

One step more is the page's own. In Latter-day Saint teaching, “Jehovah is the premortal Jesus Christ,”[@bd-jehovah] and “usually, when the word Lord appears in the Old Testament, it means ‘Jehovah’”;[@gs-jehovah] the risen Christ said, “I am he that gave the law” ([[3 Ne. 15:5]]). So the One who told Moses, “speak no more unto me of this matter,” is the One who prayed three times “saying the same words” and ended, “thy will be done.” He asked of Moses nothing He would not Himself bear. (The cross and the psalms of lament are the subject of Psalms' [The Savior Enters the Unanswered Prayer](/psalms/themes/the-unanswered-prayer/).)

<h2 id="sufficient">“My grace is sufficient for thee”</h2>

Paul told of his own refused prayer: “For this thing I besought the Lord thrice, that it might depart from me. And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness” ([[2 Cor. 12:8–9]]). One older reading calls “Let it suffice thee” “substantially equivalent to” this answer.[@keil-deuteronomy] Paul's request, like Moses', was refused: “the Lord declined to heal him.”[@oaks-2006-heals] And as Moses was given sight and a charge, Paul was given strength,[@nt-student-manual-42] enough to say, “when I am weak, then am I strong” ([[2 Cor. 12:10]]).

A disciple's prayers meet the same answer. A righteous request can be refused. “Sometimes our righteous desires are not granted in this life.”[@hales-2019-answers] “Some challenges in life will not be resolved here on earth.”[@nt-student-manual-42] The refusal is not silence. Of the prayers he offered for his own health and his wife's, an apostle testified that both “were heard and answered by a divinely compassionate Heavenly Father, even if the prayers for Pat were not answered the way I asked.”[@holland-hidden-fire-2024] And “the object of prayer is not to change the will of God.”[@bd-prayer]

So a disciple who asks for a good thing and is told “enough,” stands where Moses stood on the plains of Moab, where Paul stood with his thorn, and where the Savior knelt in the garden. “Sometimes we are ‘healed’ by being given strength or understanding or patience to bear the burdens placed upon us,”[@oaks-2006-heals] and “in either case the Redeemer will support you.”[@nt-student-manual-42] The Lord's word to a prisoner in Liberty Jail says the rest: “all these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good” ([[D&C 122:7]]).
