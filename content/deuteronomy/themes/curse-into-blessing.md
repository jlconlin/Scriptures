Moses sets a blessing and a curse before Israel in Deuteronomy: “Behold, I set before you this day a blessing and a curse” ([[Deut. 11:26]]). The noun for curse is *qĕlālâ*, and it stands in exactly eleven verses of the book.[@oshb-wlc] Nine of them belong to the covenant drama. The curse is set before Israel and given a mountain, Ebal ([[Deut. 11:26–29]]); six tribes stand on that mountain “to curse” ([[Deut. 27:13]]); “all these curses shall come upon thee, and overtake thee” ([[Deut. 28:15]]), “till thou be destroyed” ([[Deut. 28:45]]); “all the curses that are written in this book” are the reason the land will be laid waste ([[Deut. 29:27]]); and twice at the end Moses names both nouns together, “when all these things are come upon thee, the blessing and the curse” ([[Deut. 30:1]]), and “I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing: therefore choose life” ([[Deut. 30:19]]). After that verse the word does not occur again. No word for cursing of any kind stands in chapters 31 to 34, and Moses’ farewell is introduced as “the blessing” ([[Deut. 33:1]]).[@oshb-wlc]

The other two verses are in the laws, where the covenant drama is not in view, and they are the ones this page is about. A man executed and hanged on a tree must be buried the same day, “for he that is hanged is accursed of God” ([[Deut. 21:23]]), in Hebrew “the curse of God.”[@oshb-wlc] And Moab, which hired Balaam “to curse thee,” is barred from the congregation: “Nevertheless the LORD thy God would not hearken unto Balaam; but the LORD thy God turned the curse into a blessing unto thee, because the LORD thy God loved thee” ([[Deut. 23:4–5]]). My reading is that these two verses, set beside Paul, hold the two parts of how a curse ends. In the nine covenant verses the curse is set before Israel, chosen, and arrives, and it is lifted only after it has fallen and Israel has returned. In the two legal verses a curse rests on one man, whose body is buried that day so that the land is not defiled, and a curse is turned into a blessing by the Lord for love. Paul reads the first of Christ, and the Church’s footnotes at the verse follow him. The second rests, as Dana Pike shows, on the promise to Abraham that Paul builds his whole argument on. Setting the two legal verses beside each other, and beside Paul, is mine; what each verse means is the commentators’ and the Church’s.

## The curse chosen

The nine covenant verses are the book’s frame, and the chapter notes carry them. The site’s note on [Deuteronomy 11:29](/deuteronomy/11/) explains the ceremony on Ebal and Gerizim. At Ebal twelve sentences are pronounced, each beginning “Cursed be he,” and the people answer each with “Amen,” a word found in Deuteronomy only in those twelve verses ([[Deut. 27:15–26]]).[@oshb-wlc] Chapter 27 gives no sentence of blessing to answer; chapter 28 has its own six “Blessed” beside six “Cursed” ([[Deut. 28:3–6]]; [[Deut. 28:16–19]]). Keil adopts the Berleburger Bible’s explanation of why only the curses were said at Ebal: it was “to awaken a desire for the Messiah, who was to take away the curse and bring the true blessing instead,” and adds that however remote the allusion, the law “primarily and chiefly brings a curse upon man because of the sinfulness of his nature,” as Moses himself says in 31:16–17.[@keil-deuteronomy] Of the twelfth curse, which falls on whoever “confirmeth not all the words of this law to do them,” he quotes Gerlach: “Every conscious act of transgression subjects the sinner to the curse of God, from which none but He who has become a curse for us can possibly deliver us.”[@keil-deuteronomy] That sentence is Galatians 3 read back into Deuteronomy 27, and it is Gerlach’s, as Keil quotes him, not mine. The site’s notes on [Deuteronomy 27:15](/deuteronomy/27/) and [Deuteronomy 28:15](/deuteronomy/28/) have the Amens and the length of the curses.

The curse is also the book’s way of telling the future. Moses does not say “if” in chapter 30 but “when”: “when all these things are come upon thee, the blessing and the curse” ([[Deut. 30:1]]). The Book of Mormon reads its own history by this framework, as a recent study shows; Mormon’s summaries of the Nephites’ fall are written “in wording that reflects the Deuteronomic framework of ‘life and death, blessing and cursing’ (Deut. 30:19).”[@uriona-curses] The curse, in the covenant verses, is something Israel can choose and will.

## The curse on one man

The first legal verse puts the noun on a single body. “And if a man have committed a sin worthy of death, and he be to be put to death, and thou hang him on a tree: His body shall not remain all night upon the tree, but thou shalt in any wise bury him that day; (for he that is hanged is accursed of God;) that thy land be not defiled” ([[Deut. 21:22–23]]). The Hebrew phrase, *qilĕlat ʾĕlōhîm*, “the curse of God,” has been read two ways since ancient times; Scripture Central says plainly that “the ambiguity has remained.”[@sc-knowhy-752] Keil and the Cambridge commentary take it as God’s curse on the man: Keil, “the hanged man is a curse of God,” who “defiled the land”; Cambridge, “lit. a curse of God,” and then two explanations, “God’s wrath was heaped upon it; or it became doubly unclean.”[@keil-deuteronomy][@cambridge-deuteronomy] The Church’s footnote reports the other reading, an affront to God: “According to Rabbinical commentaries, to leave a body hanging was a degradation of the human body and therefore an affront to God, in whose image man’s body was made.”[@lds-scriptures] Daniel Belnap, who has studied the phrase, sets out both readings and the ancient versions behind each, and concludes, “In any case the hanged person is understood as cursed.”[@belnap-fell-tree] The site’s note on [Deuteronomy 21:23](/deuteronomy/21/) weighs them.

Paul took the verse to the cross. “Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree” ([[Gal. 3:13]]). The Church’s footnotes at “tree” and “accursed” in Deuteronomy 21:23 both lead to that verse, and the Bible Dictionary lists the pair among the New Testament’s references to the book.[@lds-scriptures][@bd-deuteronomy] Paul does not quote the verse as it stands. The Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Old Testament, reads “cursed by God is every one that hangeth on a tree,” and Paul writes “cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree,” with the two words “by God” gone and a different word for “cursed.”[@swete-lxx-deut][@greek-nt] The Cambridge commentary on Galatians says the words were omitted “not as inconsistent with, but as unnecessary for his purpose”; and it says what the purpose was: “He, though sinless, bore, nay became the curse, that on us might come the blessing.”[@cambridge-galatians] The New Testament Student Manual tells the setting. Some Jews held that a crucified man could not be the Messiah, because Deuteronomy called the hanged man “accursed of God”; “Paul showed another way of looking at the concept of being ‘cursed’ as applied to the Savior. He explained that Jesus willingly took our sins upon Himself in order to perform the work of redemption, thus becoming ‘cursed’ in our place.”[@nt-student-manual-43] Robert Millet puts the objection in its sharpest form, that on the Jewish reading “God had cursed Himself,” and Jared Ludlow calls Paul’s move “an ironic argument that Christ became a ‘curse’ in order to redeem from ‘the curse of the law.’”[@millet-glorying-cross][@ludlow-paul-ot] Ellicott reads the curse on Christ more narrowly: “Being treated as if He were accursed,” and it “was not the same curse as that described above as the consequence of human guilt in failing to keep the requirements of the Law,” but “an incidental defilement” from the manner of His death.[@ellicott-galatians]

The commentators agree on what Paul quotes and differ on what the curse was. The quotation in 3:10 is Deuteronomy 27:26, “the conclusion of the series of curses to be pronounced from Mount Ebal,” as Ellicott says.[@ellicott-galatians] Cambridge then joins 3:10 to 3:13: “To rescue us from that terrible malediction, Christ submitted to an accursed death,” and “if in any true sense Christ became a curse for us, it was the curse of God.”[@cambridge-galatians] The Student Manual’s “cursed in our place” stands with Cambridge, and Gerlach, in Keil, had already read Ebal’s twelfth curse by Galatians 3:13. On that reading, which I follow, the man hanged on the tree and the people who said Amen on the mountain are joined in two verses of Galatians; Ellicott’s dissent stands beside it.

## The curse turned

The second legal verse shows a curse that never arrives. Balak hired Balaam to curse Israel, and the site’s note on [Deuteronomy 23:3](/deuteronomy/23/) tells the story from Numbers; what Deuteronomy adds is the reason it failed: “because the LORD thy God loved thee” ([[Deut. 23:5]]). Dana Pike notes that Balaam “could only pronounce blessings,” and that the ground of it was the promise to Abraham: “Based on the promise in Genesis 12:2–3, the Israelites’ God did not permit others to curse them collectively (although he obviously allowed them to bring curses upon themselves).”[@pike-balaam] Keil says the same from the other side: Moab, by hiring Balaam, “brought upon themselves the curse which falls upon all those who curse Israel, according to the infallible word of God (Genesis 12:3)… although out of love to Israel the Lord turned the curse of Balaam into a blessing.”[@keil-deuteronomy] Nehemiah repeats its words when he bars the same nations, “our God turned the curse into a blessing” ([[Neh. 13:2]]), and the Bible Dictionary gives the four places together under “the curse turned to blessing.”[@lds-scriptures][@bd-balaam]

Pike’s parenthesis is the honest limit of the verse. This is an enemy’s curse, not the law’s; Israel could not be cursed from outside, but it could bring the covenant curse on itself, and the nine verses say it would. The two kinds meet once more at the end of the book, when the covenant curses themselves are turned: “the LORD thy God will put all these curses upon thine enemies, and on them that hate thee, which persecuted thee” ([[Deut. 30:7]]). The noun there is a different one, the oath-curse of chapter 29, and the Greek translators used a different word too.[@oshb-wlc][@swete-lxx-deut] But Keil grounds this turning where Pike grounded the first: after Israel’s conversion the curses “would fall upon its enemies and haters, according to the promise in Genesis 12:3.”[@keil-deuteronomy] Both times a curse is turned in Deuteronomy, Pike for the first and Keil for the second, it is turned on the strength of the Lord’s word to Abraham, “I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee: and in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed” ([[Gen. 12:3]]).

That is the promise Paul builds on. Five verses before he quotes Deuteronomy 21:23, he quotes the promise to Abraham, in words that the Cambridge commentary says combine Genesis 12:3 with 18:18: “the scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the heathen through faith, preached before the gospel unto Abraham, saying, In thee shall all nations be blessed” ([[Gal. 3:8]]).[@cambridge-galatians] The argument then runs from the curse of the law ([[Gal. 3:10]]) to Christ made a curse ([[Gal. 3:13]]) to its end: “That the blessing of Abraham might come on the Gentiles through Jesus Christ” ([[Gal. 3:14]]). Cambridge: “The curse has been borne, and the Law is silent. The curse has been removed, and the blessing remains; descending in all its fulness on the Gentiles, as well as the Jews, through faith.”[@cambridge-galatians] The Student Manual says what the blessing of Abraham is for a Latter-day Saint: “Because of the Atonement of Jesus Christ, all who accept His gospel are entitled to the blessings of Abraham.”[@nt-student-manual-43] Deuteronomy’s two turnings and Paul’s one all stand on the promise to Abraham. That observation is mine; Pike and Keil supply the Deuteronomy half and Paul the other.

## For love

The two legal verses concern different kinds of curse, and the page does not blend them. The curse on the hanged man is the law’s judgment on a sin “worthy of death”; the curse Balaam was hired to speak was a foreigner’s, and the Lord would not let it land. No commentator I have read sets 23:5 beside 21:23, or beside Galatians 3. What the two verses share is the book’s noun, and one more thing, which is the reason this page sets them together. In the covenant verses the curse is lifted only after it has fallen: Israel returns, the Lord turns its captivity and circumcises its heart, and the curses pass to its enemies ([[Deut. 30:1–7]]). In the two legal verses the book shows a curse resting on one man, and a curse ending in blessing because God loved. Paul joins a curse borne by one Man to the blessing that follows it ([[Gal. 3:13–14]]), and John gives the reason in Deuteronomy’s word, “For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son” ([[John 3:16]]). The Church’s footnote at “loved” in Deuteronomy 23:5 and its footnote at John 3:16 carry the same Topical Guide heading, “God, Love of.”[@lds-scriptures] Deuteronomy 23:5 does not speak of the cross, and I do not say it does. It says what Deuteronomy knows about how a curse spoken against Israel becomes a blessing: the Lord does it, and He does it for love.

The Guide to the Scriptures defines a curse as “the application of divine law that allows or brings judgments and their consequences upon a thing, person, or people primarily because of unrighteousness,” and adds that “The Lord may remove curses because of the individual’s or people’s faith in Jesus Christ and obedience to the laws and ordinances of the gospel.”[@gs-curse] That is the covenant curse and its removal in two sentences, and the second sentence names the One Paul named. The Church’s footnote at “blessing” in 23:5 leads to a promise with its own condition: “all things shall work together for your good, if ye walk uprightly and remember the covenant” ([[D&C 90:24]]).[@lds-scriptures] The turning is the Lord’s; the walking is ours. The noun for the curse last stands at 30:19, and the heading over Moses’ last words calls them “the blessing.”
