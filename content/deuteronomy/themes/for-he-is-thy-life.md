Deuteronomy’s first word about the heart is a wish, and the Lord is the one wishing. At Horeb the people had asked Moses to stand between them and the voice, and the Lord said to him, “They have well said all that they have spoken. O that there were such an heart in them, that they would fear me, and keep all my commandments always, that it might be well with them” ([[Deut. 5:28–29]]). The Hebrew behind “O that” is *mî yittēn*, literally “who will give,” which the King James renders as a wish here and as “Would God” at 28:67, the only other passage in Deuteronomy where it occurs, twice, spoken by those under the curse.[@oshb-wlc] Ellicott translates the idiom and then answers it: “Who will give that there shall be this heart in them… He who asked the question has also supplied the answer: ‘I will put my laws in their hearts.’”[@ellicott-deuteronomy] That answer is Hebrews 8:10, quoting Jeremiah’s new covenant, and the whole of this page is the road from the wish to the answer inside Deuteronomy itself.

I read four steps in the book’s account of the heart: wished for, commanded, declared not given, and promised as the Lord’s own gift. The gift’s stated purpose is life, and Moses’ last speech ends by naming whose that life is. The four steps as one line are my reading. Keil comes nearest, on Jeremiah: the law’s commands to circumcise the heart and love God “are impossibilities, unless the law be received into the heart,” and what the old covenant commanded “is given in the new”; the Cambridge commentary sets 30:6 “in contrast” to 29:4, and Ellicott hears the New Covenant in chapter 29 and finds its second half in 30:6.[@keil-jeremiah][@cambridge-deuteronomy][@ellicott-deuteronomy] The site’s Ezekiel page [A New Heart and a New Spirit](/ezekiel/themes/new-heart-new-spirit/) follows that book’s own form of the gift, the heart replaced; this page centers on Deuteronomy, which alone has the Lord’s wish for the heart, the sentence that He had not given it, the noun “for thy life,” and the naming of that life beside “cleave.”

## Commanded

The heart is first asked of Israel. “Thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart” ([[Deut. 6:5]]) is, as Kerry Muhlestein says, “the central and greatest commandment,” and Moses returns to it four times in chapter 30.[@muhlestein-love-neighbor] Then comes the harder command: “Circumcise therefore the foreskin of your heart, and be no more stiffnecked” ([[Deut. 10:16]]). The verb “circumcise” occurs in Deuteronomy twice, here and at 30:6, and here the verb is a plural command addressed to Israel.[@oshb-wlc] The Bible Dictionary lists the verse among those where circumcision means dedication from sin, and the Old Testament Student Manual says that “the Lord speaks of true circumcision as being the circumcision of the heart,” citing both Deuteronomy verses with Jeremiah 4:4 and Paul.[@bd-circumcision][@ot-manual-genesis-12-17] Ryan Wessel reads the command as an invitation “to internalize their commitment to God.”[@wessel-circumcision] The site’s note on [Deuteronomy 10:16](/deuteronomy/10/) explains the figure.

## Not given

Forty years after Horeb, on the plains of Moab, Moses says what has come of the wish. “Yet the LORD hath not given you an heart to perceive, and eyes to see, and ears to hear, unto this day” ([[Deut. 29:4]]). The verb is “give,” the verb hidden in “O that” at 5:29.[@oshb-wlc] Keil takes the heart as understanding and says Moses is not excusing the people but explaining why he must keep reminding them. The Lord had not given it, Keil says, “because the people had not yet asked for it, simply because the need of it was not felt.” The Cambridge commentary says the inward power to recognize the Lord’s deeds “is also, equally with them, of the gift of God,” and that the awkward sentence holds together “the grace of God and the responsibility of man.”[@keil-deuteronomy][@cambridge-deuteronomy] The site’s note on [Deuteronomy 29:4](/deuteronomy/29/) and the essay [Speak Not Thou in Thine Heart](/deuteronomy/themes/speak-not-in-thine-heart/) have the verse in its place. What this page takes from it is the verb. The Lord wished a heart for Israel at Horeb; at Moab the heart has not been given, and the sentence that says so names Him as the one who gives.

## Given, for thy life

Then Moses promises it. “And the LORD thy God will circumcise thine heart, and the heart of thy seed, to love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, that thou mayest live” ([[Deut. 30:6]]). The verb is the verb of 10:16, now with the Lord as its subject; the love is the love of 6:5, now the purpose of His act.[@oshb-wlc] The promise follows Israel’s return, “And shalt return unto the LORD thy God, and shalt obey his voice” ([[Deut. 30:2]]), and Keil keeps that order: when Israel turns to the Lord He will “take away the stony heart out of their flesh, and give them a heart of flesh, a new heart and a new spirit,” and he adds that the fulfilment began at the return from Babylon and “at the appearance of Christ.”[@keil-deuteronomy] *Scripture Helps* reads the figure as “a change in their nature.”[@scripture-helps-deut] The site’s note on [Deuteronomy 30:6](/deuteronomy/30/) treats the verse in full.

One word in the verse is hidden by the English. “That thou mayest live” elsewhere in Deuteronomy renders a verb, “that ye may live” ([[Deut. 4:1]]; [[Deut. 30:16]]; [[Deut. 30:19]]); here it renders a noun, “for thy life,” *lĕmaʿan ḥayyeykā*, and the Cambridge commentary notes it, “lit. for the sake of thy life.”[@oshb-wlc][@cambridge-deuteronomy] Every translation I have read gives the phrase as a verb, so the point is small and I do not lean on it hard. But the gift of the heart is, in the Hebrew, for the sake of the person’s life, and fourteen verses later the speech ends on that noun.

Jeremiah heard the promise renewed in Deuteronomy’s own words. “And I will give them an heart to know me, that I am the LORD” ([[Jer. 24:7]]). The three Hebrew words are the three of 29:4, “given… an heart to know,” there negated and here promised in the first person.[@oshb-wlc] And in the new covenant chapter, “I will give them one heart, and one way, that they may fear me for ever, for the good of them, and of their children after them” ([[Jer. 32:39]]). Keil derives that sentence from Deuteronomy himself, from 4:10, “that they may learn to fear me all the days,” and 6:24, “for our good,” both at Horeb; what I add is that its first words, “I will give them… heart,” are the Horeb wish of 5:29 answered, a heart to fear Him, always, for them and their children.[@keil-jeremiah][@oshb-wlc] Keil, on Jeremiah 31:33, sets Moses’ promise in 30:6 beside the commands of 6:5 and 10:12, 16, and says what the two covenants differ in: in the old the law was laid before the people to receive, and “what was commanded and applied to the heart in the old is given in the new.”[@keil-jeremiah] No commentator I have read sets Jeremiah 24:7 beside Deuteronomy 29:4; the match of the three words is mine. My reading is that Jeremiah heard a promise of the heart Deuteronomy said had not been given.

## He is thy life

The speech ends: “that thou mayest love the LORD thy God, and that thou mayest obey his voice, and that thou mayest cleave unto him: for he is thy life, and the length of thy days” ([[Deut. 30:20]]). The Hebrew pronoun is *hûʾ*, which the lexicon gives as “he, she,” and “used also… for the neuter it,” and the same clause, with “your” for “thy,” stands at 32:47 of the word of the law: “it is not a vain thing for you; because it is your life.”[@bdb][@oshb-wlc] The ancient Greek translation reads “this is thy life”; Keil, “for that (namely, to love the Lord) is thy life”; the Cambridge commentary, “for that is thy life,” a variant of 4:1; and the NRSVUE, “for that means life to you.”[@swete-lxx-deut][@keil-deuteronomy][@cambridge-deuteronomy][@nrsvue] On the other side stand the King James, the ESV, “for he is your life,” the NIV, which makes it explicit, “For the LORD is your life,” and Ellicott, who calls the clause “the Old Testament form” of “I am the resurrection and the life.”[@esv][@niv][@ellicott-deuteronomy] Elaine Jack, speaking to young adults, quoted the verse and said “the Lord is indeed your ‘life, and the length of thy days.’”[@jack-get-a-life]

I follow the King James here, and I say why. The nearest person the sentence names is “him,” the One Israel is to cleave to, and the sentence has just named Him three times. The Church’s footnote at “life” leads to “in him we live, and move, and have our being” ([[Acts 17:28]]) and to the light “which giveth life to all things” ([[D&C 88:13]]), which is to read the verse of a person.[@lds-scriptures] And the book has already said it once in a story. Moses proved “that ye may live” from Baal-peor: those who went after the idol are dead, and “ye that did cleave unto the LORD your God are alive every one of you this day” ([[Deut. 4:4]]). Cleaving to the Lord and being alive stand there as act and outcome; at 30:20 they stand as act and reason. The two verses use different forms of “cleave” and of “life,” an adjective in chapter 4 and a noun here, so the join is by sense and not by form, and it is mine.[@oshb-wlc] But a reader who takes “that” at 30:20 loses nothing of the page’s argument. On either reading, the heart the Lord gives is given “for thy life,” and the life is bound to Him.

## The heart He gives, the life He is

The LORD of these chapters is Jehovah, “the premortal Jesus Christ.”[@bd-jehovah] The Church’s edition joins the command of 10:16 to Benjamin’s people: its footnote at “heart” leads to their cry, “apply the atoning blood of Christ that we may receive forgiveness of our sins, and our hearts may be purified” ([[Mosiah 4:2]]), and its gloss on “circumcise” is “Purify your hearts.”[@lds-scriptures] I read those links as joining the circumcised heart to purification through the Atonement. What follows in Benjamin’s sermon is, in my reading, Deuteronomy’s fourth step told by the people it happened to. The Guide to the Scriptures defines being born again as “to have the Spirit of the Lord cause a mighty change in a person’s heart,” and the people say the Spirit “has wrought a mighty change in us, or in our hearts, that we have no more disposition to do evil” ([[Mosiah 5:2]]); Benjamin answers that their hearts are changed “through faith on his name” and “because of the covenant which ye have made” ([[Mosiah 5:7]]).[@gs-born-again] The Student Manual calls the heart “the organ of spiritual rebirth.”[@ot-manual-genesis-12-17]

The step from the gift to the Giver as the life is the one Ellicott takes at 30:20, and I take it with him, as a reading. The Savior said it of Himself: “I am the resurrection, and the life” ([[John 11:25]]). Deuteronomy does not say that. It says that the Lord will give the heart He wished for, for the sake of Israel’s life, and that Israel is to cleave to Him because He, or that, is its life. The book’s own reading is enough for the book: the life a changed heart is for is bound to the One who changes it.

## Choose life

The choosing stays with Israel, and the Church’s footnote at “life” in 30:15 says through whom the choice is open: men “are free to choose liberty and eternal life, through the great Mediator of all men” ([[2 Ne. 2:27]]).[@lds-scriptures] *Come, Follow Me* sets the same two passages side by side, and Scripture Central says Lehi’s statement “strongly echoes Deuteronomy” at 30:19.[@cfm-ot-2026-20][@sc-knowhy-287] “I have set before you life and death… therefore choose life” ([[Deut. 30:19]]) comes thirteen verses after the promise that the Lord will circumcise the heart, and the book does not blur the two. A disciple unable to love with all his heart stands where Israel stood between 10:16 and 30:6: commanded to love, and not yet given the heart to do so. The command remains, and the heart is promised to those who “return unto the LORD thy God” ([[Deut. 30:2]]). Elder Christofferson says why the command stands: “God commands us to love Him because of what He knows it will do for us.”[@christofferson-first-commandment] Mormon tells the disciple what to do in the meantime, “pray unto the Father with all the energy of heart, that ye may be filled with this love, which he hath bestowed upon all who are true followers of his Son” ([[Moro. 7:48]]). Mormon’s love is charity, “the pure love of Christ” ([[Moro. 7:47]]), and he speaks of it as bestowed where 6:5 speaks of love commanded; reading the two together is mine. The Lord who wished for Israel’s heart at Horeb promises it to those who return, and promises it for a life that is bound to Him.
