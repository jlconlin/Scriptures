Deuteronomy is a book that listens in on the heart. Four times Moses stops his speech to tell Israel a sentence it may one day say to itself, and each time the Hebrew is the same clause, “say in thine heart.”[@oshb-wlc] “If thou shalt say in thine heart, These nations are more than I; how can I dispossess them?” ([[Deut. 7:17]]). “And thou say in thine heart, My power and the might of mine hand hath gotten me this wealth” ([[Deut. 8:17]]). “Speak not thou in thine heart… saying, For my righteousness the LORD hath brought me in to possess this land” ([[Deut. 9:4]]). “And if thou say in thine heart, How shall we know the word which the LORD hath not spoken?” ([[Deut. 18:21]]). Two more sayings use other idioms for the same thing. A man about to refuse a loan is warned “that there be not a thought in thy wicked heart, saying, The seventh year, the year of release, is at hand” ([[Deut. 15:9]]), and a man who hears the covenant’s curse read aloud is pictured as “he bless himself in his heart, saying, I shall have peace” ([[Deut. 29:19]]).[@oshb-wlc]

Three things about these sayings run through the book. Only one of them is forbidden outright. Two are conditions, “if thou say” (7:17; 18:21); two are warnings, “beware… lest” (8:11–17; 15:9); one is a description (29:19); and the one about righteousness is a direct prohibition, “speak not thou.”[@oshb-wlc] Not one of them is answered from inside the heart. The answer to the first two is “remember” ([[Deut. 7:18]]; [[Deut. 8:18]]); to the third, “not for thy righteousness” twice over ([[Deut. 9:5–6]]); to the fourth, what comes to pass ([[Deut. 18:22]]); to the man with the loan, “he cry unto the LORD against thee, and it be sin unto thee” ([[Deut. 15:9]]); to the man who blesses himself, “The LORD will not spare him” ([[Deut. 29:20]]). And the book has another kind of sentence about the heart, in which the heart does not speak but receives: “these words… shall be in thine heart” ([[Deut. 6:6]]); “the word is very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart” ([[Deut. 30:14]]); and twice the heart itself is something given or not yet given, “the LORD hath not given you an heart to perceive” ([[Deut. 29:4]]), “the LORD thy God will circumcise thine heart” ([[Deut. 30:6]]).

My argument is that Deuteronomy sets these two kinds of sentence against each other on purpose, and that Paul’s words in Romans 10 follow the same shape. What the heart says to itself, in this book, is a claim or a question of its own making: about the enemy’s size, about its own hand, about its own righteousness, about what it can know, about its own money, about its own safety. What the heart is given is a word from outside and, at last, a new heart. The one saying Moses forbids outright is the claim of righteousness, and when Paul sets out the righteousness of faith he opens it with the Septuagint’s exact words for that prohibition, “Say not in thine heart,” quotes the “word… in thy heart” of chapter 30, and closes with a heart that “believeth unto righteousness” ([[Rom. 10:6]]; [[Rom. 10:8]]; [[Rom. 10:10]]). The righteousness Israel’s heart was forbidden to claim is given to the heart that believes. The commentators carry some of these steps and I carry the rest, and I say which as I go. Reading the six sayings as one sequence is mine; so is the closing of the frame at Romans 10:10.

## If thou shalt say in thine heart

The first saying is fear. Israel, “the fewest of all people” ([[Deut. 7:7]]), faces seven nations “greater and mightier” ([[Deut. 7:1]]), and Moses knows what it will think: “These nations are more than I.” His answer is not an argument but a memory: “Thou shalt not be afraid of them: but shalt well remember what the LORD thy God did unto Pharaoh, and unto all Egypt” ([[Deut. 7:18]]). The site’s chapter on [Deuteronomy 7](/deuteronomy/7/) follows the thread. Keil puts the mechanism in a sentence: “To suppress the thought that was rising up in their heart… the Israelites were to remember what the Lord had done in Egypt.”[@keil-deuteronomy]

The second saying is the opposite of fear, and it comes after the land is won. It is not a condition but a warning, a “lest” clause that runs from “Beware that thou forget not the LORD thy God” ([[Deut. 8:11]]) through “Lest when thou hast eaten and art full” ([[Deut. 8:12]]) to “thou say in thine heart, My power and the might of mine hand hath gotten me this wealth. But thou shalt remember the LORD thy God: for it is he that giveth thee power to get wealth” ([[Deut. 8:17–18]]).[@oshb-wlc] The answer is the same verb as before, “remember,”[@oshb-wlc] and what the heart is remembering is now not Pharaoh but the source of its own strength. The Cambridge commentary notices that “say in thine heart” is not about speech: “whether or not thou sayest this expressly with thy lips, thou feelest and practically behavest as if thine own power and might had gotten thee this wealth.”[@cambridge-deuteronomy] Jennifer Lane reads the verse as idolatry in its subtler form, “putting their trust in their material resources and their own power.”[@lane-redemption] Scripture Central’s commentary traces the slide: forgetting the Lord’s role in prosperity “undoes their memory of the Lord’s blessings in the wilderness,” and “the end result is their believing that they created their own ‘power and might.’”[@ot-minute-deut-rappleye] The site’s note on [Deuteronomy 8:17](/deuteronomy/8/) has the rest.

The saying in chapter 18, which I take before the third because the third needs its own section, is a question rather than a boast: “How shall we know the word which the LORD hath not spoken?” ([[Deut. 18:21]]). It is the one saying of the six that Moses does not rebuke, and its answer is still from outside the heart: “if the thing follow not, nor come to pass, that is the thing which the LORD hath not spoken” ([[Deut. 18:22]]). Keil: “the occurrence of what had been predicted is made the criterion of true prophecy.”[@keil-deuteronomy] The site’s note on [Deuteronomy 18:22](/deuteronomy/18/) weighs the test. The saying of chapter 15 is the smallest and the most ordinary. A poor brother asks for a loan, the sabbatical year that cancels debts is near, and “a thought in thy wicked heart” does the arithmetic; the answer is that the poor man’s cry reaches the Lord, and Moses adds, “and thine heart shall not be grieved when thou givest” ([[Deut. 15:9–10]]). And the saying of chapter 29, the man who blesses himself in his heart, is the one the book treats most severely: he has heard the curse and tells himself “I shall have peace, though I walk in the imagination of mine heart,” and “The LORD will not spare him” ([[Deut. 29:19–20]]). Scripture Central, following a study of the Hebrew behind “imagination,” offers that the word names “a kind of idolatrous self-reliance.”[@sc-knowhy-546] Keil reads the verb plainly: “To bless himself in his heart is to congratulate himself.”[@keil-deuteronomy] The site’s note on [Deuteronomy 29:19](/deuteronomy/29/) follows him.

## Speak not thou in thine heart

The third saying stands apart in the Hebrew and in the argument. “Speak not thou in thine heart, after that the LORD thy God hath cast them out from before thee, saying, For my righteousness the LORD hath brought me in to possess this land: but for the wickedness of these nations the LORD doth drive them out from before thee” ([[Deut. 9:4]]). Unlike the conditions and the warnings, this is *ʾal tōʾmar*, do not say.[@oshb-wlc] Moses then denies the sentence twice more in two verses, “Not for thy righteousness, or for the uprightness of thine heart… for thy righteousness; for thou art a stiffnecked people” ([[Deut. 9:5–6]]), and spends the rest of the chapter proving the point from the golden calf.

The Cambridge commentary sends the reader of 9:4 straight back a chapter, “Speak … in thine heart] See on Deuteronomy 8:17,”[@cambridge-deuteronomy] and Keil saw what joins the two sayings and named them: “Besides the more vulgar pride which entirely forgets God, and attributes success and prosperity to its own power and exertion, there is one of a more refined character, which very easily spreads—namely, pride which acknowledges the blessings of God; but instead of receiving them gratefully, as unmerited gifts of the grace of the Lord, sees in them nothing but proofs of its own righteousness and virtue.”[@keil-deuteronomy] Keil gives no verse for the first pride; that it is 8:17 is my identification. The second pride is harder to catch because it has already learned the first lesson. It admits that the Lord gave the land. It only adds why. Keil’s conclusion on the chapter is that the reason for Canaan “was simply the promise of God… nothing but the free grace of God, not any merit on the part of the Israelites.”[@keil-deuteronomy] The site’s chapter on [Deuteronomy 9](/deuteronomy/9/) carries that reading.

The noun matters. “Righteousness,” *ṣĕdāqâ*, occurs six times in Deuteronomy, and three of them are here, each with a possessive and each denied: “my righteousness,” “thy righteousness,” “thy righteousness.”[@oshb-wlc] One is the Lord’s own, “the justice of the LORD” in Moses’ blessing ([[Deut. 33:21]]). The other two say something that sounds, in English, like the forbidden sentence. “It shall be our righteousness, if we observe to do all these commandments” ([[Deut. 6:25]]); and of returning a poor man’s pledge, “it shall be righteousness unto thee before the LORD thy God” ([[Deut. 24:13]]). The commentators relate 6:25 to 9:4 in two ways, and the site’s note on [Deuteronomy 6:25](/deuteronomy/6/) gives the older readings. Keil says 6:25’s righteousness “is not really the gospel ‘righteousness of faith’; but there is no opposition between the two, as the righteousness mentioned here is not founded upon the outward (pharisaic) righteousness of works, but upon an earnest striving after the fulfilment of the law, to love God with all the heart; and this love is altogether impossible without living faith.”[@keil-deuteronomy] The Cambridge commentary separates the senses instead: at 6:25 the word means “justification, vindication, the right to live,” and at 9:4 it is “Here ethical: contr. Deuteronomy 6:25.”[@cambridge-deuteronomy] Either way the two verses are not saying the same thing. What 6:25 promises is a standing before the Lord that comes with keeping His commandments. What 9:4 forbids is a people telling itself that its own righteousness is why it has the land. The book holds both, and so does this essay.

## The word in thy heart

Set against the six sayings is a different grammar of the heart, in which the heart is the place something is put. “These words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart” ([[Deut. 6:6]]), where the Hebrew preposition is “upon” the heart, as it is again at “lay up these my words in your heart” ([[Deut. 11:18]]).[@oshb-wlc] And at the end of Moses’ long speech, the famous passage: the commandment “is not in heaven, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go up for us to heaven… Neither is it beyond the sea, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go over the sea for us… But the word is very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayest do it” ([[Deut. 30:12–14]]). Here too Israel is given sentences it might say, and here too the answer comes from outside, but the shape has changed. In chapters 7 to 9 the heart speaks and is corrected. In chapter 30 the heart does not speak at all; it is spoken into. The site’s note on [Deuteronomy 30:12](/deuteronomy/30/) explains the passage.

Between those two kinds of sentence the book says that Israel may not yet have a heart to perceive at all. “Yet the LORD hath not given you an heart to perceive, and eyes to see, and ears to hear, unto this day” ([[Deut. 29:4]]). And then it promises one. “The LORD thy God will circumcise thine heart, and the heart of thy seed, to love the LORD thy God with all thine heart” ([[Deut. 30:6]]). The site’s essay [For He Is Thy Life](/deuteronomy/themes/for-he-is-thy-life/) follows that promise, and this essay only needs its place in the pattern. The heart that said “my power” and “my righteousness” is not told to say better things. It is told to remember, it is given words from outside, and it is promised that God will remake it. The reading of these as one pattern is mine.

## The righteousness of faith speaketh

Paul read Deuteronomy, and Romans 10 and 11 draw on three of its heart passages. The plainest is chapter 30. “The righteousness which is of faith speaketh on this wise, Say not in thine heart, Who shall ascend into heaven? (that is, to bring Christ down from above:) Or, Who shall descend into the deep? (that is, to bring up Christ again from the dead.) But what saith it? The word is nigh thee, even in thy mouth, and in thy heart: that is, the word of faith, which we preach” ([[Rom. 10:6–8]]). Cambridge, Keil and the New Testament Student Manual all name the source as Deuteronomy 30:12–14, and the Student Manual says what Paul does with it: he “quoted Deuteronomy 30:12–14 to make the point that one need not ‘ascend into heaven’ or ‘descend into the deep’ to find Christ,” since “all people… can find the Savior within their own hearts.”[@nt-student-manual-37] Keil, from the Deuteronomy side, says the same: “the declaration, ‘the word is in thy heart,’ only attains its full realization through the preaching of the gospel of the grace of God, and the righteousness that is by faith; and to this the Apostle Paul applies the passage in Romans 10:8.”[@keil-deuteronomy] A chapter later Paul quotes the other heart verse, 29:4, of Israel in his own day: “God hath given them the spirit of slumber, eyes that they should not see, and ears that they should not hear” ([[Rom. 11:8]]), a combination of Isaiah and Deuteronomy that the Cambridge commentary unpacks.[@cambridge-romans]

But Paul’s opening words are not in Deuteronomy 30. The Cambridge commentary on Romans notices it: “*in thine heart*] Words not in Heb. or LXX” of 30:12, “but meaning what the Heb. (‘that thou shouldest say’) means.”[@cambridge-romans] That is true of chapter 30. The six Greek words Paul uses, *mē eipēs en tē kardia sou*, stand in the Septuagint at two places in Deuteronomy, and they are the two sayings of pride: 8:17, “my power,” and 9:4, “my righteousness.”[@swete-lxx-deut][@greek-nt] The Greek translators turned the “lest” of 8:17 into the same “say not” as 9:4, and gave 18:21 the same words without the “not.”[@swete-lxx-deut] Jared Ludlow, listing the Old Testament passages Paul quoted, gives Romans 10:6–8 as a place where he “quoted an Old Testament phrase then gave its meaning as it applied to Christ and the righteousness which came from faith,” and names the passages quoted as “Deuteronomy 9:4; 30:12–14.”[@ludlow-paul-ot] So the pairing of Paul’s clause with 9:4 is not mine; Ludlow makes it, though he does not stop to explain it. What I add is the second verse, 8:17, where the same six words stand, so that Paul’s opening is the Septuagint’s wording for both of Deuteronomy’s sayings of pride and for nothing else in the book. The clause that in Deuteronomy introduced a claim of righteousness is, in Paul, spoken by the righteousness of faith. And what follows the clause has the same shape in both. In Deuteronomy the heart is forbidden to say that the land is its own doing. In Paul the heart is forbidden to ask who will go and fetch Christ, which the Cambridge commentary reads as the same anxiety, “In order to be saved, have I to bring the necessary Manifestation of God’s will from Heaven or Hades? Have I to procure Incarnation and Resurrection?” with the answer “No; all is now done… thy part is to accept and own His Son as thy Justification.”[@cambridge-romans] Paul had just said of Israel that they were “going about to establish their own righteousness” ([[Rom. 10:3]]). Setting that beside 9:4 is my step.

In this reading, Paul then closes the frame. “If thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart that God hath raised him from the dead, thou shalt be saved. For with the heart man believeth unto righteousness” ([[Rom. 10:9–10]]). Mouth and heart are Deuteronomy 30:14’s pair, as the Cambridge commentary observes,[@cambridge-romans] and righteousness, the subject of Deuteronomy 9:4–6, is the subject of Paul’s whole passage ([[Rom. 10:3–10]]). The heart that was forbidden to say “for my righteousness” believes, and righteousness is what it believes unto. No commentator I have read puts 9:4 and 10:10 together; the pairing is mine. What I can say for it is that it is Deuteronomy’s own shape, carried through: the heart’s claim refused, a word put into the heart from outside, and a heart that receives. In my reading Paul names the word and says whose the righteousness is.

The Church’s edition does not make this join, and I should say so. Its footnotes at Romans 10:6–8 lead to no verse of Deuteronomy, and its footnotes in Deuteronomy 9 and 30 lead to no verse of Romans.[@lds-scriptures] What it does join is Paul’s thesis to Lehi’s. “Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to every one that believeth” ([[Rom. 10:4]]) is footnoted to “he offereth himself a sacrifice for sin, to answer the ends of the law, unto all those who have a broken heart and a contrite spirit” ([[2 Ne. 2:7]]), and Lehi’s verse back to Paul’s.[@lds-scriptures] Lehi says of salvation what Deuteronomy 9 says of the land, that it is not earned, and that analogy is mine: “by the law no flesh is justified” ([[2 Ne. 2:5]]), and “there is no flesh that can dwell in the presence of God, save it be through the merits, and mercy, and grace of the Holy Messiah” ([[2 Ne. 2:8]]). Elder Christofferson, on verse 7: Christ “can satisfy or ‘answer the ends of the law’ on our behalf… We are pardoned and placed in a condition of righteousness with Him.”[@christofferson-justification]

## The same sentence now

A disciple has the sentences of Deuteronomy available to him, and the book’s way with them is still the right way. The first two, fear and self-credit, are answered by remembering, and Elder Renlund has made the same point from the Lord’s own words: “In nothing doth man offend God, or against none is his wrath kindled, save those who confess not his hand in all things.” “I do not think that God is insulted when we forget Him,” he said. “Rather, I think He is deeply disappointed.”[@renlund-goodness-greatness] The third is the refined one, and a faithful disciple can say it too. Keil’s “more refined” pride, the kind that sees God’s blessings as proofs of its own righteousness, fits a Latter-day Saint as well as an Israelite.[@keil-deuteronomy] The New Testament Student Manual quotes President Ballard against exactly this: “No matter how hard we work, no matter how much we obey, no matter how many good things we do in this life, it would not be enough were it not for Jesus Christ and His loving grace… there are some within the Church who have become so preoccupied with performing good works that they forget that those works—as good as they may be—are hollow unless they are accompanied by a complete dependence on Christ.”[@nt-student-manual-37] Alma told his son the same two things in two verses: “there is no other way or means whereby man can be saved, only in and through Christ,” and “see that ye do not boast in your own wisdom, nor of your much strength” ([[Alma 38:9]]; [[Alma 38:11]]).

This is not an argument about faith and works, and the verses do not need one. Nephi’s “it is by grace that we are saved, after all we can do” ([[2 Ne. 25:23]]) stands, and President Uchtdorf has said how to read it: “‘after’ does not equal ‘because.’ We are not saved ‘because’ of all that we can do.”[@uchtdorf-gift-of-grace] The Bible Dictionary keeps both halves in one paragraph, that grace is “an enabling power” and that it “cannot suffice without total effort on the part of the recipient.”[@bd-grace] Deuteronomy asks for that effort on every page, “with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might” ([[Deut. 6:5]]). What it forbids is one sentence about that effort, said in the heart: that it is the reason. The land was the Lord’s to give and He gave it; the righteousness that saves is Christ’s and He gives it, to the heart that believes. A disciple may keep every commandment Moses gave and still not say the sentence of 9:4, because Moses forbade it to a people who had the commandments, and Paul, using Moses’ words, set the same refusal before a people who had Christ.
