# Hosea brief

Book-level decisions for Hosea. The site-wide standard is in `STANDARDS.md`; this file records what is particular to Hosea. Writers, checkers, and reviewers read both before starting a chapter. Jeremiah's brief (`content/jeremiah/BRIEF.md`) is the model, and its section “What a chapter is for” holds here word for word; the author's rules recorded in Genesis's brief carry over to every book. Ezekiel's brief (“Learned from the first twelve chapters”) holds here too and is repeated below in short.

The author has not read this brief. The book is in preview (`status: preview` in `book.yaml`). Where a section says “Proposed,” that is the approach the chapters follow; it is a decision of the coordinator's, not a fact from a source, and the author can overturn it.

Related files: `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` at the top of the repository (what waits for the author, for every book); `THEME-CANDIDATES.md` in this folder. Genesis, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Lamentations, Ezekiel, Daniel, Malachi, Moses, and Abraham are on the site.

## What the author has decided

Nothing for Hosea in particular. These standing rules apply:

- **The arrangement of `AUTHORING.md`, “Plan for the next book”:** an Opus writer, the ledger script, a Sonnet checker, an Opus reviewer, then Codex's language pass.
- **No focus questions** (author, 2026-10-06). Writers work from the questions a careful reader would ask (`STANDARDS.md` §3).
- **Don't replicate the Gospel Library** (author, 2026-10-03; `STANDARDS.md` §1). The footnotes, chapter headings, and *Scripture Helps* are beside the verse already. A note that only says what they say is not worth writing.
- **Quote the Church's material sparingly** (author, 2026-10-07; `STANDARDS.md` §1): paraphrase and cite; a short phrase where the wording matters; never the same passage in two chapters. Look beyond the Gospel Library first. In Hosea the passage at risk is President Eyring's “a love story” (printed in *Scripture Helps* and at length in the Student Manual): it is quoted once, in chapter 1 or 2, and not again. President Nelson on *hesed* is quoted once, at 2:19.
- **Newest trumps oldest** (author, 2026-10-03). The newest is *Scripture Helps: Old Testament* (2025), which treats chapters 1–6 and 10–14 in eleven paragraphs. The Student Manual (chapter 10, on all fourteen chapters) is older, and the Bible Dictionary is not an official statement of doctrine.
- **Keil is a last resort** (author, 2026-10-08): search BYU, Scripture Central, and *Interpreter* first; an older commentary comes in only for a question none of them answers, in no more than about a third of a chapter's notes. A writer whose chapter cites one says in its report what it looked for first.
- **Follow *Scripture Helps* to what it cites**; **decide what the standard decides**; **the Student Manual limits apply** (about a third of a chapter's notes at most, and a note that cites it also rests on something from beyond the Church's own publications).

## What a chapter is for here

Hosea is short (14 chapters, 197 verses) and hard. Its two parts ask different things of a chapter.

**Chapters 1–3** are the household: the command to marry, three children, the Lord's speech to His unfaithful wife, and the buying back. The Church's helps are fullest here (seven of *Scripture Helps*' eleven paragraphs, and a quarter of the Student Manual's chapter), and the danger is repeating them. A chapter does what they don't: says who is speaking and to whom in each part (the Lord to Hosea; Hosea of his wife; the Lord of Israel, with the two running together in chapter 2); explains the things named (Jezreel and Jehu's blood, the corn and wine and flax thought to be Baal's gifts, the valley of Achor, the price of fifteen pieces of silver and the barley, the ephod and teraphim); and asks the follow-up question (not only “did Hosea really marry her?” but “what did the children's names sound like to their neighbours, and what happens to those names by the end of chapter 2?”).

**Chapters 4–14** are oracles with almost no narrative and no dates. *Scripture Helps* has one section on them (6:6–7) and skips 7–9 altogether; the Student Manual has a short paragraph on a verse here and there. These chapters move fast from image to image (a backsliding heifer, a cake not turned, a silly dove, a deceitful bow, a wild ass alone, an empty vine, a morning cloud), and a reader's first questions are plain ones: *What is this picture, and what is it a picture of? Who is Ephraim? Which king, which alliance, which place?* A chapter answers those. **A note explains one image and what it charges Israel with; it does not catalogue every image in the chapter.** Places that carry a history (Gilgal, Beth-aven, Gibeah, Baal-peor, Mizpah, Tabor, Adam or Admah, Achor, Jezreel) get that history in a sentence with the `[[reference]]` where it is told. The kings and alliances are identified only as far as a source read does (see below): the book names almost none of them.

## Divisions

*Scripture Helps* divides the book in two: “Hosea 1–3: The Lord's command to Hosea to marry,” and “Hosea 4–14: Hosea's prophetic warnings and promises to Israel.” `book.yaml` has three, keeping that line and adding one of the site's own at chapter 11, where the picture changes from a wife to a child (“When Israel was a child, then I loved him,” 11:1) and the book begins its turn toward the promise it ends on. Proposed: keep them; they are only the map on the book page.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (which king a verse may mean, what a Hebrew word can also mean, how a modern translation prints a verse, which New Testament verse takes up which line) is the coordinator's direction of where to look, some of it from memory. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **Say when, as far as the book lets you.** The only dates are in 1:1: “in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash, king of Israel.” No oracle after it is dated. The Bible Dictionary: Hosea “began to prophesy during the latter part of the reign of Jeroboam Ⅱ” and “probably died before the accession of Pekah, 736 B.C., for he makes no allusion to the Syro-Ephraimitic war nor to the deportation of the northern tribes by Tiglath-pileser two years later.” *Scripture Helps*: “the latter half of the eighth century BC,” “when Israel was close to being conquered by the Assyrian empire.” The Student Manual (10-3) prints a reckoning of “about 755 B.C. to 725 B.C.” Those do not agree on when he stopped, and 1:1 itself reaches to Hezekiah. Proposed: chapter 1's setting gives 1:1, says in a sentence that the Church's helps place the ministry in the middle and later 700s B.C. and that the Bible Dictionary and the manual differ on how late it ran, and leaves it. **Every later chapter's `when` is “undated; the northern kingdom's last decades, in the 700s B.C.”** or the like, and a chapter is tied to a particular king or year only from a source read, as that source's reading. For the year Samaria fell, use the Bible Dictionary's chronology (`bd-chronology`, in `sources.yaml`), as the other books on the site do.
- **The kings of the north.** 1:1 names one king of Israel, Jeroboam the son of Joash, though the four kings of Judah beside him reigned long after his death. 2 Kings 14:23–17:6 tells what followed him: Zachariah, Shallum, Menahem, Pekahiah, Pekah, and Hoshea, four of them murdered by their successors, and Samaria taken. That is the background of “they have set up kings, but not by me” (8:4), “all their kings are fallen” (7:7), and “I gave thee a king in mine anger, and took him away in my wrath” (13:11). Tell it once, in chapter 1's setting or at 7:3–7, from 2 Kings read in the Gospel Library; the others link. **The last king is also named Hoshea** (the same name as the prophet's in Hebrew; the Bible Dictionary heads its entry “Hosea, or Hoshea”): a reader may confuse them, so say so in a sentence where the king first matters.
- **Ephraim, Israel, Jacob, Samaria, Judah.** “Ephraim” is in 32 verses, and means the northern kingdom, after its leading tribe (the Student Manual, 10-17; the Bible Dictionary, “Ephraim”). Explain it once, at its first use (4:17), and don't return to it. “Judah” (15 verses) is the southern kingdom; the book speaks to the north and turns to Judah in asides (1:7; 4:15; 5:5, 10–14; 6:4, 11; 8:14; 10:11; 11:12; 12:2). Say once, in chapter 1's setting, that Hosea is a prophet of the north and (the Bible Dictionary) “the only prophet of the northern kingdom who has left written prophecies”; Amos preached there but came from Judah, so do not call Hosea the only prophet *to* the north.
- **Assyria and Egypt.** Israel runs to both (5:13; 7:11; 8:9; 12:1; 14:3) and will be carried to one and “return to Egypt” (8:13; 9:3, 6; 11:5). The Student Manual's Enrichment D (`ot-manual-kings-d`) tells Assyria's conquest of the north; the Bible Dictionary has “Assyria and Babylonia.” “King Jareb” (5:13; 10:6) is not a known king's name: say what a lexicon and a source read say of it and no more. Which tribute or which embassy a verse means is said only from a source read.
- **Baal and the Baalim.** The Student Manual's introduction (10-1) and the Bible Dictionary (“Baal”) explain the worship Hosea is charging. “Whoredom” in this book is both figure (idolatry as unfaithfulness to a husband) and fact (4:13–14 speaks of what was done at the shrines). Say which a verse means where the text shows it; what was done in Canaanite worship is described only from a source read, plainly and briefly. The site's readers include young people: say what the text says without dwelling on it.
- **The calves.** “Thy calf, O Samaria” (8:5–6), “the calves of Beth-aven” (10:5), “kiss the calves” (13:2) are Jeroboam the son of Nebat's golden calves at Bethel and Dan (`[[1 Kgs. 12:28–30]]`). **Beth-aven** (“house of vanity,” or of wickedness) is Hosea's name for Bethel, “house of God” (4:15; 5:8; 10:5; the Student Manual, 10-15): explain once, at 4:15, with the lexicon. `[[Gen. 28:19]]` on the site tells how Bethel got its name.
- **Two Jeroboams.** Jeroboam the son of Nebat divided the kingdom and set up the calves; Jeroboam the son of Joash (Jeroboam Ⅱ) is the king of 1:1, about a century and a half later. A reader confuses them. Say which, the first time each appears.
- **Verse numbers.** The Hebrew Bible numbers 1:10–11 as 2:1–2, so that its chapter 2 is two verses ahead through 2:23 (Hebrew 2:25); 11:12 is Hebrew 12:1, and chapter 12 is one ahead; 13:16 is Hebrew 14:1, and chapter 14 is one ahead (`scripts/hebrew.mjs` prints both). Commentaries may follow the Hebrew; cite the King James verse.
- **A hard text.** The King James is obscure in many verses of this book; the Church's footnotes offer 46 alternate renderings (“HEB,” “OR,” “IE”) across the book. Where the King James is obscure (“the calves of our lips,” 14:2; “they that take off the yoke on their jaws,” 11:4; “Judah yet ruleth with God,” 11:12; “like men,” 6:7; “the revolters are profound to make slaughter,” 5:2), give the footnote's rendering, the Hebrew word from `hebrew.mjs` and a lexicon page, and what one or two modern translations do (`nrsvue`, `niv`, `esv`); where the Septuagint reads differently and a New Testament writer follows it (14:2 and `[[Heb. 13:15]]`), say so from Brenton's page. **Don't call the text corrupt or emended from memory**; say that it is hard and show the renderings.
- **Names of God.** “LORD” (the divine name) throughout; copy any phrase with the name from `data/kjv/hosea.json`. In 12:14 the Church edition has “his Lord” (*adonai*). As in the other books, the Bible Dictionary identifies the LORD of the Old Testament as the premortal Christ; say it once where it first matters. The place is 2:16–20 or 13:4 (“there is no saviour beside me”); the Student Manual prints President Eyring saying of the husband in chapter 2 that he “was a metaphor for Jehovah, Jesus Christ.”
- **Repeated things are explained once and linked.** The marriage figure (chapters 1–3; later uses in 4:12–15, 5:3–7, 6:10, 9:1 point back); the children's names (1:4–9; reversed at 1:10–2:1 and 2:22–23); Ephraim (4:17); Beth-aven and the calves (4:15, 8:5); Gilgal (4:15; then 9:15, 12:11); Gibeah (5:8; the full note at 9:9 or 10:9, with Judges 19–20); Egypt and Assyria (7:11); “knowledge of God” (4:1, 6; 6:3, 6; and *know* in 2:20, 5:4, 13:4); *hesed*, mercy or lovingkindness (the full note at 2:19 or 6:6, not both; 4:1, 6:4, 10:12, 12:6 link); “return” (in 14 verses; the book's last call, 14:1, is the place for it).
- **Other books on the site.** Isaiah was Hosea's contemporary in Judah under the same four kings (`[[Isa. 1:1]]`): say so once. The marriage figure is taken up by Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel, all on the site: `[[Isa. 54:5]]`, `[[Jer. 2:2]]`, `[[Jer. 3:1]]`, `[[Jer. 31:32]]`, Ezekiel 16 and 23; Isaiah's theme page Zion and Her Husband, and Jeremiah's The Covenant Written in the Heart. The Bible Dictionary says Hosea “deeply influenced succeeding writers (see Isa. 40–66; Jer. 2–3; Ezek. 16; 33).” Link rather than re-explain, and say Hosea came first only as the Dictionary's sentence does. Hosea 12 retells Jacob (`[[Gen. 25:26]]`, `[[Gen. 28:12–19]]`, `[[Gen. 29:20]]`, `[[Gen. 32:24–28]]`), and 11:8 names Admah and Zeboim, cities destroyed with Sodom (`[[Gen. 14:2]]`; `[[Deut. 29:23]]`): Genesis on the site tells these. The note on `[[Gen. 48:16]]` already quotes Hosea 12:4–5 (`OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`, G1); chapter 12's note on the angel should agree with it and need not settle it.
- **Where the New Testament quotes Hosea.** 1:10 and 2:23 (`[[Rom. 9:25–26]]`; `[[1 Pet. 2:10]]`); 6:6 (`[[Matt. 9:13]]`; `[[Matt. 12:7]]`); 10:8 (`[[Luke 23:30]]`; `[[Rev. 6:16]]`); 11:1 (`[[Matt. 2:15]]`); 13:14 (`[[1 Cor. 15:55]]`); 14:2 (`[[Heb. 13:15]]`). The Church's footnotes give most of these (not 1 Corinthians 15:55 at 13:14, where they give the Topical Guide's “Resurrection” and “Jesus Christ, Prophecies about”; not Matthew 2:15 at 11:1, where the chapter heading and the Topical Guide do the work). Each is a note. Say what the verse means in Hosea first, then how the later writer uses it, and whether he is quoting, applying, or seeing a pattern, from a source read.
- **The Book of Mormon and modern revelation.** The Church's footnotes join Hosea to them in some thirty places; the weightier ones: 1:10–11 (D&C 132:30; 2 Ne. 29:8); 2:18 (D&C 45:9); 3:5 (2 Ne. 6:11; D&C 113:10); 6:1 (D&C 103:6); 8:7 (Mosiah 7:30, where King Limhi quotes the whirlwind as the Lord's word); 10:1 and 14:6 beside Jacob 5; 10:8 (2 Ne. 26:5; Alma 12:14); 13:4 (Mosiah 12:35; 2 Ne. 25:20); 14:2 (D&C 59:8); 14:5 (D&C 128:19). A cross-reference that only repeats the footnote is not a note; one that shows a Book of Mormon prophet using Hosea's words (Mosiah 7:30) is. **No comparison panel**; use the `scripture` kind. Nothing moves to a Book of Mormon chapter (author, 2026-10-07: no more Book of Mormon content for now).
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** One reading is in the Church edition's footnotes: 11:8, where “mine heart is turned within me, my repentings are kindled together” becomes “… toward thee, and my mercies are extended to gather thee.” It belongs in chapter 11 with a ledger row from the footnote (`lds-scriptures`), and it bears on 13:14 too (“repentance shall be hid from mine eyes”), though the translation changes nothing there.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports what runs across the book; the coordinator adds it to `THEME-CANDIDATES.md`. No theme pages are written until the author chooses them.

**Habits to avoid, learned on Ezekiel and Daniel** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter (the setting, the thread, a note, and the Witness of Christ each say what only they say); don't re-explain what an earlier chapter explained, point back with a `[[reference]]`; a Witness of Christ does not open by saying what the chapter lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer, not a promise of one (the site shows that sentence alone as the note's card); a reading stays a reading in every sentence; “In plain words” adds nothing to the verse; Hebrew only where it changes what the reader understands; don't join two verses yourself and call it the text's meaning (the reviewers cut more of this from Daniel than anything else).

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that fourteen chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-09.

- **Did the Lord command Hosea to marry an immoral woman (1:2–3; 3:1–3)?** This is the question every reader brings, and the Church's sources do not settle it. *Scripture Helps* (2025): “Scholars have long debated whether the Lord's command for Hosea to marry ‘a wife of whoredoms’ was literal or symbolic. Either way, the central message of the book of Hosea is not the marriage itself but what it symbolizes.” The Student Manual (10-5) lays out five views (a literal marriage to a harlot; a dream or vision; a wife who was faithful at first and became unfaithful; a wife who was a worshipper of Baal; an allegory of the prophet's call) and prints Sidney B. Sperry for the last, who held that a literal command “would be imputing to God a command inconsistent with His holy character.” Kent P. Jackson (`jackson-hosea-marriage`) gives three lines of interpretation, writes “My inclination is to reject number one, the literal interpretation,” and finds merit in the other two. Aaron P. Schade (`schade-hosea-family`): “even if the marriage is indeed historical, we do not possess sufficient information to interpret with any accuracy the details,” and he agrees with Sperry. An essay on Scripture Central (*The Jesus Christ Focused Old Testament*; find its author on the page) says most commentators take the marriage and births as actual events. **Proposed: the newest wins, and it declines to choose.** The note at 1:2 gives *Scripture Helps*' sentence as the frame, lays out the readings briefly as readings (three is enough: an actual marriage to a woman already or afterwards unfaithful; a vision or allegory; the prophet's call to a faithless people), says which Latter-day Saint scholars have leaned which way, and says what all of them agree the marriage means. **The chapters do not say what Gomer was or did beyond the text, do not write her inner life, and do not say that she repented or returned** (the text never says so of her; it says so of Israel). Sperry is cited only as the manual and Schade print him. This is the decision the author is most likely to want to make himself (`OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`, H1).
- **Is the woman of chapter 3 Gomer?** She is not named (“a woman beloved of her friend, yet an adulteress”). The Student Manual (10-8): “Scholars disagree on whether these represent two separate marriages or the same one. Either way …” Proposed: say that the text does not name her and that she is usually taken to be Gomer; write “the woman” where the text does; don't build on the identification.
- **The price (3:2).** The Student Manual (10-9) prints Keil's reckoning that silver and barley together come to thirty shekels, the price of a slave in `[[Ex. 21:32]]`, and adds its own application to the Redeemer's purchase. The reckoning depends on a guessed price for barley (Keil: “a very natural supposition”). Proposed: give it as a reckoning, with its “if”; thirty pieces of silver and `[[Zech. 11:12]]` or `[[Matt. 26:15]]` are joined to it only if a source read joins them. What the buying back pictures of the Lord and Israel is the manual's, in a sentence.
- **The blood of Jezreel (1:4) and Jehu.** In 2 Kings 9–10 Jehu destroys the house of Ahab at Jezreel at the word of a prophet and is commended for it (`[[2 Kgs. 10:30]]`); here the Lord will “avenge the blood of Jezreel upon the house of Jehu.” A reader will ask how both can be. *Scripture Helps*' endnote 11 quotes Ogden and Skinner on the forewarning to Jehu's dynasty; the Church's footnotes point to 2 Kings 10:11 and 15:12. Proposed: tell the history from 2 Kings; give an explanation of the tension only from a source read (2 Kings 10:29–31 itself says Jehu did not depart from Jeroboam's sins; Keil treats the question), as that source's. Jeroboam Ⅱ is Jehu's great-grandson and his son Zachariah the last of the line (`[[2 Kgs. 15:8–12]]`).
- **Jezreel means two things.** “God sows”: scattering in 1:4 and planting in 2:22–23 (“I will sow her unto me in the earth”). *Scripture Helps* and the manual both give it. This is the best wordplay in the book and belongs in chapter 2's note on 2:22–23, pointed to from 1:4.
- **“Not my people” and “the sons of the living God” (1:9–10; 2:23).** *Scripture Helps* reads the promise of the covenant by which people become “children of Christ” (Mosiah 5:7); Paul and Peter apply the verses to Gentiles brought in (`[[Rom. 9:25–26]]`; `[[1 Pet. 2:10]]`). 1:9 in Hebrew ends “and I am not *I am* to you” (the word of `[[Ex. 3:14]]`): give that only from `hebrew.mjs` and a source that says it. Proposed: the text first (the north cast off and regathered, with Judah, under “one head,” 1:11), then *Scripture Helps*, then the New Testament use as an application that widens it.
- **The Lord strips and shames the wife (2:3, 9–13).** These verses are violent, and modern readers are troubled by them. Seth N. Hord, “God Is Not Fragile” (*Religious Educator* 23, no. 1, 2022; readable, not yet read through), is written for teachers on exactly this. Proposed: say what the picture is (the husband withdraws the gifts she credited to her lovers: corn, wine, wool, flax) and what it is a picture of (famine, loss of the feasts, exile); don't soften it and don't moralize about it; read Hord before writing chapter 2 and use what he says, as his.
- **Ishi and Baali (2:16–17).** *Scripture Helps* has a section on it; the footnote gives “my husband.” Not a note unless it adds the Hebrew (*baal* is also the ordinary word for a husband or owner, which is why the name must go) from a lexicon page.
- **“I will betroth thee unto me for ever” (2:19–20).** *Scripture Helps* gives betrothal as legally binding and *hesed* with President Nelson's sentence; the Student Manual reads the verses of “the new and everlasting covenant restored to Israel in the latter days”; the footnote on “betroth” is the Topical Guide's “Abrahamic Covenant.” President Nelson's article (`nelson-everlasting-covenant`) and Muhlestein's (*Religious Educator* 21, no. 2) are readable. The note's own work: the five things the betrothal is made “in” (they stand where a bride-price would), and “thou shalt know the LORD.” This is the book's most-read passage: keep the note clear and unhurried. It carries chapter 2's Witness of Christ, with the bridegroom of the New Testament only as a footnote or source joins them.
- **“David their king … in the latter days” (3:5).** The Student Manual (10-11): “one of the titles of the Messiah or Jesus Christ.” Ezekiel on the site has the same figure (`[[Ezek. 34:23–24]]`, `[[Ezek. 37:24]]`; Ezekiel's brief follows *Scripture Helps* that “my servant David” is Jesus Christ) and so does `[[Jer. 30:9]]`. Follow them and link. The footnotes add 2 Nephi 6:11 and D&C 113:10. This is chapter 3's Witness of Christ.
- **Without ephod and teraphim (3:4).** The manual (10-11) says the list joins true worship and false, and that both would be lost. The Bible Dictionary has “Ephod” and “Teraphim” (`bd-teraphim` is in `sources.yaml`). Give what each thing was, and the manual's reading as one reading.
- **“My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge” (4:6).** Often quoted alone, of ignorance in general. In the verse it is said to the priests, who “rejected knowledge” and “forgot the law” and so will be “no priest to me.” Say that first; a wider application is given only as a source's.
- **“After two days will he revive us: in the third day he will raise us up” (6:1–3).** The footnote on “revive us”: “OR restore us to life; i.e., resurrect us.” The Student Manual (10-21): it “may be a symbolic reference to the gathering of Israel and the Millennium,” if a day is a thousand years. Christians have long read it of the Lord's rising on the third day; whether `[[1 Cor. 15:4]]` (“according to the scriptures”) has this verse in view is said only from a source read. And a reader should be told that the speakers are the people, and that the Lord's answer in 6:4 (“your goodness is as a morning cloud”) does not accept their words at face value; say that only as far as the text and a source show it. Proposed: the people's words and the Lord's answer first; then the footnote; then the manual's reading with its “may”; then the third-day reading if a source read makes it. **No count of days or millennia is worked out.**
- **“I desired mercy, and not sacrifice” (6:6).** *Scripture Helps* treats it (the word is *hesed*; the Savior quoted it twice; sacrifice still mattered). The note's work is what it does not do: who was offering sacrifice and where (the north's shrines), the parallel line (“the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings”), `[[1 Sam. 15:22]]`, and what each of the two occasions in Matthew was. `[[Isa. 1:11–17]]` on the site says the like. Chapter 6's Witness of Christ.
- **“Like men” or “like Adam” (6:7).** The footnote: “OR Adam.” KnoWhy #28 reports a study that reads it of the Fall. A place called Adam is also possible (`[[Josh. 3:16]]`) since the next verses name places; say that only from a source read. Give the readings as readings.
- **“The prophet is a fool, the spiritual man is mad” (9:7).** The Student Manual (10-29): “Hosea was referring to false prophets.” Others read it as what the people said of Hosea. Give the manual's reading and, if a source read gives the other, that too.
- **Hard sayings against children (9:11–16; 10:14; 13:16).** “Give them a miscarrying womb and dry breasts” (9:14); “the mother was dashed in pieces upon her children” (10:14); “their infants shall be dashed in pieces, and their women with child shall be ripped up” (13:16). A reader will stop here. Say what the text says: these describe what Assyrian conquest did (Enrichment D, “The Standardization of Terror,” tells it; `[[2 Kgs. 15:16]]` has Menahem doing the same), foretold as the end of the road Israel chose. Whether 9:14 is the prophet's prayer for a lesser evil is said only from a source read. Don't justify and don't pass over.
- **Shalman and Beth-arbel (10:14).** The Student Manual (10-33) gives Clarke's guess (Shalmaneser; Arbela). Unknown otherwise: say that it is not known, and give the guess as a guess from Clarke's own page.
- **“Out of Egypt have I called my son” (11:1; Matthew 2:15).** In Hosea the son is Israel and the calling is the Exodus (`[[Ex. 4:22–23]]`, the footnote); the next verse says the son went on to sacrifice to Baalim. Matthew says the child Jesus' return from Egypt fulfilled it. The Church's chapter heading: “Israel, as a child, was called out of Egypt in similitude of our Lord, as a child, coming out of Egypt.” The Student Manual (10-34): “a type or pattern.” That settles how the site reads it: **a similitude, in the heading's word**; Hosea's own sense first, then Matthew's use, with the heading. Don't call it a prediction of the flight to Egypt and don't call Matthew's use loose. This is chapter 11's Witness of Christ, with the Father's words over the son in 11:3–4, 8–9.
- **“How shall I give thee up, Ephraim?” (11:8–9), and “my repentings.”** The Joseph Smith Translation (above) removes the Lord's “repentings” and reads “my mercies are extended to gather thee.” Other books on the site have notes on the Lord “repenting” (`[[Gen. 6:6]]`; Jeremiah 18): link, and give the Joseph Smith Translation as the Church's footnote prints it. “I am God, and not man” (11:9) is the reason given for mercy, not for judgment: the note should say so from the verse.
- **Jacob in chapter 12.** Hosea reads Jacob's life against his descendants: the heel in the womb, the wrestling, Bethel, the flight to Syria, serving for a wife (12:3–4, 12). “He had power over the angel, and prevailed: he wept, and made supplication unto him” (12:4) adds weeping to Genesis 32. Whether the comparison blames Jacob or holds him up as the model (“therefore turn thou to thy God,” 12:6) is a reading: give what a source read says. Who the angel was: see `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`, G1, and the note on `[[Gen. 32:24]]`; agree with them.
- **“I have … used similitudes, by the ministry of the prophets” (12:10).** The chapter heading takes it up (“The Lord uses prophets, visions, and similitudes to guide His people”). It is the book's own account of its method, and Hosea's marriage is the first example. A note, from the heading, the manual's 10-2 (“the manner of prophesying among the Jews,” `[[2 Ne. 25:1]]`), and Donald W. Parry's article on symbolic action if it serves.
- **“By a prophet the LORD brought Israel out of Egypt” (12:13).** Moses; the footnote gives 1 Nephi 17:24. A short note at most.
- **“There is no saviour beside me” (13:4).** The footnotes: Acts 4:12; 2 Nephi 25:20; Mosiah 12:35; `[[Isa. 43:11]]` (on the site); the Topical Guide, “Jesus Christ, Jehovah.” *Come, Follow Me* asks children to see Jesus Christ as Savior and Redeemer in 13:4, 14. This and 13:14 are chapter 13's Witness of Christ.
- **“I will ransom them from the power of the grave” (13:14).** The King James reads a promise. The chapter heading: “He ransoms from the grave and redeems from death.” The Student Manual (10-37): the verse “uses the figures of resurrection as a metaphor that promises the gathering and restoration of Israel,” which “does not diminish” its witness of the Resurrection; and “repentance shall be hid from mine eyes” “could mean that the Lord will not swerve in His purpose.” Paul takes up “O death … O grave” in `[[1 Cor. 15:55]]`, in the Greek's wording (Brenton's page for the Septuagint); KnoWhys #533 and #93 touch it, with Abinadi's “the bands of death.” A reader with a modern translation will find the first two lines printed as questions (“Shall I ransom them …?”), a threat and not a promise, which is how the verses on either side read. Proposed: follow the heading and the King James, which Paul's use supports; say in a sentence that the Hebrew can be read as questions and that some modern translations do (quote one, from its page); then the manual on gathering and resurrection together. Don't argue it down either way.
- **“So will we render the calves of our lips” (14:2).** The Student Manual (10-38) explains the King James; the Septuagint has “the fruit of our lips,” which `[[Heb. 13:15]]` follows; the footnotes give D&C 59:8 and Psalm 51:17. A good note: the words of repentance stand where the young bulls stood. The Hebrew from `hebrew.mjs`; the Greek from Brenton's page.
- **“Take with you words” (14:1–3) and “I will love them freely” (14:4–8).** The chapter heading: “In the last days, Ephraim will repent and return unto the Lord.” The prayer is given to Israel word for word, and it renounces the book's three false helps by name (Asshur, horses, the work of our hands). The dew, lily, olive, and Lebanon answer the morning cloud and early dew of 6:4 and 13:3. Jacob 5 beside the olive tree (14:6) only as the footnote joins them. Chapter 14's Witness of Christ rests on the healing and free love of 14:4, with a source.
- **The last verse (14:9)** speaks to the reader (“Who is wise, and he shall understand these things?”). The footnotes give 2 Nephi 1:19 and 31:19. A short note: the book ends by handing itself to whoever reads it.
- **Applying the book to marriages.** The Student Manual's “Points to Ponder” (10-40 to 10-42) applies Hosea and Gomer to betrayal between spouses. That is counsel, not commentary, and it needs care a note cannot give (the Lord's taking Israel back is not a rule that a betrayed spouse must). **The chapters do not draw lessons for a reader's marriage.** A sentence may say that the Church's manual does.
