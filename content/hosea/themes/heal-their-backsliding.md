The verb “heal,” *rāpāʾ*, stands five times in Hosea, in five different forms, and the one who heals changes as the book goes on.[@oshb-wlc] First it is Assyria, and the verb is denied: “yet could he not heal you” (5:13). Then the people say it of the Lord: “he hath torn, and he will heal us” (6:1). Then the Lord says it of Himself three times, in the past and the future: “When I would have healed Israel” (7:1), “they knew not that I healed them” (11:3), and at the end, “I will heal their backsliding” (14:4). The Church’s edition joins the first and the last: the footnote on “heal” at 5:13 leads to 14:1–3, and the footnote on “save” at 14:3, “Asshur shall not save us,” leads back to 5:13.[@lds-scriptures]

The commentaries connect some of these five uses, and the lexicon lists them all, but none reads them as one story. The Cambridge commentary comes nearest, calling 11:3 “the same figure as in Hosea 5:13, Hosea 6:1, Hosea 7:1,” and the Interpreter study of the name Ephraim quotes four of them in order for the sound they share with Ephraim’s name.[@bdb][@cambridge-hosea][@bowen-fruit-of-loins] I read these five uses as one argument about where healing comes from. The argument turns on a difference the Cambridge commentary did see: the people say “he will heal us” in chapter 6 and are answered with a complaint, and they make a fuller confession in chapter 14 and are answered with “I will heal.” What that confession adds is a naming of the false healer that names the false healer of 5:13 and gives him up. On this reading, what the Lord then heals is their *backsliding* itself.

## “Yet could he not heal you”

The wound comes first. “When Ephraim saw his sickness, and Judah saw his wound, then went Ephraim to the Assyrian, and sent to king Jareb: yet could he not heal you, nor cure you of your wound” (5:13). The word for wound, *māzôr*, occurs here twice and elsewhere in the Old Testament only at Jeremiah 30:13; Keil reads it as “a festering wound, an abscess,” and names the disease: “The real disease of the two kingdoms was apostasy from the Lord.”[@oshb-wlc][@bdb][@keil-hosea] The Cambridge commentary: “Both states are conscious of the destroying cancer, but neither of them adopts the only possible means of arresting its progress.”[@cambridge-hosea] The Student Manual, quoting Sidney Sperry on Assyria and Egypt: “Political alliances would not remedy the real cause of their trouble—moral disease and rebellion against God.”[@ot-manual-10] Assyria is named nine times in Hosea, and the people go to it for help at 5:13, 7:11, 8:9 and 12:1; the first of these is where the book says what Assyria cannot do.[@oshb-wlc]

The Lord’s answer to the wound is to wound further, and then to wait. “I, even I, will tear and go away … I will go and return to my place, till they acknowledge their offence, and seek my face” (5:14–15). The Lord’s return depends on their acknowledging their offence; Keil ties it to the next chapter’s call, “As the endurance of punishment impels to seek the Lord (Hosea 5:15), so the motive to return to the Lord is founded upon the knowledge of the fact that the Lord can, and will, heal the wounds which He inflicts.”[@keil-hosea]

## “He hath torn, and he will heal us”

On the reading followed here, the people speak next. “Come, and let us return unto the LORD: for he hath torn, and he will heal us; he hath smitten, and he will bind us up” (6:1). The Hebrew gives no speaker; the verse is simply in the first person plural.[@oshb-wlc] Keil hears the prophet speaking for the exiles and takes the words at their worth: “The Assyrian cannot heal (Hosea 5:13); but the Lord, who manifested Himself as Israel’s physician in the time of Moses (Exodus 15:26), and promised His people healing in the future also (Deuteronomy 32:39), surely can.”[@keil-hosea] Barnes reads them as “words put into their mouth by God Himself.”[@barnes-hosea]

Verse 4 complicates that reading. “O Ephraim, what shall I do unto thee? O Judah, what shall I do unto thee? for your goodness is as a morning cloud, and as the early dew it goeth away” (6:4). The Cambridge commentary reads 6:1–3 as the words of “the only too quickly repentant Israelites,” a “hasty resolution, from which a full and free confession of sin was fatally absent (contrast penitent Israel’s words in Hosea 14:2). Hence the complaint of the omniscient Holy One which follows in Hosea 6:4.”[@cambridge-hosea] Ellicott calls them “words of imperfect penitence,” spoken by those “counting too soon on the boundless compassion of Jehovah.”[@ellicott-hosea] Keil reads 6:4 differently, not as an answer to 6:1–3 at all, since for him it “does not contain an answer from Jehovah,” but as the thought that “God has already tried all kinds of punishment to bring the people back to fidelity to Himself, but all in vain.”[@keil-hosea] Nothing in 6:1–3 is false. The Lord does tear and heal, and He is the right one to return to. What the sentence lacks, on the Cambridge reading this page follows, is any word about what the speakers had done or whom they had gone to.

## “When I would have healed Israel”

On one reading, the Lord’s first use of the verb describes an attempt to heal. “When I would have healed Israel, then the iniquity of Ephraim was discovered, and the wickedness of Samaria” (7:1). The Hebrew has no “would”; the form is “when I heal” or “as I healed,” and the commentaries split on its time.[@oshb-wlc] Keil reads it of what God had already tried: “As the dangerous nature of a wound is often first brought out by the attempt to heal it, so was the corruption of Israel only brought truly to light by the effort to stem it,” the effort being “the preaching of repentance and the reproofs of the prophets, and partly by chastisements designed to promote reformation.”[@keil-hosea] The Cambridge commentary, following an older proposal to change the Hebrew text, reads it of a healing still to come, “When I turn the fortunes of my people, when I heal Israel, then will be manifest Ephraim’s guilt”; Ellicott reads it of the Lord’s occasional healings in Israel’s history, “a cessation in the attacks of the menacing foe, or to such a thrill of finer feeling as that which is recorded in 2 Chronicles 28:8–15.”[@cambridge-hosea][@ellicott-hosea] Across these readings the verse says the same thing about the patient: healing exposes what was wrong. Chapter 7 goes on to the other physician again, “they call to Egypt, they go to Assyria” (7:11), and to the verb of chapter 6 used against them: “They return, but not to the most High” (7:16).

## “They knew not that I healed them”

The Lord’s second use is of a healing that had already happened. “I taught Ephraim also to go, taking them by their arms; but they knew not that I healed them” (11:3). Keil and the Cambridge commentary both hear Exodus 15:26 behind it, “I am the LORD that healeth thee,” spoken at Marah when the bitter water was made sweet.[@keil-hosea][@cambridge-hosea] The sentence sets the book’s two healers side by side a second time: the one Ephraim went to could not, and the one Ephraim did not notice did. The Church’s footnote on “healed” leads to 1 Nephi 17:41 and Alma 33:20, the brazen serpent, where those who looked were healed and “many … would not look”; Scripture Central reads Nephi’s verse of “healing and protection through Jesus Christ’s Atonement” for “all today who look to the Lord and follow Him.”[@lds-scriptures][@sc-knowhy-646] What Hosea’s verse itself says is that a people can be healed by the Lord and not know it.

## “Take with you words”

The last chapter gives Israel the sentence it did not have in chapter 6. “O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God; for thou hast fallen by thine iniquity. Take with you words, and turn to the LORD: say unto him, Take away all iniquity, and receive us graciously … Asshur shall not save us; we will not ride upon horses: neither will we say any more to the work of our hands, Ye are our gods” (14:1–3). The words begin with the iniquity 6:1 never mentioned, and they renounce the three things the verse lists, Assyria first and by name. Keil: “Rebellious Israel placed its reliance upon Assyria and Egypt (Hosea 5:13; Hosea 7:11; Hosea 8:9). It will do this no longer,” and “if the conversion is to be of the right kind, it must begin with a prayer for the forgiveness of sin, and attest itself by the renunciation of earthly help and simple trust in the mercy of God.”[@keil-hosea] The Cambridge commentary says the prophet now “cherishes the belief that Israel’s repentance will after all not be as superficial as he once feared.”[@cambridge-hosea] The Student Manual reads the “words” as prayer offered in place of sacrifice, “as precious as the best offerings in the Mosaic law.”[@ot-manual-10] The Church’s footnote at “save” sends the reader back to 5:13, so the Church’s footnote links “Asshur shall not save us” to the Assyrian who could not heal in 5:13.[@lds-scriptures] Matthew Bowen and Loren Spendlove hear the book’s own name in the line, since “Asshur will not save us” plays on the prophet’s name, which the Student Manual glosses as “‘help,’ ‘deliverance,’ and ‘salvation.’”[@bowen-fruit-of-loins][@ot-manual-10]

Then the Lord speaks, and it is the fifth “heal.” “I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely: for mine anger is turned away from him” (14:4). The object is new. The four earlier uses had a person or a people as what is healed; here the Lord heals *mĕšûbātām*, “their backsliding.”[@oshb-wlc] The noun comes from the verb “return,” *šûb*, the verb of 6:1 and 14:1–2, and means its opposite: Spendlove calls it “a Hebrew noun with the exact opposite meaning from teshuvah,” the word for repentance, used twelve times in the Old Testament, nine of them by Jeremiah, and in Jeremiah always of faithlessness.[@spendlove-turning-whole-heart] The lexicon glosses it “turning back, apostasy.”[@bdb] The commentators divide over the object. Keil and the Cambridge commentary take it as the damage the backsliding has done; Barnes and the Seminary manual take it as the backsliding itself. Keil: the Lord “promises first of all to heal their apostasy, i.e., all the injuries which have been inflicted by their apostasy from Him, and to love them with perfect spontaneity”; the Cambridge commentary: “the damage which their ‘backsliding’ has brought upon them”; Barnes: “that wound of their souls, from where every other evil came, their fickleness and unsteadfastness … In healing that disease of unsteadfastness, God healed all besides.”[@keil-hosea][@cambridge-hosea][@barnes-hosea] Ellicott: “If the foregoing be the offering of penitent lips, then the majestic reply of Jehovah is full of superlative grace.”[@ellicott-hosea] The Seminary manual: the Lord “would heal them of their backsliding, or apostasy, when the people of Israel return to Him.”[@ot-seminary-teacher-148] This page follows Barnes, and on that reading the disease Keil named at 5:13 is what the Lord says He will heal at 14:4; the join is mine, since Keil himself glosses 14:4 as the injuries. The Assyrian who was asked to heal the wound is the one the people have just given up.

## “Return unto me … that I may heal you”

Jeremiah, whose debt to Hosea the Bible Dictionary notes, says the sentence again with the same verb and the same noun: “Return, ye backsliding children, and I will heal your backslidings. Behold, we come unto thee; for thou art the LORD our God” (Jer. 3:22).[@oshb-wlc] Setting the two verses together is the site’s; no commentary read makes the join, though the lexicon files them under one entry.[@bdb][@bd-hosea] The Church’s footnote on “heal” at Jeremiah 3:22 leads to 3 Nephi 9:13 and 18:32, and in the first Jesus Christ’s own voice says it to the survivors during the darkness: “will ye not now return unto me, and repent of your sins, and be converted, that I may heal you?”[@lds-scriptures] The LORD who says “I will heal” in Hosea is Jehovah, the premortal Jesus Christ; Hosea’s own chapter 13 says “there is no saviour beside me” (13:4), and the Church’s footnote there leads to Acts 4:12 and to Nephi’s words on the brazen serpent, that, as surely as those who looked at the serpent were healed, there is “none other name … whereby man can be saved.”[@bd-jehovah][@lds-scriptures] *Come, Follow Me* quotes 14:4 and says, “This is the same message He gives us today as we seek to live our covenants with love and devotion,” and the Bible Dictionary sums the book up as the love of God, Israel’s restoration “due to His love (2:19; 14:4).”[@cfm-ot-2026-47][@bd-hosea] Seth Hord: “God is not fragile and can heal his people if they will turn to him and seek him out.”[@hord-not-fragile]

For a disciple who has sought healing from what cannot give it, the five sentences give the order of things, and *Come, Follow Me* reads 14:4 as a message for today.[@cfm-ot-2026-47] The wound is real, and Israel’s instinct was to take it somewhere that could not heal it (5:13). To say “he will heal us” is true, and on the reading this page follows it is not enough while what was done and where one went are left unsaid; Keil’s rule for chapter 14 is that conversion “must begin with a prayer for the forgiveness of sin, and attest itself by the renunciation of earthly help.”[@keil-hosea] The Lord has been healing all along, unnoticed, and Scripture Central reads that healing as still “available to all today who look to the Lord and follow Him.”[@sc-knowhy-646] The words that bring His “I will heal” are a confession that names the false help and lets it go, and His own voice in the Book of Mormon asks for the same, “repent of your sins, and be converted, that I may heal you” (3 Ne. 9:13).[@lds-scriptures] For those who have sought healing from false help, Israel’s confession says both things at once, the repentance and the giving up of that help.
