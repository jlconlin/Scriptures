Egypt is named in thirteen verses of Hosea, and the name does not mean one thing.[@oshb-wlc] In five of them Egypt is the past, the place the Lord brought Israel from: the wife “came up out of the land of Egypt” ([[Hos. 2:15]]), the Lord “called my son out of Egypt” ([[Hos. 11:1]]), “by a prophet the LORD brought Israel out of Egypt” ([[Hos. 12:13]]), and twice He names Himself “the LORD thy God from the land of Egypt” ([[Hos. 12:9]]; [[Hos. 13:4]]). In two Egypt is the present, a foreign power Ephraim courts: “they call to Egypt, they go to Assyria” ([[Hos. 7:11]]); “oil is carried into Egypt” ([[Hos. 12:1]]). In four Egypt is a future: “they shall return to Egypt” ([[Hos. 8:13]]); “Ephraim shall return to Egypt” ([[Hos. 9:3]]); “Egypt shall gather them up, Memphis shall bury them” ([[Hos. 9:6]]); “this shall be their derision in the land of Egypt” ([[Hos. 7:16]]). The remaining two verses turn the future around. “He shall not return into the land of Egypt” ([[Hos. 11:5]]), and the children come “as a bird out of Egypt, and as a dove out of the land of Assyria” ([[Hos. 11:11]]).

My argument is that Hosea’s Egypt verses are one argument about whether the Exodus can be undone. The book remembers the coming out as the Lord’s own act of love, in three voices: a husband’s, a father’s, and the covenant voice that says “I am the LORD thy God.” It then threatens a return to the land the Lord called Israel from, and the threat stands: Assyria is the bondage it names. But in chapter 11, in the father’s speech, the book will not send the son back to the land itself, on Keil’s reading of a disputed verse, and it promises the Exodus a second time, in the very words of the first. Matthew read the first verse of that chapter as fulfilled in the child Jesus, and he quoted it from the Hebrew, where the one called is “my son,” not from the Greek Bible, where it is “his children.” The commentators carry the individual steps and I say where each comes from. Reading the thirteen verses as one argument is mine.

## From the land of Egypt

The Lord’s own name for Himself in Hosea is tied to Egypt twice, and the grammar is strange. “And I that am the LORD thy God from the land of Egypt will yet make thee to dwell in tabernacles” ([[Hos. 12:9]]). “Yet I am the LORD thy God from the land of Egypt, and thou shalt know no god but me: for there is no saviour beside me” ([[Hos. 13:4]]). At Sinai the sentence had a verb: “I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt” ([[Ex. 20:2]]). The Hebrew phrase has no verb at all; it says “the LORD thy God from the land of Egypt” and stops.[@oshb-wlc] The Greek Bible, the Septuagint, supplies a verb in both verses, “I brought thee up out of the land of Egypt.”[@swete-lxx-hosea] The Hebrew dates the Lord’s relationship with Israel from Egypt. Keil renders it “from Egypt hither” and glosses it, “Israel has known no other God than Jehovah, i.e., has found no other God to be a helper and Saviour.”[@keil-hosea] Kent Jackson, translating 13:4–5 himself, reads the two verses as the covenant “expressed in the vocabulary of marriage,” and puts the point in one sentence: “Egypt is where Jehovah found Israel, his bride.”[@jackson-hosea-marriage] The site’s note on [Hosea 13:4](/hosea/13/) has the treaty background as well.

The husband’s voice is in chapter 2. When the Lord leads the wife back into the wilderness, she will answer Him “as in the days of her youth, and as in the day when she came up out of the land of Egypt” ([[Hos. 2:15]]). Keil reads the wife’s whole life against the Exodus: her birth is the bondage, the deliverance “the time of the divine courtship,” the covenant the marriage; and her answer “in the days of her youth” is the song at the Red Sea and the willingness at Sinai.[@keil-hosea] The site’s note on [Hosea 2:14](/hosea/2/) and the essay [I Will Betroth Thee unto Me](/hosea/themes/betroth-thee/) carry the marriage. Keil reads the promise as a repetition: “What took place in the olden time is to be repeated, in all that is essential, in the time to come. Egypt, the Arabian desert, and Canaan are types.”[@keil-hosea] Hosea’s first mention of Egypt is already a promise to do the Exodus over.

Hosea never uses “bring out,” the verb of Exodus 20:2; his words are “came up” for the wife and “brought up” for the people “by a prophet” ([[Hos. 2:15]]; [[Hos. 12:13]]), and, for the son, “called.”[@oshb-wlc] The prophet of 12:13 is Moses, as Keil and the Cambridge commentary agree and as the Church’s footnote to 1 Nephi 17 shows.[@keil-hosea][@cambridge-hosea][@lds-scriptures]

## Called my son

“When Israel was a child, then I loved him, and called my son out of Egypt” ([[Hos. 11:1]]). The Church’s footnote sends the reader to Exodus, where the Lord tells Moses what to say to Pharaoh, “Israel is my son, even my firstborn… Let my son go” ([[Ex. 4:22–23]]), and Keil says the verse “rests upon” that passage.[@lds-scriptures][@keil-hosea] The word for “child” stands in Hosea only here, and the Hebrew of “my son” carries a small preposition, so that the clause can be heard as “called him as my son,” which Matthew Bowen glosses that way; he applies to it the rule of the naming texts, “To be ‘called’ God’s ‘son’… was to become such.”[@oshb-wlc][@bowen-benjamin-addendum] Keil takes the adoption as the first step of something larger: it “began with its deliverance out of the bondage of Egypt, and was completed in the conclusion of the covenant at Sinai,” and “forms the first stage in the carrying out of the divine work of salvation, which was completed in the incarnation of the Son of God.”[@keil-hosea]

The verses that follow are a father’s memory. “I taught Ephraim also to go, taking them by their arms; but they knew not that I healed them. I drew them with cords of a man, with bands of love” ([[Hos. 11:3–4]]). Keil hears Deuteronomy behind it, “in the wilderness the Lord thy God bare thee, as a man doth bear his son,” and the healing of Exodus 15:26.[@keil-hosea] The site’s note on [Hosea 11:3](/hosea/11/) and the essay [I Will Heal Their Backsliding](/hosea/themes/heal-their-backsliding/) follow the healing. The key here is the verb in verse 1. The Lord “called” His son out of Egypt, and the next verse answers with the same verb: “As they called them, so they went from them” ([[Hos. 11:2]]).[@oshb-wlc] The site’s note on [Hosea 11:2](/hosea/11/) notices the echo. The verb has one more use that bears on Egypt, and it is Ephraim’s: “like a silly dove without heart: they call to Egypt, they go to Assyria” ([[Hos. 7:11]]).[@oshb-wlc] The son who was called out of Egypt calls back to it. I connect 7:11 with 11:1 through this shared verb, as the old page did.

## They shall return to Egypt

The threat comes three times. “Now will he remember their iniquity, and visit their sins: they shall return to Egypt” ([[Hos. 8:13]]). “They shall not dwell in the LORD’s land; but Ephraim shall return to Egypt, and they shall eat unclean things in Assyria” ([[Hos. 9:3]]). And then not a return but a burial: “Egypt shall gather them up, Memphis shall bury them” ([[Hos. 9:6]]). The Church’s footnote at 8:13 gives the source of the threat, the last of the covenant curses, “the LORD shall bring thee into Egypt again with ships, by the way whereof I spake unto thee, Thou shalt see it no more again” ([[Deut. 28:68]]), where “bring… again” means causing someone to “return.”[@lds-scriptures][@oshb-wlc] The law had also forbidden the king to “cause the people to return to Egypt,” with the Lord’s own words, “Ye shall henceforth return no more that way” ([[Deut. 17:16]]). To return to Egypt is to run the history backward. The site’s note on [Hosea 8:13](/hosea/8/) says it in those words.

Whether Hosea means the country is a real question, and the commentators divide. Keil reads Egypt in these three verses as “a type of the land of bondage,” a “special application” of Deuteronomy 28:68 to the north, “so that Assyria is represented as a new Egypt”; the Old Testament Student Manual agrees, “thus, Assyria is the new Egypt.”[@keil-hosea][@ot-manual-10] The Cambridge commentary calls the figurative reading “a most unnatural supposition,” and asks why a people that swung between the two alliances could not be threatened with an Egyptian captivity as well as an Assyrian one; and adds that “The word ‘return’ is pointed with the terrible associations of the ‘house of bondage.’”[@cambridge-hosea] Both sides agree on what the word carries. In Hosea the Hebrew verb for “return” stands twenty-two times, and Egypt is its destination in exactly three verses.[@oshb-wlc]

Hosea 11:5 turns from the threat. “He shall not return into the land of Egypt, but the Assyrian shall be his king, because they refused to return” ([[Hos. 11:5]]). The text is disputed, and the site’s note on [Hosea 11:5](/hosea/11/) sets out the readings: the Septuagint has “Ephraim dwelt in Egypt,” some drop the negative, Cambridge calls it “pointless,” adds that it “is also inconsistent with the statements in Hosea 8:13, Hosea 9:3; Hosea 9:6, Hosea 11:11,” and concludes, “the sense required is, ‘He shall return into the land of Egypt.’”[@swete-lxx-hosea][@cambridge-hosea] Keil keeps the “not,” and his reason is the one this essay rests on. In 8:13 and 9:3 Egypt was a type, but here “the typical interpretation is precluded partly by the contrast to Asshur, and still more by the correspondence in which the words stand to Hosea 11:1.” And then: “Into the land from which Jehovah called His people, Israel shall not return, lest it should appear as though the object, for which it had been brought out of Egypt and conducted miraculously through the desert, had been frustrated by the impenitence of the people. But it is to be brought into another bondage.”[@keil-hosea] Keil does not take the threat back. For him Egypt in 8:13 and 9:3 was Assyria under a type, and 11:5 names what the type meant. What the verse withholds, on his reading, is the literal land only, so that the Exodus is not seen undone on the map. The verse has “return” twice, once denied of Egypt and once of the people who “refused to return,” and four verses later the Lord uses the word of Himself: “I will not return to destroy Ephraim: for I am God, and not man” ([[Hos. 11:9]]).[@oshb-wlc] The people refuse to return; the Lord withholds His return to destroy them. I do not claim the text of 11:5 is settled. On Cambridge’s reading the children of 11:11 come out of an Egypt they will have been sent to, the Exodus redone rather than the return refused; on Keil’s the return is refused and the Exodus redone. Either way the chapter’s last word on Egypt is the next section’s, and it is “out.”

## Out of Egypt again

“They shall walk after the LORD: he shall roar like a lion: when he shall roar, then the children shall tremble from the west. They shall tremble as a bird out of Egypt, and as a dove out of the land of Assyria: and I will place them in their houses, saith the LORD” ([[Hos. 11:10–11]]). Three details tie the promise to the earlier verses; the chapter notes have the third. “Out of Egypt” is the same Hebrew form as in 11:1, “from Egypt” without “the land of,” and it stands nowhere else in the book but 12:13.[@oshb-wlc] “Children” is the plural of the “son” of 11:1, and the noun is in chapter 11 only in those two verses.[@oshb-wlc] And the dove, which in 7:11 flew to Egypt and Assyria “without heart,” flies in 11:11 out of the same two lands; the word is in Hosea only in those two verses.[@oshb-wlc] The site’s notes on [Hosea 7:11](/hosea/7/) and [Hosea 11:10](/hosea/11/) draw the bird’s two flights. The Cambridge commentary hears the trembling as “a thrill of eagerness doubling the speed of motion,” and renders “place them” as “cause them to dwell.”[@cambridge-hosea] The same verb stands in 12:9, “will yet make thee to dwell in tabernacles,” which both commentators read as a threat of another wilderness; Keil softens it to “only… temporary banishment.”[@oshb-wlc][@keil-hosea][@cambridge-hosea] The two verses share a verb, but 11:11 promises houses.

Keil places the fulfilment “in the last days, when Israel as a nation shall enter the kingdom of God,” with Egypt and Assyria standing for “all quarters of the globe” and Isaiah 11:11 beside it; Cambridge calls the return of the dispersed “one of the most characteristic features of the Messianic age.”[@keil-hosea][@cambridge-hosea] Isaiah’s verse heads its list of lands with Hosea’s pair: the Lord “shall set his hand again the second time to recover the remnant of his people… from Assyria, and from Egypt” ([[Isa. 11:11]]), and the site’s essay [The New Exodus and the Highway](/isaiah/themes/new-exodus/) follows it. Keil already reads 2:14–15 and 12:9 as the Exodus repeated; no source I have read says it of 11:11 by name. I do, on the ground that it brings the children out of Egypt in the words that brought the son, after the book has threatened to send them back. And if the Joseph Smith Translation of 11:8 is read with the roar two verses later, which is my step, the roar is for gathering: “my mercies are extended to gather thee.”[@lds-scriptures]

## Out of Egypt have I called my son

Matthew tells how Joseph “took the young child and his mother by night, and departed into Egypt: And was there until the death of Herod: that it might be fulfilled which was spoken of the Lord by the prophet, saying, Out of Egypt have I called my son” ([[Matt. 2:14–15]]). The quotation’s wording matters. The Septuagint of Hosea 11:1 reads “out of Egypt I called his children,” in the third person and plural; Matthew’s Greek reads “my son,” the Hebrew’s word.[@swete-lxx-hosea][@biblehub-interlinear][@oshb-wlc] Ellicott notes that Matthew “translated from the Hebrew instead of reproducing the Greek version,” and the Cambridge commentary on Matthew that “the LXX. differs considerably.”[@ellicott-matthew][@cambridge-matthew] The Hebrew’s “my son” fits Matthew’s one child as “his children” would not. Why the Greek translators wrote “children” no source I have read explains.

The commentators largely agree on what Matthew means by “fulfilled,” and the Church’s edition says it in a word. Hosea’s son is Israel; Ellicott says the words “refer, beyond the shadow of a doubt, to the history of Israel.”[@ellicott-matthew] Keil says they are quoted “not because the words of the prophet refer directly and immediately to Christ, but because the sojourn in Egypt, and return out of that land, had the same significance in relation to the development of the life of Jesus Christ, as it had to the nation of Israel.”[@keil-hosea] Cambridge: “The national ideal never realized in the nation was realized in the Christ.”[@cambridge-hosea] The Old Testament Student Manual calls Israel’s coming out “a type or pattern of Jesus’ coming out of Egypt”; Nicholas Frederick calls Matthew’s reading “typological prophecy,” Tyler Griffin says Matthew “chooses to interpret the Hosea passage typologically,”[@griffin-matthew-portrayal] and Jason Combs says Jesus “does not simply fulfill a prediction from Hosea rather, he fills out the divine message that Hosea had conveyed about Israel’s deliverance.”[@ot-manual-10][@frederick-ot-in-nt-gospels][@rsc-combs-ahaz-sign] Elder Talmage, placing the fulfilment at the family’s return, calls Hosea’s words a “prophetic vision of what should be.”[@talmage-jesus-christ-9] And the chapter heading of the Church’s edition says Israel “was called out of Egypt in similitude of our Lord, as a child, coming out of Egypt.”[@lds-scriptures] Its footnotes do not join the two verses directly: Matthew 2:15 leads to the Topical Guide, and Hosea 11:1 to Exodus 4:22 and the same Topical Guide entry.[@lds-scriptures] The site’s note on [Hosea 11:1](/hosea/11/) has the rest of this discussion.

One step remains, and it is the one Hosea’s own words make possible. The speaker of 11:1 is the one who says in 13:4, “I am the LORD thy God from the land of Egypt… there is no saviour beside me.” The Bible Dictionary identifies that LORD: “Jehovah is the premortal Jesus Christ.”[@bd-jehovah] Nephi swears by Him under the same description, “as the Lord God liveth that brought Israel up out of the land of Egypt… there is none other name given under heaven save it be this Jesus Christ” ([[2 Ne. 25:20]]), and in 1847 He named Himself so again: “I am he who led the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt; and my arm is stretched out in the last days, to save my people Israel” ([[D&C 136:22]]). In Matthew the voice that says “my son” is the Father’s, and Ellicott hears it so: Israel was “the one representative instance of the Fatherhood of God,” and “Now there was a higher representative in the person of the only begotten Son.”[@ellicott-matthew] What the Latter-day Saint verses add is the identity of Hosea’s speaker. The God who says “I am the LORD thy God from the land of Egypt,” and who in 1847 said “I am he who led the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt,” is the child Matthew sees brought out of it; and I read the arm “stretched out in the last days, to save my people Israel” as the roar of 11:10 in another figure. Setting 13:4 and the 1847 revelation beside Matthew 2 is this page’s own step, carried from the old page.

## Not back to Egypt

The Bible Dictionary says that in Hosea “all His chastisements are inflicted in love,” and that Israel’s “restoration shall be due to His love.”[@bd-hosea] Chapter 11 is where the book shows how those two fit. The son is punished, and the punishment is a real captivity; on Keil’s reading the father will not send him back to the place He brought him from, because that would make the bringing out look “frustrated.”[@keil-hosea] His “How shall I give thee up, Ephraim?” ([[Hos. 11:8]]) is, in my reading, not a refusal to punish but a refusal to undo. Van Dyke and Ogden say of the lion of verse 10 that, where Amos’s lion devours, “Hosea’s lion defends the returning prodigal and escorts her to a haven of safety.”[@vandyke-ogden-twelve]

Alma made the Exodus a disciple’s own matter. Praising the God who “has brought our fathers out of Egypt,” he adds that He “has delivered them out of bondage and captivity from time to time,” and tells his son, “I have always retained in remembrance their captivity; yea, and ye also ought to retain in remembrance, as I have done, their captivity” ([[Alma 36:28–29]]). Hosea’s book is such a remembrance, and it answers the son who has gone back the way he came with a call to return to the Lord. The verb’s last uses in Hosea are the call to return to the Lord and the Lord’s anger turned away: “O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God… I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely: for mine anger is turned away from him” ([[Hos. 14:1]]; [[Hos. 14:4]]).[@oshb-wlc] Nephi says of the first Exodus what Hosea says of the second, that it was love: “he loved our fathers, and he covenanted with them… wherefore, he did bring them out of the land of Egypt” ([[1 Ne. 17:40]]). The God “from the land of Egypt” is, in Alma’s words, the God who delivers “from time to time,” and my reading of Hosea’s Egypt verses is that He does not undo the deliverance by which He names Himself. He called a son out; He will call the children out of the lands they ran to; and in the child of Matthew 2 He came out of Egypt Himself ([[Matt. 2:21]]).
