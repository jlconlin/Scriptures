# John brief

Book-level decisions for the Gospel of John. The site-wide standard is in `STANDARDS.md`. **What John shares with Matthew, Mark and Luke is in `content/volumes/new-testament/GOSPELS.md`; read it first**: every chapter answers its own reader's questions, how a story's research is shared between the Gospels, Talmage's place, Ellicott as the outside commentary of last resort, the Greek, the themes and the images. This file holds what is John's own. Luke's brief is the model for its form, and what Matthew's, Mark's and Luke's briefs settle (the Pharisees and “the Jews” in the Synoptics, the Transfiguration, divorce, Markan priority and Q, the virgin birth, Joseph Smith—Matthew, the anointing at Bethany, the bloody sweat, “paradise”) holds here unchanged and is pointed to, not argued again.

**Status (2026-11-22):** drafted from a research survey made with the research scripts on 2026-11-22; each source named below was opened then, and a claim about what it says comes from that reading. **The author chose this book** (the Gospels in canonical order, `GOSPELS.md`) **and has not read this brief.** Where a section says “Proposed,” that is the drafter's decision, not a fact from a source, and the author can overturn it.

Related files: `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` at the top of the repository (“The Gospels” among them); `content/volumes/new-testament/GOSPELS.md`, `stories.yaml` and `stories/`; `UNANSWERED.md` in this folder once there is something to put in it. Theme candidates are not collected book by book for the Gospels (`GOSPELS.md`).

## What the author has decided

- **The four Gospels' shared decisions** are in `GOSPELS.md`, except two rules the writer, the checker and the reviewer must have in front of them:
  - **Talmage is opened last** (2026-11-21). *Jesus the Christ* follows the Gospel story in order, so a writer who opens it early finds an answer to nearly every question and looks no further. List the reader's questions first, look for each answer in the sources that belong to it (the Joseph Smith Translation, the Greek, conference talks, the Religious Studies Center's studies of the passage, the Bible Dictionary), and only then open Talmage, for the questions still unanswered and for what he says that the others do not. The same holds for every work that covers every verse in order: **Ellicott, the *Cambridge Bible*, the *Expositor's Greek Testament*, Meyer, Huntsman and Hatch's chapters for the last week (*Greater Love Hath No Man*), and Huntsman's translation of John**, whose headings would hand a writer an outline.
  - **A source is cited for what it adds** (2026-11-21). Where one source already answers the note's question and a second only agrees, the second is not cited. This holds for every source, not Talmage alone. The checker reports each note where a citation adds nothing the note's other sources don't already give, and the reviewer decides; it is a judgment on the note, never a count.
- **The Church's teaching manuals are finders, never cited** (`STANDARDS.md` §1): *Come, Follow Me*, *Scripture Helps: New Testament*, the *New Testament Student Manual*. `footnotes.mjs` prints the *Scripture Helps* chapter for each John chapter; read what its endnotes cite and cite that. A KnoWhy that quotes a manual (`sc-knowhy-809` quotes the Doctrine and Covenants teacher's manual) is cited only for its own words.

## What a chapter is for here

The author's purpose for the Gospels: “the purpose is to anticipate questions a reader might have, and answer those questions” (`GOSPELS.md`). Read the chapter, write down what a careful reader would stop and ask, and answer that from sources read in the session (`STANDARDS.md` §3). A note exists because a reader would ask; a source's having something to say is not a reason for a note. A question no source answers goes in the writer's report and then `UNANSWERED.md`.

**Begin with the chapter's stories.** Run `node scripts/stories.mjs john <n>` and, for each story told in another Gospel, `node scripts/stories.mjs <id> --text`, and follow `GOSPELS.md`, “How a story's research is shared.” Where a research file exists, read it before you research, reopen with `source.mjs` any finding you use (it goes into your own ledger), and correct a finding you find wrong or thin, with your chapter in its `by`. The other Gospels' writers left in the files' `open` lists about forty questions for John (`grep -A15 '^open:' content/volumes/new-testament/stories/*.yaml | grep John`), most of them on 13 and 18–21: the washing of feet in place of the bread and cup, “I am he” and the falling back (18:5–6), “Behold the man!” (19:5), “It is finished” (19:30), the blood and water (19:34), “Touch me not” (20:17), “Receive ye the Holy Ghost” (20:22), Thomas, the three questions to Peter (21:15–17). Those are John's first questions. No Gospel comes after John, so add to `findings` only what corrects or completes a file.

**What the stories show for John** (`stories.mjs` run for all 21 chapters): 50 stories fall in John's chapters. 23 have a research file; 27 are told only in John, and they hold about 697 of its 879 verses (the Bible Dictionary: John is “about 92 percent exclusive,” `bd-gospels` [5]).

- **Told only in John, so the chapter's ledger is its research:** the prologue and the first disciples (1:1–14, 35–51); chapters 2, 3, 5, 7, 9, 10 and 14–17 whole; the woman at the well and the nobleman's son (4:3–42, 46–54); the bread of life (6:22–71); all of 8; Lazarus and the council (11); the Greeks and the hour (12:20–50); the washing of feet (13:4–30); Thomas (20:24–31); chapter 21. 1:34, 4:45 and 16:31–33 are in no story.
- **Shared, with a file:** the Baptist and the baptism (1:15–33); the return to Galilee (4:43–44); the feeding and the walking on the sea (6:1–21); the anointing and the entry (12:1–19); the supper, the plot, the new commandment and Peter's denial foretold (13); all of 18 and 19; the tomb, Mary Magdalene and the appearance to the disciples (20:1–23). John's own details inside them (the lad's barley loaves, the falling back, Pilate's dialogues, the mother at the cross, the spear) are in the files only as open questions, if at all.
- **Not the same story:** the first cleansing of the temple (2:13–25) is its own event in the author's chronology, not the last week's; the nobleman's son (4:46–54) is not the centurion's servant, though the footnote to Matt. 8:6 points there. A note says what John says, and that the others tell of a cleansing at the end, without settling whether there were two.

Questions a reader of John asks again and again (a start, not a limit): *Why is John so different?* (John “was writing to members of the Church who already had basic information about the Lord,” `bd-gospels` [3]; a note says what this passage adds, not what the whole Gospel is). *Who are “the Jews” here?* *What does “I am” mean? Why “sign”?* (below). *What did Joseph Smith change, and teach from it?* (“The Joseph Smith Translation in John,” “Restoration connections”). *What does this word mean?* The footnotes gloss many (“GR”: 1:12 “power” is “authority, right, privilege”); a note goes further only where the question needs more.

**Where answers come from** is `STANDARDS.md` §1's order. John is well served by Latter-day Saint studies of single passages (named under each question), by Restoration scripture that quotes and expands it, and by Joseph Smith: “From the beginning of his ministry to the end of his life, the Gospel of John played a highly significant role for Joseph Smith” (`frederick-life-eternal` [7]). **Don't always go to the same source first.** The entries quoted here are leads for the questions they are quoted under.

## Divisions

John's own shape. Huntsman's outline: a prologue (1:1–51); the “Book of Signs” (2:1–11:54), in first signs and dialogues (2:1–4:54), “signs and discourses in the context of Jewish feasts” (5:1–10:42) and Lazarus (11); the “Book of Glory” (12:1–20:31), in the scene set for the Passion (11:55–12:50), the supper and farewell discourses (13–17), the crucifixion and burial (18–19) and the resurrection (20); and an epilogue (21) (`huntsman-gospel-john` [27]–[45]). The first signs are “framed by miracles performed in the Galilean town of Cana” ([47]). His translation sets 11:1–12:11 apart as “The Bethany Cycle” (`huntsman-john-rendition`, PDF p. 5). A reading in *BYU Studies* takes 13–17 as one chiasm, from the foot washing to the prayer, with “abide” at its center (15:1–17) (`brouwer-farewell` [6]). The epilogue “resumes a narrative that seemed to end” at 20:30–31 (`huntsman-gospel-john` [49]).

**Verdict: eight divisions of whole chapters.** The prologue and the epilogue stand alone, as single chapters, because the Gospel is built that way: chapter 1 holds the book's key (the Word, the Lamb, “come and see”), and 20:30–31 closes the Gospel before chapter 21. The turn at 12:23 (“The hour is come”) cannot open a division, and the Bethany cycle runs into chapter 12, so 11 and 12 go together and the blurb ends on the hour, as Luke's `lukeprep` ends on 9:51. Chapters 5–10 are one movement, the feasts (Sabbath, Passover, Tabernacles, Dedication). The upper room (13–17) is one evening and one discourse; splitting it would cut the farewell from the supper. Chapter 20 ends with the statement of purpose that names its division. The keys are used by no other book (checked against every `book.yaml`); the blurbs are in `book.yaml`.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks were copied from the page named. The rest is the drafter's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **Long chapters: save in pieces.** Matthew's writers of long chapters were cut off with “Output blocked by content filtering policy” before saving; a writer told to save in pieces finished at the first try (`GOSPELS.md`, “State”). **Every writer of John 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 11 and 12 (43 verses or more; 6 has 71 and 8 has 59) saves the chapter as a small skeleton first (the YAML frame, `setting`, section titles, no notes), then adds two or three notes at a time by Edit.** The coordinator gives this in the workflow's `focus` for those chapters; any writer may do it for any chapter.
- **John's own wording is the text.** A note quotes the verse it sits on; another Gospel's wording comes in only where the difference is the question (`GOSPELS.md`). John's paired terms (“life and death, light and darkness, spirit and flesh,” `huntsman-gospel-john` [26]) and repeated words (“verily, verily,” “abide,” “glorify,” “the world”) are noticed only where the verse turns on them.
- **The text: King James and the Greek.** The text is the King James Version, whose Greek is the Textus Receptus (`greek.mjs john <c>:<v>`; lexicon `thayer`). Modern editions differ in two places a reader asks about, 5:3b–4 and 7:53–8:11 (below): the SBL text on Bible Hub prints neither, the Nestle text of 1904 prints 5:4, Westcott and Hort print 8:1 (`biblehub.com/texts/john/5-4.htm`, `…/8-1.htm`). **Proposed:** a difference is mentioned only where the reader of the King James verse would ask, as what the manuscripts show, never as a claim that the verse is not scripture, and from a source that discusses it (rule 6) or the editions side by side.
- **Dates and places.** The author's chronology is the order the site follows (`GOSPELS.md`). John names three Passovers (2:13; 6:4; 11:55) and a feast (5:1) “often taken to be a Passover,” so the ministry “lasts two or three years” (`huntsman-gospel-john` [47]); a note may say that is John's frame. A year is given only from a source read, as that source's. For the day of the crucifixion, see below.
- **“The Jews” in John: the book's most sensitive convention.** The phrase is in 64 verses. What the studies say: it “occurs frequently in John, often negatively. In these instances, it appears to refer to either the Jewish leadership or a specific group of opponents to Jesus” (`matson-fourth-gospel` [23]); “even this early in John's Gospel, the term ‘Jews’ has more to do with a lack of receptivity to Jesus than it does with national or religious identity” (Strathearn, after Bassler, `strathearn-johannine-dialogues` [12]); Judd's note takes it “typically” as “the Jewish leaders” (`judd-other-sheep` [121]); in John 6 the audience changes from “the multitude” to “a new, more hostile audience” called *hoi Ioudaioi*, and John's use of the term “is problematic and has been the focus of much debate” (`huntsman-bread-of-life` [35], [72]). Many scholars read “put out of the synagogue” (9:22) as reflecting Jewish Christians “expelled from their synagogues later in the century” (`huntsman-gospel-john` [22]): their reading, attributed. John also uses the word without hostility: “salvation is of the Jews” (4:22), and many of “the Jews” believe (11:45; 12:11). The Book of Mormon speaks of the Lord “lifted up by the Jews” ([[3 Ne. 28:6]]) and has Jacob say “they shall crucify him” ([[2 Ne. 10:3]]), and the Lord rebukes the Gentiles who “have cursed” the Jews, “mine ancient covenant people,” “and have hated them” ([[2 Ne. 29:5]]). **Proposed, the rule for every chapter** (the plain words' part is `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` N10, and stands until the author answers): a note names the group the verse means, from the context and a source read: the temple authorities, the Pharisees, the council, the crowd at that feast, the people of Judea, or Jews who believed. Where “the Jews” are the leaders, the plain words say so (“the Jewish leaders,” “the authorities in Jerusalem”), and a note explains why once in the chapter where the reader first asks. No note lets its wording pass from a group in a scene to the Jewish people, then or now, and none says or implies that the Jewish people bear the guilt of the crucifixion (the rule of Matthew's brief on Matt. 27:25). Jesus, His mother and every disciple in the book were Jews; a note may say so where it helps. 2 Ne. 10:3 is quoted only where a verse needs it, with 2 Ne. 29:4–5 beside it.
- **The Pharisees, the chief priests and the council.** Name the group the verse names (Matthew's convention). John's Pharisees can put a man out of the synagogue (9:22; 12:42) and sit with the chief priests in council (7:32, 45; 11:47); Nicodemus is “a man of the Pharisees” and “a ruler of the Jews” (3:1). Who they were comes from `bd-pharisees` or a study read.
- **The “I am” sayings.** Seven with a predicate (bread, light, door, shepherd, resurrection, way, vine) and seven “absolute” (4:26; 6:20; 8:24, 28, 58; 13:19; 18:5), which recall the divine name of Exodus 3:14 in the Septuagint (`huntsman-gospel-john` [52]–[61]). The footnote at 8:58: “The term I Am used here in the Greek is identical with the Septuagint usage in Ex. 3:14 which identifies Jehovah”; the chapter heading reads “Before Abraham was I, Jehovah.” **Proposed:** a note on an absolute saying says what the Greek has where the King James adds “he” (4:26; 8:24, 28; 13:19; 18:5–6), from `greek.mjs` and the footnote; a predicated saying is explained for its image where it stands. No note lists all fourteen; that is a theme page's work.
- **Signs.** John's word is *sēmeion*, “sign,” where the Synoptics' usual word for a miracle is *dynamis* (`huntsman-gospel-john` [64]); John counts the first two (2:11; 4:54) and wrote the signs “that ye might believe” (20:30–31). Counts of seven differ (Huntsman's, [65]–[73], includes the walking on the water). **Proposed:** a note says what this sign shows about who Jesus is, from the text and a study of the passage (`van-dyke-miracles-john` for several), and does not number it.
- **The hour.** “Mine hour is not yet come” (2:4; the JST keeps it), “his hour was not yet come” (7:30; 8:20), “The hour is come” (12:23; 17:1), “his hour was come” (13:1). At Cana the words “point toward his final sacrifice” (`lane-word-made-flesh` [28]); 13:1 “establishes the emphasis of chapters 13–17” (`huntsman-lamb-of-god` [21]). **Proposed:** each note says what the hour is where it stands; no chapter makes an arc of it, since it is `GOSPELS.md`'s own example of a theme that waits for all four Gospels.
- **Titles of Jesus** (the Word, the Lamb of God, the Son of man, the King of Israel, Rabboni). A note says what a title means where it stands, from a source; John's Christology as a whole is a scholar's reading, attributed (`strathearn-johannine-dialogues` [6]; `lefevre-christology-jst` [107]; `huntsman-gospel-john` [50]–[62]).
- **Talmage.** Opened last, cited for what it adds (above). Find the chapter on the contents page; `talmage-jesus-christ-<chapter>` keys exist for 7–11, 13–24, 26, 27, 29–35 and 37.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each. The writer reads the source again before writing the note and quotes from the page.

- **Who wrote it, and the beloved disciple ([[John 13:23]]; [[John 21:20–24]]).** The Bible Dictionary: John, son of Zebedee, “In his own Gospel he refers to himself as ‘the disciple whom Jesus loved’” (`bd-john` [1]). The Gospel is “formally anonymous” (`huntsman-gospel-john` [10]); Irenaeus and Clement named John ([14]); the identification “is still a real possibility, one that Latter-day revelation and tradition support,” and D&C 7:1–3 “directly equates John with the ‘the disciple whom Jesus loved’” ([15], [19]). Judd: “these anonymous Christians possibly compiled and wrote down the recollections of John” (`judd-who-wrote-gospels` [37]). The JST titles the book “The Testimony of St. John”; “Joseph Smith named the Gospels of Matthew and John testimonies, but not the Gospels of Mark and Luke. We do not know why” (`jackson-jst-new-testament` [14]). **Proposed:** chapter 1's `setting` says once who wrote it as the Bible Dictionary and D&C 7 give it; the notes on 13:23, 19:26, 20:2 and 21:20–24 name the beloved disciple as John, with D&C 7. Theories of how it was composed are attributed, and only where a verse raises them (21:24's “we know”).
- **When, and beside the Synoptics.** John's record “is quite different from the other three in vocabulary, phraseology, and presentation of events” (`bd-gospels` [2]); it has no wilderness temptation, no Transfiguration, no mention “of true parables” ([4]). “Scholars frequently suggest a date in the A.D. 90s,” while the silence about Jerusalem's fall and the present tense of 5:2 “could be taken to suggest an earlier date” (`huntsman-gospel-john` [21]). **Proposed:** a date only as these give it, both views; nothing built on whether John knew the Synoptics; where John differs in order or detail, the note says what John says, that the others differ, and a reason only from a source.
- **The Word, and JST John 1:1–18.** JST 1:1: “In the beginning was the gospel preached through the Son. And the gospel was the word, and the word was with the Son, and the Son was with God, and the Son was of God” (`jst-john-1`). LeFevre: “the theme of the changes is making the identification of ‘the Word’ unambiguous: he is ‘the Son of God’” (`lefevre-christology-jst` [109]); JST 1:16 makes the fulness “immortality and eternal life, through his grace” ([116]). The Logos “is the equivalent of the Old Testament Hebrew term *daḇar*” and recalls the creation (`lane-word-made-flesh` [12]); John “seems to simply use biblical language that his audience would have understood” ([13]). The Lord: “in the beginning the Word was, for he was the Word, even the messenger of salvation” ([[D&C 93:8]]; Bowen reads it with Malachi 3:1, `bowen-messenger-salvation`, abstract read). “Dwelt” (1:14) is *eskēnōsen*, “pitched his tent” (`huntsman-gospel-john` [51]; `strongs-greek` already keys G4637). **Proposed:** the note on 1:1 explains “the Word” from Genesis 1 and the Old Testament's word of God, with D&C 93:8 as the Lord's gloss; a separate note gives the JST as Joseph Smith's inspired rendering, which explains the King James verse rather than replacing it. Greek philosophy only if the reader would ask, as Lane and Lane put it. JST verse numbers differ from 1:16 on; quote them as the appendix does.
- **“No man hath seen God at any time” ([[John 1:18]]).** JST 1:19: “And no man hath seen God at any time, except he hath borne record of the Son; for except it is through him no man can be saved.” The footnote gives D&C 67:11 (“no man has seen God at any time in the flesh, except quickened by the Spirit of God”) and 84:22. A KnoWhy calls the King James verse “a confusing teaching which contradicts a fair number of passages” (`sc-knowhy-628` [13]). JST 6:44 (appendix) bears on 6:46. **Proposed:** the note reads 1:18 with the JST and D&C 67:11, says that prophets have seen God (the writer reads Moses 1:2 and Joseph Smith—History 1:17 on the page), and argues from the Greek against neither.
- **“Born of water and of the Spirit” ([[John 3:3–8]]).** “Again” is *anōthen*, also “from above,” the word of 3:31 and 19:11; Nicodemus takes the first sense (`strathearn-johannine-dialogues` [16]); “to be born ‘from above’ is to be born of both water and the spirit” ([17]), and Strathearn quotes Joseph Smith, “baptism by water is but 1/2 a baptism” (read the report in *The Words of Joseph Smith* before citing it). Moses 6:59–60 adds “cleansed by blood” (`bradshaw-bowen-born-again`, abstract [30]). **Proposed:** the note says what *anōthen* holds, and reads water and Spirit as baptism and the gift of the Holy Ghost, as Restoration scripture does ([[Moses 6:59–60]]), with Joseph Smith's words as reported. No survey of other churches' readings.
- **“God is a Spirit” ([[John 4:24]]).** JST 4:26: “For unto such hath God promised his Spirit. And they who worship him, must worship in spirit and in truth” (`jst-john-4`). The footnote gives D&C 93:33 and 130:22, which the writer reads. **Proposed:** the note gives the JST as Joseph Smith's answer, says plainly that Restoration scripture teaches the Father has a body, and leaves the Greek alone unless a source read explains it.
- **The angel at Bethesda ([[John 5:3–4]]).** The Textus Receptus has 5:4; the SBL text and Westcott and Hort omit it (above). The Gospel Library has no footnote or JST change there. “A tradition of the day claimed that the waters in the pool possessed curative powers” (`van-dyke-miracles-john` [21]). No Latter-day Saint source read discusses the manuscripts of 5:4. **Proposed:** the note says what the people believed of the pool and that the man was healed by Jesus's word, not the water (5:8–9); it mentions the manuscripts only from a source that discusses them, or as the editions show them, and does not call the angel real or a superstition in the site's voice.
- **The resurrection of life and of damnation ([[John 5:28–29]]).** JST 5:29: “in the resurrection of the just … in the resurrection of the unjust.” “We came to the twenty-ninth verse of the fifth chapter of John, which was given unto us as follows” ([[D&C 76:15–17]]); “this caused us to marvel” (76:18). **Proposed:** the note quotes D&C 76:15–17 as what the verse opened in this dispensation and reads “damnation” with the JST's “unjust”; the kingdoms of D&C 76 are named and linked, not explained. 5:25 (the dead shall hear) is read with D&C 138 on the page.
- **The bread of life, and eating flesh ([[John 6:26–71]]).** The audience turns hostile at 6:41 (`huntsman-bread-of-life` [35]); “How can this man give us his flesh to eat?” “seems disingenuous,” and “drinketh my blood” sharpened the offense, given the law against consuming blood ([40]). The feeding “re-creates the table fellowship of the Passover meal” and recalls the manna; the crowd took Him for the prophet like Moses (`sc-knowhy-664` [6]). Elder Gerrit W. Gong reads the bread with the sacrament (`gong-bread-of-life` [21]). JST 6:40, 44, 54. The Lord at Bountiful: “He that eateth this bread eateth of my body to his soul” ([[3 Ne. 20:8]]). **Proposed:** the notes explain the metaphor from the discourse and its Passover setting (6:4), and connect it to the sacrament as Restoration scripture does (3 Ne. 18; 20:8), without calling John 6 the institution of the sacrament.
- **The woman taken in adultery ([[John 7:53–8:11]]).** As a Latter-day Saint author reports Metzger and Ehrman: the earliest Greek manuscript with the passage is Codex Bezae, of the fifth century; it is absent from many diverse manuscripts; its style differs from John's (`densley-adultery` [86], note 6). Wayment, quoted there: some manuscripts place it “at John 7:36, after John 21:25, or after Luke 21:38”; it “appears to have strong external support that it originated with Jesus” but “may not have originally been placed here” ([88], note 7). A KnoWhy: it “was not always located at its current place” is possible (`sc-knowhy-869`, note 7). Densley reads the scene as Jesus honoring the law's procedure: “He did not actually forgive the woman (John 8:11)” ([29]). JST 8:11: “And the woman glorified God from that hour, and believed on his name.” **Proposed:** chapter 8 treats the passage as scripture, as the Church's edition and the JST do. One note says once that early manuscripts lack it or place it elsewhere and that scholars, Latter-day Saints among them, take it as a true event of Jesus's life; the notes then answer what the reader asks (the law, the trap, “go, and sin no more”), with JST 8:11 for what became of her. Densley's reading is his; no note makes the story a teaching that sin does not matter.
- **“Before Abraham was, I am” ([[John 8:58]]).** The footnote and the heading (“The ‘I am’ sayings”); they took up stones (8:59), which shows how they heard it. **Proposed:** the note reads it as Jesus's claim to be Jehovah, from the footnote, the heading and Exodus 3:14 (link the site's note), and says why they took up stones. Premortal life in general is said at 9:2, not here.
- **The man born blind, and sin before birth ([[John 9:1–3]]).** The question assumes the man could have sinned before birth; Jesus answers, “Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents.” A KnoWhy: “Jesus did not challenge the disciples on the reality of a premortal life” (`sc-knowhy-808` [7], quoting S. Kent Brown: read Brown before citing him). The footnote gives TG “Man, Antemortal Existence of.” **Proposed:** the note says what the question assumed, that Jesus rejected this blindness as punishment, and that Restoration scripture teaches a premortal life ([[D&C 93:29]]; [[Abr. 3:22–23]], read on the page). It does not teach that illness or disability is punishment, nor that Jesus taught the disciples premortal life here.
- **Other sheep ([[John 10:16]]).** The Lord told the Nephites they were among the “other sheep” ([[3 Ne. 15:21]]; `sc-knowhy-207` [4]), and that His hearers “supposed it had been the Gentiles” (3 Ne. 15:22). “Many scholars have concluded that Jesus was referring to Gentiles” (`judd-other-sheep` [33]); “Scholars are divided on what John 10:16 actually means, so this is one occasion when the Book of Mormon can help readers to understand the New Testament” (`sc-knowhy-207` [5]). **Proposed:** the note gives the Lord's identification in 3 Nephi 15–16 as the answer, that the first hearers took Him to mean the Gentiles, and 3 Ne. 16:1–3 (still others) where it helps.
- **“Ye are gods” ([[John 10:34–36]]).** The site's Psalm 82 note on 82:6 already explains the psalm and the Savior's use of it (`peterson-ye-are-gods`, `bokovoy-ye-really-are-gods`, `frederick-ot-in-nt-gospels`); a KnoWhy sets it beside D&C 76:58 (`sc-knowhy-701` [4]–[6]). **Proposed:** the note answers the argument in John briefly (if scripture called men “gods,” how is the Son's claim blasphemy?) and links Psalm 82 for the rest.
- **The Comforter and the Second Comforter ([[John 14:16–26]]).** The chapter heading: “He promises the first and second Comforters.” Joseph Smith, 1839, reported by Willard Richards: the Holy Ghost “is the first Comforter”; the faithful may then “receive the other Comforter which the Lord hath promised the saints as is recorded in the testimony of St John in the XIV ch,” noting verses 16–18, 21 and 23; “Now what is this other Comforter? It is no more or less than the Lord Jesus Christ himself” (`jsp-discourse-1839-comforter`, pp. 19–21). Another report: “There is one Comforter [i.e. the Holy Ghost] & another Comforter to abide with you forever” (`ehat-cook-words-1839-08-a` [3]). D&C 130:3: the appearing in 14:23 “is a personal appearance.” D&C 88:3–4 uses the words for the Holy Spirit of promise: “another Comforter … which other Comforter is the same that I promised unto my disciples, as is recorded in the testimony of John.” Section 88 “takes the language of John 14 and expands upon it,” without contradicting it (`frederick-life-eternal` [46]). **Proposed:** the note on 14:16–17 says the Comforter is the Holy Ghost, whom 14:26 names; the note on 14:18–23 gives Joseph Smith's teaching that the Lord Himself is “the other Comforter,” as reported, with D&C 130:3; where the reader asks about D&C 88:3–4, the note quotes it and does not harmonize the two beyond what they say. Nothing is said of how or to whom the Second Comforter has come beyond what a prophet read on the page says.
- **“Many mansions” ([[John 14:2]]).** Joseph Smith, 11 June 1843, as reported: “Jesus said their were many mansions in my fathers Kingdom,” beside Paul's “three glories” (`ehat-cook-words-1843-06-11` [26]). The footnote gives D&C 59:2; 98:18 and TG “Telestial Glory.” **Proposed:** the note says what “mansions” meant in 1611 (from a lexicon or Thayer) and gives Joseph Smith's reading of them as kingdoms of glory, linking D&C 76; no map of heaven is built on the verse.
- **The intercessory prayer and oneness ([[John 17:3]]; [[John 17:20–23]]).** Joseph Smith, 16 June 1844, reported by Thomas Bullock: “I am agreed with the Far. & the Far. is a greed with me & we are agreed as one—the Greek shews that is shod. be agreed”; by George Laub: “that we might be one, or to Say be of one mind in the unity of the faith” (`ehat-cook-words-1844-06-16` [9], [14]). The footnotes give 4 Ne. 1:17, D&C 35:2 and 38:27 at 17:21, and D&C 132:24 at 17:3; 3 Ne. 19:23, 29 echo the prayer (read on the page). Temple readings: `sc-knowhy-674` and Hamblin's “Hidden Temple in John 17” (*Interpreter*, `hamblin-john-17`, not read); attributed. **Proposed:** the note on 17:21–23 says the Father and the Son are one as the disciples are to be one, in purpose and love, from the verse and Joseph Smith, with D&C 130:22 for their being distinct persons; it does not argue with creeds.
- **The day of the crucifixion ([[John 13:1]]; [[John 18:28]]; [[John 19:14]]; [[John 19:31]]).** In John the Passover begins at sunset after Jesus dies, “the preparation day was likely the day when the Passover was prepared,” while the Synoptics make the supper a Passover meal; Huntsman gives three explanations (John's timing is literary; the Synoptics' is; or Jesus kept the meal a day early) and finds John's “more historical verisimilitude” (`huntsman-gospel-john` [92]). A KnoWhy: Jesus “was killed at the same time the Passover lambs were being slaughtered” (`sc-knowhy-845` [6]); another reads the “high day” of 19:31 as a festival Sabbath that “most likely fell on Friday in AD 30,” so the Sabbath after the crucifixion may not have been Saturday (`sc-knowhy-300` [9]). The Synoptic side is in the Last Supper's story file. **Proposed:** the notes say what John says, that the Synoptics differ, and why the timing matters to John (the Lamb, 1:29; no bone broken, 19:36), with the explanations as explanations and no day or year settled in the site's voice.
- **“Touch me not” ([[John 20:17]]).** JST: “Hold me not.” The Greek is *mē mou haptou*; the JST clarifies “that the Greek word referred not just to touching but the more powerful clinging, grasping, gripping, or clutching” (`pierce-recognizing-christ` [13]), and Pierce reads it as the Lord's telling Mary He will not always be present to cling to (her reading). The story file leaves this to John, with Talmage 37 as a start; Talmage comes after the JST, the Greek and Pierce. **Proposed:** the note follows the JST and the Greek (do not hold on to Me, for I go to the Father), gives the verse's own reason, and says no more of the risen body than 20:27 and Luke 24:39 do; no rule about touching resurrected beings.
- **Thomas ([[John 20:24–29]]).** His “My Lord and my God” “echoes the description of the Divine Word from the prologue” (`huntsman-gospel-john` [48]); Wilcox's “Be Not Faithless, but Believing” (*Religious Educator* 25:2, 2024) is readable (not read). **Proposed:** the notes say what Thomas said and how the Lord answered, in John's words, without “doubting Thomas” in the site's voice; 20:29 is where the chapter reaches the reader, and 20:30–31 is the Gospel's statement of purpose.
- **John 21 and the disciple who tarries ([[John 21:20–23]]).** D&C 7 answers it: given when Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery asked “whether John, the beloved disciple, tarried in the flesh or had died,” “a translated version of the record made on parchment by John” (headnote); “thou shalt tarry until I come in my glory, and shalt prophesy before nations” (7:3). “John did not die but has been allowed to remain on the earth as a ministering servant until the time of the Lord's Second Coming” (`bd-john` [2]); the Three Nephites desired what “John, my beloved” desired ([[3 Ne. 28:6–8]]). Judd and Szink: 21:23's “yet Jesus said not unto him, He shall not die” “likely reflect[s] an editor's view of the speculation about John” (`judd-szink-dc7` [41]; the 1833 heading, “translated from parchment,” [35]; translated beings, [46]). **Proposed:** the note on 21:22–23 gives D&C 7 and 3 Ne. 28 as the Restoration's answer and the editor's-note reading as Judd and Szink's; the epilogue is scripture. Peter's three answers (21:15–17) are read against his three denials from the story file's leads. Where John has ministered since stays out unless a prophet read on the page says it (a KnoWhy quotes Joseph Smith in 1831 that John “was then among the Ten Tribes,” `sc-knowhy-809` [19]: read the original first; its Orthodox legends, [15]–[18], stay out).
- **D&C 93 and “the record of John.”** Which John is meant “is not always clear” (`sc-knowhy-809` [4]); Judd notes that 93:15 has John bear record at the baptism, which in the Gospel is the Baptist's witness (`judd-who-wrote-gospels` [20]); in D&C 93 “an assortment of verses originally located throughout the Gospel of John are reworked” so that the Son “received not of the fulness at first” (`frederick-dc-john` [40]). **Proposed:** a note on the prologue may say the Lord gave Joseph Smith part of “the record of John” (D&C 93:6–18) and quote what answers the verse; it does not settle which John, and says that sources differ.

## The Joseph Smith Translation in John

- **How it was done.** For Matthew 1 through John 5 Joseph Smith dictated the text in full; for the rest he “dictated chapter and verse numbers and then only the changed words” (`jackson-jst-new-testament` [11]). The manuscripts are New Testament Revision 1 and 2 (`jsp-nt1`, `jsp-nt2`); find John's pages there before quoting an unprinted reading.
- **How heavy it is.** Light, except chapter 1: “the already high Christology of John may have required fewer changes. Indeed, JST John includes very few christological changes” (`lefevre-christology-jst` [107]).
- **What the reader already has.** The appendix has entries for chapters 1, 4, 6, 13 and 14 only (`jst-john-<n>`, `…/scriptures/jst/jst-john/<n>?lang=eng`; all five read; the other sixteen return no page): 1:1–34 (the gospel preached through the Son; the Baptist “not that Elias who was to restore all things,” Jesus “that prophet, even Elias”) and 1:42 (Cephas “a seer, or a stone”); 4:1–4 (the Pharisees sought His death; He baptized fewer than His disciples, “for he suffered them for an example, preferring one another”) and 4:26; 6:44; 13:8–10 (the washing of feet was “the custom of the Jews under their law,” done “that the law might be fulfilled”); 14:30 (“the prince of darkness … hath no power over me”). The footnotes give the rest: the title; 2:1 (“third day of the week”), 2:4; 3:18, 27, 34, 36; 5:29, 35, 37; 6:26, 40, 54, 65; 7:24, 39; 8:11, 43, 47; 9:4, 32; 10:8; 11:2, 16, 17; 12:7; 13:19; 16:23; 19:17 (“burial” for “skull”); 20:1 (“two angels sitting thereon”), 20:17. Chapters 15, 17, 18 and 21 have none.
- **The Baptist as Elias (JST 1:21–28)** is explained from the JST and `bd-elias`, as Mark's and Luke's briefs explain the title.
- **How to use it.** Where the change answers the reader's question (`STANDARDS.md` §1). It is “not the official Bible” of the Church but “an invaluable aid to biblical interpretation” (`bd-jst`). Cite the change from the appendix or footnote (`lds-scriptures`); where a study discusses it, that is the source for what it means. Never argue from the Greek that a change is or is not what John wrote (Joseph Smith—Matthew's brief). The JST numbers verses differently in chapters 1, 3, 4 and 5; quote its numbers.
