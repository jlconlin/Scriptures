## What a chapter is for here

Micah is 105 verses in seven chapters of poetry: oracles of judgment and of hope, a lament, a lawsuit, a psalm. A reader meets famous lines in an unfamiliar setting and asks: *Who was Micah, and where is Moresheth? Why Samaria and Jerusalem together? What are the strange town names in 1:10–16 doing? Who are the people lying awake planning to take fields (2:1–2)? Who are the “heads of Jacob” who “eat the flesh of my people” (3:1–3)? Did Jerusalem fall when Micah said it would? Why are 4:1–3 the same as Isaiah 2, and who said it first? How can Zion go to Babylon (4:10) when Assyria rules? Who is the ruler from Bethlehem, and why does Matthew quote it differently? Who is the remnant that is dew and a lion? Why does the Lord put His people on trial before the mountains (6:1–2)? Did anyone offer a firstborn (6:7)? Why does the book end with “Who is a God like unto thee?”* A chapter answers those from the text first.

**Judgment, then hope, three times.** Every source read divides the book so (below). A chapter's `setting` says where it falls in the pattern; the `thread` of a judgment chapter says what the hope that follows it answers, and of a hope chapter what judgment it answers. The book's last word is mercy, and the chapters point to 7:18–20 rather than closing on the threats.

**Micah's complaint is injustice.** The Bible Dictionary names “the idolatry at the high places and also the oppression by the upper classes”; the Student Manual (printing Sperry) says Micah, unlike Isaiah, “was not so much concerned about his nation's political sins” but “his grievances were social in character.” Say what the text says the powerful were doing (fields, houses, bribes, false weights, prophets for hire) concretely, and let the text judge it; don't modernize it into a sermon on economics.

**The famous lines are three.** 4:1–3 (with Isaiah), 5:2 (with Matthew) and 6:8 (with Elder Renlund). Each is explained once, at its verse, and the rest of the book is given its due: the lament of 1:10–16, the false prophets of chapter 3, the lawsuit of chapter 6 and the psalm of chapter 7 are what a reader of Micah has not met before.

## Divisions

**Proposed: three divisions, chapters 1–3, 4–5 and 6–7** (`book.yaml`: `micahjudgment`, “Zion Shall Be Plowed”; `micahlastdays`, “In the Last Days”; `micahrequire`, “What Doth the Lord Require”). This is the Bible Dictionary's division (“(1) Micah 1–3, prophecies of judgment and ruin … (2) Micah 4–5, prophecies of restoration, with brilliant messianic promises … (3) Micah 6–7, prophecies of a mixed character”), *Scripture Helps*'s outline (paragraphs 7–9), and the one Pike says “many scholars suggest.” Another division is as well grounded, and chapter settings may mention it once: Spencer reads “three sequences (chapters 1–2, chapters 3–5, and chapters 6–7), each concluding with a reflection on the theme of the remnant,” and Correa divides the book into “three blocks, each beginning with a call to action (Micah 1:2; 3:1; 6:1)” (each opens with “Hear”). Pike: “There is some disagreement on the details of the structure of the book.” The Church's division is used for the book page because the reader of the Gospel Library meets it there; chapter 2's setting can say that 2:12–13, a promise in the middle of judgment, is where Spencer's first sequence ends. The keys repeat no other book's.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, which later scripture takes up which line) is the coordinator's direction of where to look, some of it from memory. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **When.** Micah 1:1: “in the days of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah.” *Scripture Helps*: “during the reigns of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah in the eighth century BC.” Pike: “from about the 730s to the 690s BC, although certitude about actual dates is not possible.” Van Dyke and Ogden give the reigns as Jotham “742–735 BC,” Ahaz “735–715 BC,” Hezekiah “715–687 BC.” The Bible Dictionary and Jeremiah 26:18 place him “under Hezekiah.” **Proposed: every chapter's `when` is “Micah prophesied under Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah of Judah, about 740–700 B.C.,”** with a ledger row from *Scripture Helps* or the Student Manual (Sperry: “approximately 740 B.C. to 697 B.C.”). A chapter whose oracle can be placed more closely says so in its setting only from a source (chapter 1: Samaria still standing, so before 722; Pierce dates the lament of 1:10–16 before Sennacherib's campaign of 701, “probably a few years before it occurred”).
- **Who and where.** “Micah the Morasthite” (1:1): of Moresheth-gath (1:14), “in the plain country of Judah” (Bible Dictionary), in the Shephelah, the low hills between the coastal plain and the Judean hills (Pierce; Van Dyke and Ogden: “about twenty miles southwest of Jerusalem, near the border between Judah and Philistia”). Pierce: “Most scholars agree that Moresheth-gath is Tell ej-Judeideh, northeast of Lachish.” Chadwick, in an RSC endnote, reads him instead as from Mareshah (1:15); give it once at most, as his. **There is no Bible Dictionary entry “Moresheth-gath” or “Ephratah”** (the addresses return the dictionary's index). Correa's suggestion that Micah may have had Philistine ancestry, “another foreign prophet like Samuel the Lamanite,” is not used. The name: the Student Manual (Sperry) and Keil, an abbreviation of Micaiah, “who is like unto Jehovah?”; At Jeremiah 26:18 the Hebrew is written “Micaiah” and read “Micah” (`hebrew.mjs jer 26:18`: *ketiv* and *qere*). Explained once, in chapter 1's setting; chapter 7 returns to it at 7:18 (below). Not the Micah of Judges 17–18, nor Micaiah son of Imlah (1 Kgs. 22): one clause, in chapter 1.
- **Samaria and Jerusalem; Assyria.** Micah “saw concerning Samaria and Jerusalem” (1:1); Van Dyke and Ogden: “Micah's call was specifically to the capital cities.” The fall of Samaria (722 or 721), Hezekiah's reign and Sennacherib's campaign of 701 (Lachish, Jerusalem spared) are carried by the volume guides: point to [Two Kingdoms and Assyria](/old-testament/guides/two-kingdoms-and-assyria/#rulers) and [A Timeline of the Old Testament](/old-testament/guides/israels-story/#two-kingdoms), and don't retell them. Isaiah 36–37 on the site covers 701 from Jerusalem.
- **Wordplay on names.** Micah 1:10–16 and 7:18 turn on the sound of names, which the English hides. The Church's footnote at 1:10 (“Aphrah”) says: “Each of the cities named in vv. 10–16 will meet a fate appropriate to the meaning of its name.” This is where the Hebrew pays (`STANDARDS.md` §3): one Language note on the lament as a whole in chapter 1, from Pierce (“Micah employs paronomasia based on the names of towns that he is lamenting”) and Bowen, with the plainest pairs (Beth-le-aphrah, “House of Dust,” told to roll in the dust; Achzib and *ʾakzāb*, a lie; Mareshah and *yôrēš*), and two corrections to the King James taken from Pierce, each as his: at 1:15 the word rendered “heir” is “better translated as ‘conqueror’ or ‘plunderer,’” and at 1:16 the “eagle” (*nešer*) “would be better translated as ‘vulture.’” The note does not go town by town; where the towns were is for the archaeology Pierce gives, a sentence, and Pierce says some identifications are tentative and some names may be chosen for the pun. 7:18's wordplay on Micah's own name is at 7:18 (below).
- **The remnant.** A word that runs through the book: 2:12; 4:7; 5:3, 7, 8; 7:18 (*Scripture Helps* endnote 19; Pike). Explained once, at 2:12, from Pike (the Old Testament remnant is the “part of a nation … which survives a divine catastrophe”) and Spencer; 4:7, 5:7–8 and 7:18 point back. The Church's footnote at 2:12 is “TG Israel, Remnant of.” Spencer's reading that for Micah “Judah itself comes eventually to serve as a kind of remnant of Israel” is his.
- **“In the last days,” Zion and the mountain of the Lord's house (4:1–2).** Already explained on the site at `[[Isa. 2:2]]` and `[[Isa. 2:3]]`. Chapter 4's notes link them and add only what is Micah's own: the placement after 3:12 (the temple mount “plowed as a field” and then “established in the top of the mountains”: Spencer, “In the book of Micah, this vision follows prophecies of destruction”), and 4:4–5, which Isaiah 2 lacks. *Scripture Helps* (paragraph 10) itself points to its Isaiah 2 sections. President Harold B. Lee's reading of “out of Zion shall go forth the law” (the Student Manual, 11-9, quoting a 1945 dedicatory prayer) is given once at most, as his, and only if `[[Isa. 2:3]]` does not already carry it.
- **Verse numbers.** **The Hebrew chapters differ in chapters 4 and 5.** King James 5:1 is Hebrew 4:14, and King James 5:2–15 is Hebrew 5:1–14 (`hebrew.mjs` prints both). A note quoting a translation or a lexicon that numbers by the Hebrew gives the King James number. “Beth-lehem” is spelled with a hyphen in the Old Testament edition (5:2), “Bethlehem” in Matthew.
- **Other books on the site.** Link, and don't say who borrowed from whom except as a source read says. The Isaiah parallels *Scripture Helps* names (endnote 3): 2:12 and `[[Isa. 10:20–22]]`; 3:9–12 and `[[Isa. 1:23]]`; 4:1–3 and `[[Isa. 2:2–4]]`. Also 1:4 and `[[Isa. 64:3]]` (the footnote); 2:1–2 and `[[Isa. 5:8]]` (the footnote); 6:2 and `[[Hosea 4:1]]` (the footnote: the Lord's “controversy”); 6:6–8 with `[[Hosea 6:6]]`, `[[Amos 5:21–24]]`, `[[Isa. 1:11]]` and `[[1 Sam. 15:22]]` (the site's note on `[[Isa. 1:11]]` sets them together: link, don't list them again; the Student Manual, 11-13, prints Sperry setting 6:8 beside the two great commandments, as Elder Renlund does); 7:6 and `[[Matt. 10:35–36]]` (the footnote: the Saviour takes up Micah's words); 7:20 and `[[Luke 1:55]]`, `[[Luke 1:72]]` (the footnote: Mary and Zacharias echo the oath to Abraham), and `[[2 Ne. 29:14]]` (the footnote; the institute manual points to it for 7:20).
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** No change in Micah (the footnotes list none). The one JST reading that touches the book is **JST Matthew 3:4–6** at Matthew 2:4–6, Herod's question and the scribes' answer from Micah 5:2 (the footnote at 5:2: “Matt. 2:6; JST Matt. 3:6 (Appendix)”). Explained at 5:2 (below).
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports what runs across the book; the coordinator adds it to `THEME-CANDIDATES.md` (not yet created). Leads from the sources, not choices: the remnant (Spencer, Pike); justice and false prophets; the Lord's mercy (7:18–20 and 6:8).

**Habits to avoid** are listed in Amos's brief and hold here. In Micah especially: **don't explain the Isaiah and Matthew passages again** where the site already has them; and **don't make a verse of judgment say more than it says about modern society**: the Student Manual's “you will find his message remarkably up-to-date” (11-1) is not the site's voice.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that seven chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-23.

- **Is the whole book Micah's? (chapter 1's and chapter 4's settings).** The Church's sources speak of the book as Micah's prophecy and do not raise the question. Pike (RSC, 2022): “The book of Micah likely reached its final form after Micah’s death, and there is real diversity of opinion as to when that final form occurred.” Correa (Scripture Central) on 4:10, which names Babylon: “Whether a prophecy or a later addition, the text speaks of the exile to Babylon.” The Student Manual (11-10) reads 4:10 as prophecy: “This prophecy is amazing because Assyria was mistress of the world in Micah’s day, Babylon being only a province of Assyria.” Keil, last and older, says the book's heading is genuine and “confirmed by the contents of the book.” Spencer, against the view that the book is loosely gathered: “the book of Micah reads as rather tightly ordered.” **Proposed** (by analogy with the author's answer for Jonah, JN1: say what the sources establish, and no more): chapter 1's setting says the book is “the word of the LORD that came to Micah” (1:1) in the days of three kings, and, in one sentence, that scholars, including Latter-day Saint scholars, think the book reached its final form after Micah's death and differ on when (Pike). Chapter 4's note on 4:10 reads it as the text does, a prophecy of Babylon, from the Student Manual; it may add that some read the line as later (Correa), as a reading, once. No chapter assigns verses to a later hand.
- **Micah 4:1–3 and Isaiah 2:2–4: who said it first?** The sources differ, and **the site does not decide.** *Scripture Helps* (endnote 3): “It is unclear whether Micah was quoting from Isaiah, Isaiah was quoting from Micah, or both were quoting from a different source.” Chadwick (RSC): “Micah seems clearly to be repeating the chief oracle of his more famous prophet contemporary, Isaiah.” Keil: “The originality of the passage in Micah is open to no question.” The Interpreter Foundation's study aid (2022): “they may have both been independently inspired by the Lord in recording these prophecies.” Spencer (endnote 25): scholars “have long debated which prophet borrows from which,” and there is “a kind of consensus that there are more interesting questions to address concerning the place of the prophecy in each of the two books.” **The site's note on `[[Isa. 2:4]]` already gives *Scripture Helps*'s three possibilities and Chadwick's reading.** **Proposed:** chapter 4's note on 4:1 says the passage is nearly the same as Isaiah 2:2–4, links the Isaiah notes, does not repeat who-borrowed-from-whom (it points to `[[Isa. 2:4]]`), and turns to Spencer's question: what the vision does here, right after Zion is “plowed as a field” (3:12). The differences worth a sentence: Micah has 4:4 (“every man under his vine and under his fig tree”; already noted at `[[Isa. 2:4]]`, so point there) and 4:5. Nephi quotes the passage as Isaiah's (`[[2 Ne. 12:2–4]]`; Pike's endnote 15): one clause, no more.
- **The ruler from Bethlehem (5:2) and Matthew 2:6.** *Scripture Helps*: “This is the only prophecy in the Old Testament that specifically names the birthplace of the Messiah,” used by the chief priests and scribes when Herod asked; and (paragraph 16) “Ironically, this same prophecy was used by some later in the New Testament to incorrectly argue that Jesus was not the Messiah” (`[[John 7:40–43]]`; Matson, RSC, whom *Scripture Helps* cites, reads the passage). The Bible Dictionary and the Guide say the same of Bethlehem; *Scripture Helps* endnote 13 and the Student Manual (11-11) say “Ephratah” distinguishes the Bethlehem of Judah from the one in Zebulun (`bd-bethlehem` lists both). The Guide: Bethlehem “means ‘house of bread’; it is also called Ephrath, meaning ‘fruitful.’” Bethlehem was David's town (*Scripture Helps* endnote 13; `[[1 Sam. 16:1]]`, the footnote): the ruler comes from where David came from. Matthew's wording differs from Micah's: Micah has “though thou be little,” Matthew “art not the least among the princes of Juda” (`[[Matt. 2:6]]`). The Joseph Smith Translation (JST Matt. 3:4–6, in the appendix) makes the scribes say “It is written by the prophets” and reads “in thee shall be born a prince, which art not the least among the princes of Judea; for out of thee shall come the Messiah, who shall save my people Israel.” **Proposed:** one note at “Beth-lehem Ephratah” (what and where; little among the clans of Judah; David's town) and one Witness of Christ note at “whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting”: the fulfilment in Matthew and Luke, the difference in Matthew's wording as Matthew's (a reader comparing the two will ask; a source must explain the difference, or the note only states it), and the JST, which answers the question a reader of Matthew asks (where is it written?) and belongs in the note. The context of 5:1–6 (a besieged city, a judge struck on the cheek, Assyria) is in the chapter's setting: Correa notes that the ruler of 5:5–6 delivers from Assyria, and that “in the first century Jesus did not participate in any violent defense of Judah”; the Student Manual (11-12, printing Keil) reads Assyria as “a type [symbol] of the nations of the world.” Give the plain reading first, then the fulfilment in Christ, as the Church reads it. **“From everlasting.”** The Hebrew is *miqqedem mîmê ʿôlām* (`hebrew.mjs micah 5:2`); the lexicon gives *môṣāʾôt* “origin, source.” Correa (endnote 17) says the word “conveys the idea of unknown time”; that is his. A Latter-day Saint reader will hear Christ's premortal existence: say so only from a Church source that reads the verse so (the footnote at 5:2 is “TG Immortality”); if none is found, the note says what the words say and lets the reader hear it.
- **The remnant as dew and lion; the threshing ox (4:11–13; 5:7–15); the Saviour's use in 3 Nephi.** The images are violent, and the Saviour applied them to the last days. *Scripture Helps*: 4:11–13 shows “His people would triumph over their enemies as easily as an ox threshes grain,” and both passages “likely ha[ve] multiple fulfillments” (paragraphs 13 and 18). Pike reads the Saviour's use as “not as a correction of Micah’s words but rather as a focused application of Micah 5:8–9 to a different remnant of the greater ‘house of Jacob’”; the conditional “if the Gentiles do not repent” (`[[3 Ne. 20:15]]`) is His addition (Pike). “Consecrate” at 4:13 is *ḥ-r-m*, “now often translated ‘devote,’ as in devoting something to the Lord by destroying it” (Pike; the root of Jericho's “accursed thing”): a Language note at 4:13, since “consecrate their gain” misleads. The dew of 5:7: “Most commentators have assumed that this image is a positive one,” but Spencer, after Hillers, reads it with the lion as judgment; give both as readings. **Proposed:** each image is first explained as Micah's hearers would hear it (Pike on lions and threshing, as his); then one Related Scriptures note at 4:13 and one at 5:8 say that the resurrected Saviour quoted these verses to the Nephites, in what order and with what added (Pike's chart: 5:8 at `[[3 Ne. 16:14–15]]`; 5:8–9 at `[[3 Ne. 20:16–17]]`; 4:12–13 at `[[3 Ne. 20:18–19]]`; 5:8–15 at `[[3 Ne. 21:12–18]]` and 21; 5:8 at `[[Morm. 5:24]]`), and what He made of them: the remnant's power, and the Gentiles who repent “numbered among this the remnant of Jacob” (*Scripture Helps*, from `[[3 Ne. 21:22]]`). KnoWhy #214 reads His use of Micah and Isaiah as about “God’s covenants with his people”: as its reading. Why He did not name Micah, and whether Micah was on the brass plates: Pike, “I think it likely that at least some of the writings of Micah were on the brass plates,” and the question “cannot presently be answered and in some ways is a nonissue”: one sentence, in chapter 5's `explore` or the note at 5:8, if at all. The site does not describe the destruction of the Gentiles beyond what the text says (`STANDARDS.md` §3, graphic passages).
- **What the Lord requires (6:6–8).** The verse everyone knows, in a lawsuit few have read. The Lord calls the mountains as witnesses (6:1–2; the footnote at 6:2: “controversy,” `[[Hosea 4:1]]`); the institute manual: the chapter “describes an imagined conversation between the Lord and the people of Judah,” and Pierce reads the book's legal words as a “prophetic lawsuit by Jehovah against Israel,” whose climax is 6:8. The site's Hebrew-poetry guide already names the form ([How the prophets speak](/old-testament/guides/hebrew-poetry/#prophets)): point there. Elder Renlund (quoted once, at 6:8): the offerings of 6:7 are Micah's satire (“Micah satirically wondered whether even exorbitant offerings might be enough to compensate for sin”), and “Salvation is not earned”; the three requirements are “three interconnected elements.” “Mercy” is *ḥesed* (`hebrew.mjs micah 6:8`; also 7:18, 20): the site's note on `[[Jonah 2:8]]` explains the word, and `[[Hosea 6:6]]` (“Not sacrifice, or more than sacrifice?”) the contrast with sacrifice; point there, don't explain them again. “Shall I give my firstborn” (6:7): a reader will ask whether Israel did. Say only what a source read says (Correa: “The practice of offering a firstborn may have been an ancient practice in Israel and was specifically condemned by the Abraham and Isaac story”; the site's notes on child sacrifice in Jeremiah 7 and 19 or 2 Kings 16:3 may already answer: check with `occurrences.mjs all "firstborn" --site all` and link). **Proposed:** the note on 6:8 does not say the verse replaces covenants or ordinances; Elder Renlund's reading ties walking humbly to making and keeping covenants, and the note follows him.
- **Prophets for hire and the true prophet (3:5–8; 2:6, 11).** Micah sets himself against prophets who “bite with their teeth, and cry, Peace” (3:5; the site's `[[Ezek. 13:19]]` already quotes it) and who “divine for money” (3:11), and says “I am full of power by the spirit of the LORD” (3:8; the footnote: “TG Priesthood, Power of”; “God, Spirit of; Holy Ghost, Mission of”). The footnote at 3:11 is “TG Priestcraft,” and the Interpreter study aid calls it “one form of ‘priestcraft’ (as defined in 2 Nephi 26:29)”: link `[[2 Ne. 26:29]]` once. “Is not the LORD among us? none evil can come upon us” (3:11) is the false security that Jeremiah 7 attacks a century later; the site's Jeremiah notes may already make the point (check and link).
- **Jeremiah 26:18 and whether Jerusalem fell (3:12).** Micah said Zion would “be plowed as a field” (3:12); Jerusalem was spared in 701, and the elders of Jeremiah's day cited Micah as a prophet whose warning Hezekiah heeded, so that “the LORD repented him of the evil” (`[[Jer. 26:18–19]]`). *Scripture Helps*: “His prophecies concerning the destruction of Jerusalem were remembered a century later during the time of Jeremiah.” Pike calls Jeremiah 26:18 the place “officials in the kingdom of Judah referenced Micah, who lived and prophesied about a century earlier.” **The site's note on `[[Jer. 26:18]]` (“Micah and Hezekiah”) already explains the quotation, the JST of Jeremiah 26:19 and the reading that the elders “combined” Micah's words with Hezekiah's prayer.** **Proposed:** chapter 3's note on 3:12 says what the verse says (the footnote: “the house,” “IE the temple”), that it is the only prophecy of one prophet quoted word for word by name in another prophet's book (check before claiming: if no source says so, leave the superlative out), and that a warning heeded was postponed, not falsified: a conditional warning, as Jeremiah 26:19 reads it and as `[[Jer. 18:7–10]]` states; then links Jeremiah 26:18 for the rest. The city fell to Babylon in 586; one clause.
- **“Who is a God like unto thee” (7:18–20).** Bowen: “Micah 7:18 employs a wordplay on the name Micah as a sign of mercy”; the name means “who is like…?” (Student Manual, Keil, Correa; the Interpreter study aid: “Who is like unto Jehovah?” or “Who is like God?”). Correa: the prophet's name and the closing question “serve as bookends.” This is the one place in chapter 7 where the Hebrew pays: a note at 7:18, from Bowen. *Come, Follow Me* (2026) takes its lesson title from the verse (“He delighteth in mercy”) and says of these prophets “They weren’t just prophets of doom—they were prophets of deliverance.” 7:19 (“cast all their sins into the depths of the sea”) is quoted by Elder Renlund (chapter 6's note points here) and already noted at `[[Isa. 38:17]]`: link. 7:20 and the oath to Abraham: the footnotes give `[[Luke 1:55]]` and `[[2 Ne. 29:14]]`; a Related Scriptures note, once.
- **Micah and the Book of Mormon beyond 3 Nephi.** Pike, agreeing with Thompson, says the footnote references to Micah earlier in the Book of Mormon are not “explicit references to words uttered by Micah.” So the footnotes to Alma, Helaman and Mosiah at 2:7, 3:4, 3:8 and elsewhere are cross-references only; a note is not built on one.
