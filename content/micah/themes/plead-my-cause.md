Micah is a courtroom book. It opens by calling the whole earth to order and naming the Lord as the witness for the prosecution: “Hear, all ye people; hearken, O earth… and let the Lord GOD be witness against you” ([[Micah 1:2]]). Its rulers are men who should “know judgment” and instead “abhor judgment, and pervert all equity” ([[Micah 3:1]]; [[Micah 3:9]]). Its sixth chapter is a trial in form, with the mountains summoned as witnesses: “the LORD hath a controversy with his people, and he will plead with Israel” ([[Micah 6:2]]). George Pierce lists the legal words running through the book, “justice,” “judgment,” “judge,” “witness,” “indictment,”[@pierce-micah] and Dana Pike calls chapters 6 and 7 “a covenant lawsuit,” using the Hebrew term for it, *rîb*.[@pike-micah]

Then, near the end, a fallen speaker says something that ought to stop a reader who has followed the trial. “I will bear the indignation of the LORD, because I have sinned against him, until he plead my cause, and execute judgment for me” ([[Micah 7:9]]). “Plead my cause” is the word behind “controversy” in 6:2, *rîb*; the “plead” of 6:2 itself is another verb.[@oshb-wlc] The Lord who had a *rîb* with Israel will now plead a *rîb* for her, and she has just confessed to sinning against Him.

My argument is that this is not an accident of vocabulary but the shape of the book. Micah shows the Lord bringing His case as the injured party and refusing to answer those who presume on Him. When the accused finally answers, and answers with a confession instead of a charge, He takes up her cause as her advocate. The Judge becomes the Advocate, and the book shows exactly what stood between the two roles. The verse itself is about Israel and her enemies, and I will keep it so. But Isaiah's Servant uses the same courtroom words, and Paul gives the Servant's challenge to every believer. And the role Micah shows is one the Restoration names: the Judge of all is the Advocate of all who confess.

## The Lord's case

Begin with the trial as Micah lays it out. The Lord is witness against the people in 1:2. In chapter 3 the prophet turns on the rulers, and the word that defines them is *mišpāṭ*, judgment or justice: it is theirs to know it, and they hate it ([[Micah 3:1]]; [[Micah 3:9]]). So when trouble comes, “they cry unto the LORD, but he will not hear them: he will even hide his face from them at that time” ([[Micah 3:4]]). The Hebrew is more exact than “hear.” The verb is *ʿānâ*, to answer, the word the lexicon lists for God “answering (graciously)”;[@bdb] here it is negated.[@oshb-wlc] They will cry and He will not answer.

The refusal is not arbitrary and it is not final. The verse ties it to their conduct, “as they have behaved themselves ill,” and to “that time.” The Church's footnote sends the reader to King Noah's priests, of whom the Lord said, “except they repent in sackcloth and ashes, and cry mightily to the Lord their God, I will not hear their prayers” ([[Mosiah 11:25]]).[@lds-scriptures] Silence toward the unrepentant is conditional silence. And Micah shows what these men were doing while they cried: they “lean upon the LORD, and say, Is not the LORD among us? none evil can come upon us” ([[Micah 3:11]]). They presumed on His presence while refusing His justice. That is the cry He will not answer.

Chapter 6 brings the case to court. “Arise, contend thou before the mountains… Hear ye, O mountains, the LORD's controversy, and ye strong foundations of the earth: for the LORD hath a controversy with his people” ([[Micah 6:1–2]]). The mountains are the witnesses, Keil says, summoned “to bear witness on behalf of the Lord, and against Israel.”[@keil-micah] The Church's institute manual calls the chapter “an imagined conversation between the Lord and the people of Judah.”[@ot-institute-manual-47] The site's guide to Hebrew poetry explains the form ([How the prophets speak](/old-testament/guides/hebrew-poetry/#prophets)), and the note on [Hosea 4:1](/hosea/4/) shows the same word opening Hosea's case. The *rîb* is the Lord's. He is the plaintiff. Israel is the defendant.

## Answer against me

Now watch what the plaintiff does. He does not begin with the charge. He asks the defendant to charge Him. “O my people, what have I done unto thee? and wherein have I wearied thee? testify against me” ([[Micah 6:3]]).

“Testify against me” is *ʿănēh bî*, answer against me, which Keil renders “accuse me.”[@keil-micah] It is the verb of 3:4. The Lord who would not answer the rulers' cry now invites His people to answer against Him. He recalls Egypt, Moses, Aaron and Miriam, and Balaam turned to blessing ([[Micah 6:4–5]]), and waits for the accusation.

None comes. What the people bring instead is a question about payment: “Wherewith shall I come before the LORD… shall I come before him with burnt offerings… thousands of rams… shall I give my firstborn for my transgression?” ([[Micah 6:6–7]]). Elder Renlund reads the escalation as Micah's satire, and gives the answer the chapter implies: “Good deeds are not sufficient. Salvation is not earned. Not even the vast sacrifices Micah knew were impossible can redeem the smallest sin.”[@renlund-do-justly] The Lord's answer is the verse Micah is known for. “He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?” ([[Micah 6:8]]). Two words in that requirement will come back. “Do justly” is *ʿăśôt mišpāṭ*, to do judgment, the thing the rulers of chapter 3 abhorred; “love mercy” is *ʾahăbat ḥesed*, to love covenant kindness.[@oshb-wlc] The lexicon files 6:8 under what God “requires that man should do.”[@bdb] (The notes on [chapter 6](/micah/6/) treat the verse and Elder Renlund's reading of it.)

So the case stands at the end of chapter 6 as follows. The Lord has brought His *rîb*. He has invited an answer against Him and received none. He has stated what He requires, justice and kindness, and the chapter ends with the sentence: the city's rich are “full of violence,” its scales are false, and “therefore also will I make thee sick in smiting thee” ([[Micah 6:12–13]]).

## I have sinned against him

Chapter 7 begins in lament, “Woe is me!” ([[Micah 7:1]]), and then, at verse 7, the voice turns from lament to trust. Keil hears the whole chapter as spoken “in the name of the believing church,” which “confesses and bemoans its rebellion against the Lord” and then “rises, through belief in the fidelity of God, to the confidential hope that the Lord will cause the light of His grace to rise again.”[@keil-micah] The Student Manual reads the speaker as Israel, looking to “the God of [her] salvation.”[@ot-manual-11] Noé Correa thinks Micah is “speaking on behalf of the people.”[@correa-jonah] Every reading gives her voice to Israel: the people, for the Student Manual and Correa, or, for Keil, the penitent church of the future, which he distinguishes from the corrupt people chapter 6 accuses. I take her to be the defendant of chapter 6 brought to repentance, and what follows rests on that reading.

Hear what she says. “Therefore I will look unto the LORD; I will wait for the God of my salvation: my God will hear me” ([[Micah 7:7]]). The rulers cried and He would not answer; she says He will hear. The verbs differ in Hebrew as the outcomes do: 3:4 is *ʿānâ*, 7:7 is *šāmaʿ*, which the lexicon glosses here as to hear “favourably.”[@oshb-wlc][@bdb] Then the verse this essay turns on:

> I will bear the indignation of the LORD, because I have sinned against him, until he plead my cause, and execute judgment for me: he will bring me forth to the light, and I shall behold his righteousness. ([[Micah 7:9]])

Let me be exact about what the verse says, because the argument depends on not overreading it. “My cause” is Israel's quarrel with the enemy of verses 8 and 10, the one who gloats, “Where is the LORD thy God?” Keil: the *rîb* here “is the judicial conflict between Israel and the heathen power of the world,” which Israel bears “till the Lord conducts its conflict and secures its rights.”[@keil-micah] The Student Manual: “He will plead her cause and bring her ‘forth to the light.’”[@ot-manual-11] The cause the Lord pleads in 7:9 is not Israel's case against Him. It is her case against those who have trampled her, and He takes it up. The righteousness she will behold, Keil adds, is “the righteousness of God revealing itself in the forgiveness and restoration of Israel.”[@keil-micah]

But look at who is speaking and what she has just said. In chapter 6 she was invited to answer against the Lord and gave no answer. Here she answers. “I have sinned against him.” It is the only answer the book gives to the Lord's “testify against me,” and it is an accusation she brings against herself. And the verse that carries her confession is the verse that gives the Lord His new role. “Until he plead my cause”: the word is the *rîb* of 6:2.[@oshb-wlc] The lexicon marks the difference. It lists 7:9 under *rîb* “in good sense, = take one's part,”[@bdb] where 6:2 was the Lord's case against His people.

Two other words make the same turn. In 6:8 the Lord required man to “do justly,” *ʿāśâ* with *mišpāṭ*; in 7:9 He will “execute judgment for me,” *ʿāśâ* with *mišpāṭ* again, and these are the only two places in Micah where the verb and the noun meet.[@oshb-wlc] The lexicon files 7:9 under the execution of judgment “in favour of” someone.[@bdb] What He required of man, He does for the penitent. And *ḥesed*, the mercy He required Israel to love in 6:8, is the mercy the book's closing verses say He delights in: “he delighteth in mercy” ([[Micah 7:18]]).[@oshb-wlc]

That these three words, the case, the judgment and the mercy, each appear first as the Lord's demand and last as the Lord's gift is my observation; no commentary I have read makes it, and Keil, who reads chapter 7 as the church's answer to the threats of chapter 6, does not remark on the shared vocabulary. But the words are in the text, and the order is the book's. The Lord who brought the case pleads the cause. The Lord who required justice does justice for her. The Lord who required her to love mercy is the one who delights in it. And between the demand and the gift stands one sentence: “I have sinned against him.”

## Pleads, and judges

Is it strange that the same Lord should be judge and advocate in one trial? The Old Testament does not think so, and the Church's footnotes point the way. At “he will plead with Israel” in 6:2 the footnote sends the reader to Isaiah: “The LORD standeth up to plead, and standeth to judge the people” ([[Isa. 3:13]])[@lds-scriptures], a verse whose chapter heading reads, “The Lord pleads for and judges His people.”[@lds-scriptures] Pleading and judging are two acts of one Lord in one verse, and the “plead” of Isaiah 3:13 is *rîb*, the word of Micah's “controversy.”[@oshb-wlc] David asked the same of Him in a breath: “The LORD therefore be judge, and judge between me and thee, and see, and plead my cause” ([[1 Sam. 24:15]]). So does a psalmist: “Judge me, O God, and plead my cause against an ungodly nation” ([[Ps. 43:1]]). No one in these verses finds a contradiction in asking the Judge to be his Advocate. Elder Holland, explaining the title Wonderful Counselor, quoted Isaiah 3:13 and the Restoration's Advocate together: the Messiah “will be our mediator, our intercessor, defending our case in the courts of heaven.”[@isaiah-in-bom]

One caution about the word. Kent Jackson, writing on Hosea's *rîb*, says the verb “never describes an appeal or call to repentance, but always a hostile confrontation, an accusation.”[@jackson-hosea-marriage] That is true of the word's edge. The lexicon lists 7:9, with Jeremiah 50:34, under *rîb* “in good sense, = take one's part,” and in the same paragraph lists Isaiah 50:8 as a case “against whom.”[@bdb] In these passages an advocate's pleading is hostile; it is hostile toward the other party. What changes between Micah 6:2 and 7:9 is not the sharpness of the word but whom it is pointed at. In chapter 6 the Lord's *rîb* is against Israel. In chapter 7 it is on her side.

## Who will contend with me?

The penitent's words in 7:9 have a near twin in Isaiah, and the twin is spoken by someone else. The Servant of the Lord, beaten and spat upon, says: “He is near that justifieth me; who will contend with me? let us stand together: who is mine adversary? let him come near to me. Behold, the Lord GOD will help me; who is he that shall condemn me?” ([[Isa. 50:8–9]]). “Contend” is *yārîb*, the very form of Micah 7:9's “plead,” though here the word is pointed against the speaker; and “mine adversary” is *baʿal mišpāṭî*, the master of my judgment, with *mišpāṭî* spelled as Micah spells it.[@oshb-wlc] Delitzsch glosses the phrase “he who has a judicial cause of lawsuit against me.”[@delitzsch] The Church's heading on the chapter reads, “Isaiah speaks as the Messiah”;[@lds-scriptures] the Old Testament Student Manual also hears Israel speaking in 50:7–8.[@ot-manual-17] (The note “The servant in court” on [Isaiah 50](/isaiah/50/) sets the courtroom out.)

So Micah's penitent and Isaiah's Servant stand in the same court and speak the same words, and they stand there differently. She has sinned and waits for the Lord to take up her *rîb*. He has done no wrong, and dares any adversary to bring a *rîb* against Him, because the one who justifies is near. Paul takes the Servant's challenge and hands it to the believer. “Who shall lay any thing to the charge of God's elect? It is God that justifieth. Who is he that condemneth? It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us” ([[Rom. 8:33–34]]). The Church's footnotes join the two passages in both directions, from Isaiah 50:8 to Romans 8 and from Romans 8:32 back to Isaiah 50:8[@lds-scriptures], and Delitzsch compares the emphatic “who is he” of Isaiah 50:9 with Paul's in Romans 8:34.[@delitzsch] Paul names Christ, who died and intercedes. Since “the Father judgeth no man, but hath committed all judgment unto the Son” ([[John 5:22]]), I read Paul's answer as putting the Judge in the advocate's seat; President Benson says it outright: “He can simultaneously plead our cause and judge our destiny.”[@benson-savior-redeemer-1983] The footnotes connect Isaiah and Paul; connecting Micah's penitent to their courtroom is my reading.

## Judge and Advocate

The Restoration says plainly what Micah shows. Christ is the Judge: Moroni closes the Book of Mormon before “the pleasing bar of the great Jehovah, the Eternal Judge of both quick and dead” ([[Moro. 10:34]]). And Christ is the Advocate: “Listen to him who is the advocate with the Father, who is pleading your cause before him” ([[D&C 45:3]]); “if any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous” ([[1 Jn. 2:1]]); “he advocateth the cause of the children of men” ([[Moro. 7:28]]); and in the Kirtland Temple, “I am he who was slain; I am your advocate with the Father” ([[D&C 110:4]]). The Bible Dictionary defines the word: an advocate is “one who pleads another's cause.”[@bd-advocate]

The two roles are held by one person, and the Church says so. President Benson: “He is our Advocate, Mediator, and Judge.”[@benson-savior-redeemer-1983] A Church institute manual: “Jesus Christ is not only our judge but also is our ‘advocate with the Father.’”[@jcgospel-2023-lesson-24] John Tanner, answering the natural question of how the Father fits into this court, says the Father is not our accuser, and “just as the Son is both our merciful advocate and our just judge, so the Father possesses in Himself the qualities of both justice and mercy in perfect fullness.”[@tanner-advocate-high-priest] The Savior Himself puts both roles in one sentence to the Nephites: whoever repents and endures, “him will I hold guiltless before my Father at that day when I shall stand to judge the world” ([[3 Ne. 27:16]]).

I should say what this does and does not claim about Micah. Micah 7:9 is not a prophecy of Christ's advocacy; no Church source reads it so, and the verse is about Israel's enemies. In Micah the Lord is Himself the injured party who pleads; in the revelation the Advocate pleads before the Father, saying, “Father, behold the sufferings and death of him who did no sin… spare these my brethren that believe on my name” ([[D&C 45:4–5]]). The courts are not drawn identically. I read Micah as showing the thing itself, before it had a name: the one with the case against the sinner becoming, upon her confession, the one who takes up her cause. The Restoration gives that one a name and a reason. He can plead because He paid: “he hath answered the ends of the law, and he claimeth all those who have faith in him” ([[Moro. 7:28]]), and His advocacy, Elder Renlund says, “is, at least in part, to remind us that He has paid for our sins.”[@renlund-choose-2018] That pleading “is not adversarial,” since the Father “has wanted all along” what the Son asks.[@renlund-choose-2018] Micah's own close says as much of the Lord who brought the case: “he retaineth not his anger for ever, because he delighteth in mercy” ([[Micah 7:18]]).

## Unto those who confess

Micah draws the line between the cry He will not answer and the cry He will, and it is the line this essay has followed. The rulers of chapter 3 cried while they leaned on His presence and perverted His justice, and He hid His face. The speaker of chapter 7 said “I have sinned against him,” and was sure He would hear her and plead her cause. The difference was not the volume of the cry. It was the confession in it.

The Restoration draws the same line between advocacy and repentance. The Joseph Smith Translation adds the condition to John's sentence: “if any man sin and repent, we have an advocate with the Father.”[@lds-scriptures] The Lord says, “I, the Lord, forgive sins unto those who confess their sins before me and ask forgiveness” ([[D&C 64:7]]), and gives the sign of repentance: “he will confess them and forsake them” ([[D&C 58:43]]). Elder Renlund: “For those who believe in Jesus Christ, repent, are baptized, and endure to the end… the Savior forgives, heals, and advocates.”[@renlund-choose-2018] And of the requirement of 6:8 he says that a just person “repents when falling short, and keeps on trying.”[@renlund-do-justly]

So a disciple who has sinned stands where Micah's speaker stood, and has her words to use. Not the words of chapter 3, which presume on the Lord's presence while refusing what He requires, but the words of chapter 7, which bear His displeasure, confess the sin, and wait. The Lord's part is what Micah shows it to be. He hears. He pleads. He does for the penitent the justice He required of her. He delights in the mercy He told her to love. Micah's own name asks the book's last question, “Who is a God like unto thee, that pardoneth iniquity?” ([[Micah 7:18]]); Matthew Bowen hears in it the prophet's message, that “God, not least in his mercy, is incomparable.”[@bowen-ominous-onomastics] Elder Holland has said the same thing in a sentence: “surely the thing God enjoys most about being God is the thrill of being merciful, especially to those who don't expect it and often feel they don't deserve it.”[@holland-laborers]

Who is like Him? A judge who asks the accused to accuse him, and who, when she accuses herself instead, rises to plead her cause. Micah's answer is that there is none like Him. The Restoration gives Him His name: Jesus Christ, the Judge who is our Advocate.
