Three prophets promise Israel a changed heart, and each uses a different verb for it. Moses says the Lord “will circumcise thine heart” (Deut. 30:6). Jeremiah says He will “write it in their hearts” (Jer. 31:33). Ezekiel says He will take the heart out: “I will take the stony heart out of their flesh, and will give them an heart of flesh” (11:19). Keil calls the stone and the flesh “figures, which are peculiar to Ezekiel,” and sets the three prophets together: the promise “has for its basis the prediction in Deuteronomy 30:6,” and for its substance one compares Jeremiah 31:33.[@keil-ezekiel] The site’s Deuteronomy page [For He Is Thy Life](/deuteronomy/themes/for-he-is-thy-life/) follows the circumcised heart, and the Jeremiah page [The Covenant Written in the Heart](/jeremiah/themes/new-covenant/) the written one. This page follows what is Ezekiel’s alone, and argues one thing from it: Ezekiel alone describes the new heart as a replacement, and in his book the Lord commands Israel to make it and then gives it for His own name’s sake, which shows that the heart a person is told to make can only be received, with no claim to it.

Ezekiel says it three times, and the three sentences are not the same kind of sentence. To the exiles it is a promise: “I will give them one heart, and I will put a new spirit within you” (11:19). To the house of Israel it is a command: “make you a new heart and a new spirit: for why will ye die, O house of Israel?” (18:31). To gathered Israel it is a gift with every verb the Lord’s: “A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you … and I will give you an heart of flesh” (36:26). “New” occurs in Ezekiel only in these three verses.[@oshb-wlc] In the last of them the Lord takes the verb of the command: Israel was told to “make” a heart (18:31, *ʿāśâ*), and in 36:27 the Lord says He will make Israel’s walking, *wĕʿāśîtî*, and the verse ends with the same verb in Israel’s hands, “and do them,” as 11:20 already had.[@oshb-wlc] Reading the three sentences by that verb is mine, and it is named once more where the verses are read; Keil, Ellicott and the Cambridge commentary each address how the command and the gift fit, and I quote them. The Deuteronomy page makes the parallel move with that book’s verb, “circumcise,” commanded at 10:16 and promised at 30:6.

Two more things are Ezekiel’s own. The “new spirit” of the three verses becomes, in the last promises, “my spirit”: “I will put my spirit within you” (36:27), “shall put my spirit in you” (37:14), “I have poured out my spirit upon the house of Israel” (39:29), and the verb of that last sentence, “poured out,” is used of the spirit nowhere else in the book.[@oshb-wlc] And the reason is not Israel’s. Deuteronomy’s heart is circumcised “that thou mayest live” (Deut. 30:6); Ezekiel’s is replaced “not for your sakes … but for mine holy name’s sake” (36:22). Where the page sets Ezekiel beside the other two books, the comparison is the commentators’.

## “Stiffhearted”

The book begins with the heart as it is. Ezekiel is sent to “impudent children and stiffhearted” (2:4), to a house “impudent and hardhearted” (3:7), and the Lord says of the exiles who will remember Him, “I am broken with their whorish heart, which hath departed from me” (6:9). The elders “have set up their idols in their heart” (14:3), “their heart went after their idols” (20:16), and the people who come to hear the prophet have a heart that “goeth after their covetousness” (33:31). The Cambridge commentary sums up the heart of chapters 2 and 3 as “strong, obdurate, unimpressible and rebellious,” and reads the stone of 11:19 the same way: “The old stony heart, unimpressible and obstinate, shall be taken away, and a heart of ‘flesh,’ sensitive and responsive to the touch of Jehovah, shall be given them.”[@cambridge-ezekiel] The heart Ezekiel promises to replace is the one the book describes.

## “I will give them one heart”

The first promise goes to the exiles whom Jerusalem despised (11:15–16): “I will give them one heart, and I will put a new spirit within you; and I will take the stony heart out of their flesh, and will give them an heart of flesh: That they may walk in my statutes, and keep mine ordinances, and do them: and they shall be my people, and I will be their God” (11:19–20). The Hebrew has “one heart,” *lēb ʾeḥād*; the ancient Greek translation read “another,” and some manuscripts “new,” the word of 36:26. Keil defends “one” from Jeremiah 32:39, “I will give them one heart, and one way,” and reads it as the Lord causing “all hearts and minds to become one,” which “can only be effected by His giving a ‘new spirit,’ taking away the stone-heart, and giving a heart of flesh instead.”[@keil-ezekiel] The Cambridge commentary reports the variants and notes that “another” and “new” make “a better antithesis to ‘stony heart’ than ‘one’ does.”[@cambridge-ezekiel] The Church’s footnotes at 11:19 read the verse as conversion: “heart” leads to the Topical Guide heading “Conversion,” “new” to “Man, New, Spiritually Reborn,” and the second “heart” to 2 Corinthians 3:3, where Paul writes of the Spirit writing “not in tables of stone, but in fleshy tables of the heart.”[@lds-scriptures] Elder D. Todd Christofferson cites these verses for the change of heart and says, “Surely this is what happened to the people of King Benjamin when they said their hearts had been changed.”[@christofferson-converted]

## “Make you a new heart and a new spirit”

Seven chapters later the same words are a command. After the long argument that each soul answers for itself (18:1–29), the Lord says, “Repent, and turn yourselves from all your transgressions … Cast away from you all your transgressions, whereby ye have transgressed; and make you a new heart and a new spirit: for why will ye die, O house of Israel?” (18:30–31). Keil notes that “The appeal in Ezekiel 18:31 points back to the promise in Ezekiel 11:18–19,” and answers the obvious question: “a man cannot, indeed, create either of these by his own power; God alone can give them (Ezekiel 11:19). But a man both can and should come to God to receive them: in other words, he can turn to God, and let both heart and spirit be renewed by the Spirit of God.”[@keil-ezekiel] Ellicott: “it is not meant to say that this can ever be the work of any other than God’s Holy Spirit; but that Spirit is ever given to them that ask Him, and the question of salvation is still one which each man must decide for himself before God.”[@ellicott-ezekiel] The Cambridge commentary: “The words are those of practical exhortation; to charge the prophet with assigning to man a power greater than that which Scripture in general allows to him is to distort his language.”[@cambridge-ezekiel] The Church’s footnotes at 18:31 give the same headings as at 11:19, “Conversion” and “Man, New, Spiritually Reborn.”[@lds-scriptures] No Church source I read comments on the command itself; the manuals and talks quote the promise and the gift.

## “A new heart also will I give you”

The third sentence sets the change in order. Israel is gathered (36:24); “Then will I sprinkle clean water upon you, and ye shall be clean” (36:25); “A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh” (36:26); “And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them” (36:27). Keil reads the sequence as cleansing “followed by renewal with the Holy Spirit, which takes away the old heart of stone and puts within a new heart of flesh, so that the man can fulfil the commandments of God.”[@keil-ezekiel] The Cambridge commentary distinguishes the two gifts: “The heart is more passive and receptive and but responds to influences, the spirit is active and regulative.”[@cambridge-ezekiel] The Student Manual: “They are to be converted to the Lord and receive His Spirit (see vv. 26–27),” and the recovery “will be done for the Lord’s reasons, not because Israel has earned it (see v. 32).”[@ot-manual-27] The Seminary manual gives the principle, “If we follow Jesus Christ, He can change our hearts,” and sets Mosiah 5:2 beside the verse.[@ot-seminary-teacher-141] Elder Dale G. Renlund quotes 36:26 as “the operation that we all need,” which comes “Through the Atonement of Christ and by obedience to the laws and ordinances of the gospel.”[@renlund-mighty-change] The clean water of 36:25 the commentaries trace to the law’s ritual washings, “the lustrations prescribed by the law,” and Ellicott adds that “It was the same symbolism which led in later ages to the use of baptism.”[@keil-ezekiel][@ellicott-ezekiel] *Come, Follow Me* pairs verses 26–27 with Alma 7:14–16, where the call to be born again continues, “come and be baptized unto repentance, that ye may be washed from your sins”; no Church source I read names baptism at the clean water of verse 25 itself.[@cfm-ot-2026-45]

Here the verb matters. The command of 18:31 was “make you,” *ʿăśû lākem*; in 36:27 the Lord does not say He will make the heart, which He gives, but that He will make the walking: “I will cause,” literally “I will make it that in my statutes ye walk,” *wĕʿāśîtî*, which Keil glosses “to make or effect your walking.”[@keil-ezekiel] Then the verse hands the verb back: “and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them,” *waʿăśîtem*, as the first promise had ended, “and do them” (11:20).[@oshb-wlc] The promise of the heart ends with Israel keeping the Lord’s commandments, and reading the command and the gift through that verb is mine.

## “My spirit”

The three “new spirit” sentences give way, from 36:27 on, to “my spirit,” which the Lord says of His own spirit three times and nowhere earlier in the book.[@oshb-wlc] In the valley the whole house of Israel says “our hope is lost,” and the answer is “shall put my spirit in you, and ye shall live” (37:11, 14). The Cambridge commentary reads the spirit of 37:14 as “merely the life-giving spirit, and not the regenerating spirit, as in Ezekiel 36:27—though the distinction is merely part of the figure,” since “The resuscitation of the dead nation could come about only through their moral regeneration.”[@cambridge-ezekiel] Keil refuses the distinction: the spirit “is the creative principle both of the physical and of the ethical or spiritual life.”[@keil-ezekiel] The Church reads the vision as showing both the Resurrection (D&C 138:43) and the gathering, and the footnote on “spirit” at 37:14 leads to Alma 40:23 and D&C 88:15.[@lds-scriptures]

The last sentence before the temple vision is the strongest: “Neither will I hide my face any more from them: for I have poured out my spirit upon the house of Israel” (39:29). The verb is *šāpak*, which Ezekiel uses thirty-three times, of fury poured out (“I poured my fury upon them,” 36:18), of blood shed (36:18 again), of siege mounds cast up (4:2), of whoredom (16:15), and once, here, of the spirit.[@oshb-wlc] The Cambridge commentary: “This states in brief all the regenerating influences more fully dwelt upon in Ezekiel 36:25–31,” with Joel 2:28 and Zechariah 12:10 beside it; the Church’s footnote on “poured” leads to Zechariah 12:10, “I will pour upon the house of David … the spirit of grace and of supplications: and they shall look upon me whom they have pierced,” and the footnote on “spirit” at 36:27 to Acts 2:17.[@cambridge-ezekiel][@lds-scriptures]

## “Not for your sakes”

Ezekiel gives a reason for the new heart that Deuteronomy does not. “I do not this for your sakes, O house of Israel, but for mine holy name’s sake, which ye have profaned among the heathen” (36:22), and again after the gift, “Not for your sakes do I this, saith the Lord GOD, be it known unto you: be ashamed and confounded for your own ways” (36:32). “For my name’s sake” is the refrain of chapter 20 (20:9, 14, 22), where the Lord spared Israel in Egypt and the wilderness so that His name should not be profaned, and of its last promise (20:44), where He will deal with restored Israel “not according to your wicked ways”; the Church’s footnote on “sakes” at 36:22 leads to Deuteronomy 9:5, “Not for thy righteousness, or for the uprightness of thine heart.”[@lds-scriptures] Keil: “not because you have any claim to deliverance on account of your behaviour.”[@keil-ezekiel] Deuteronomy’s circumcised heart is “for thy life,” literally “for the sake of thy life,” as the Deuteronomy page shows, and Jeremiah’s written law is the new covenant.[@oshb-wlc] Ezekiel keeps life as the end, “turn yourselves, and live ye” (18:32), “and ye shall live” (37:14), and adds a motive Deuteronomy does not give for the heart: it is for the Lord’s name, and the people who receive it are told to be ashamed. Keil, writing on Deuteronomy 30:6, reads Moses’s promise with Ezekiel’s words, that the Lord “would take away the stony heart out of their flesh, and give them a heart of flesh, a new heart and a new spirit,” and the Bible Dictionary lists Ezekiel 44:7 with Deuteronomy 10:16 and 30:6 and Jeremiah 4:4 and 9:26 as circumcision “from sin.”[@keil-deuteronomy][@bd-circumcision] Ezekiel never uses the verb “circumcise”; what he keeps of Deuteronomy’s figure is the bar at the end of the book, that no stranger “uncircumcised in heart” shall enter the sanctuary (44:7, 9), which Keil extends to Israelites “circumcised in flesh” who lack “circumcision of heart.”[@keil-ezekiel]

## “Have ye experienced this mighty change in your hearts?”

The Church’s footnote on “heart” at 36:26 leads to 3 Nephi 10:6, and the Seminary manual sets the verse beside King Benjamin’s people, who had “no more disposition to do evil, but to do good continually” (Mosiah 5:2); Benjamin told them, “your hearts are changed through faith on his name” (Mosiah 5:7).[@lds-scriptures][@ot-seminary-teacher-141] Alma’s question, “Have ye experienced this mighty change in your hearts?” (Alma 5:14), stands beside Ezekiel’s three sentences; setting them together is mine. Keil dates the newness of heart to “the coming of Christ,” and Alma’s call to be born again names “the Lamb of God.”[@keil-ezekiel] The LORD who promises the heart is Jehovah, the premortal Jesus Christ, as the Bible Dictionary says; the change He promises comes, in Elder Renlund’s words, “Through the Atonement of Christ.”[@bd-jehovah] Lynne Hilton Wilson sets Deuteronomy and Ezekiel together and names the difference this page has followed: “a new heart—not merely circumcised—but a full transplant for whole-hearted obedience.”[@wilson-holy-spirit]

For a disciple the three sentences stand together. The heart cannot be made by the one who needs it, and it is commanded of him anyway; the Lord says He will give it, and the sentence ends with the man walking. He does it for His name’s sake, which means the one who receives it has no claim to it, and, in Ellicott’s words, the Spirit that makes it “is ever given to them that ask Him.”[@ellicott-ezekiel] And the gift is not a repaired heart but a different one, given, in Ezekiel’s order, after the washing, with the Lord’s own spirit put within, to a people who had said their hope was lost.
