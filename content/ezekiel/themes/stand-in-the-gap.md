Ezekiel’s Lord goes looking for a man and does not find one. “And I sought for a man among them, that should make up the hedge, and stand in the gap before me for the land, that I should not destroy it: but I found none. Therefore have I poured out mine indignation upon them” ([[Ezek. 22:30–31]]). The gap is a breach in a city wall, the place a defender runs to when the wall is broken, and the Hebrew lexicon takes the figure as “effect to avert calamity… by instruction and (especially) intercession.”[@bdb] The book has used it once before. The prophets who cried peace “have not gone up into the gaps, neither made up the hedge for the house of Israel to stand in the battle in the day of the LORD” ([[Ezek. 13:5]]). Twice, then, a wall is broken and no one stands in it; the second time the Lord Himself has searched.

The verse is well known and, in the Church’s own materials, almost untouched. *Scripture Helps*, the Student Manual and this year’s *Come, Follow Me* pass over it; its only cross-references are to Abraham’s plea for Sodom and the Lord’s offer through Jeremiah to pardon Jerusalem for one just man ([[Gen. 18:26]]; [[Jer. 5:1]]).[@lds-scriptures] The site’s notes on [Ezekiel 22](/ezekiel/22/) and [Isaiah 59](/isaiah/59/) already set it beside Isaiah’s “no intercessor,” and this essay starts from them. What I want to show is larger than that pairing: that the verb of the Lord’s search runs through Ezekiel’s book in the Lord’s own mouth, and that the book answers its own failed search.

The argument has three steps. First, the gap is Moses’s figure. The one time scripture shows a man standing in it, the man is Moses, and the Church’s heading calls what he did mediation. Second, Ezekiel’s book shows the figure going unfilled: prophets who do not go up, righteous men who deliver only themselves, a prophet who pleads and is answered with judgment, and a Lord who seeks a man and finds none. Third, the Lord who says “I sought… I found none” says “I will seek” twelve chapters later, of sheep no shepherd sought, and Isaiah tells the same story in the same shape, “no man… no intercessor… his arm brought salvation.” Older commentators join Ezekiel’s verse to Isaiah’s; the Church’s footnotes join Isaiah’s to the Savior who “ever liveth to make intercession” and who is “pleading your cause before” the Father. The last link, from Ezekiel’s empty gap to that advocate, is mine, and I say so when I come to it. Ezekiel 22:30 names no one. The claim is not that the verse is about Jesus, nor that the man it sought was He; the man was to stand “before me,” and the one speaking is the Lord. The claim is the one the Cambridge commentary makes of Isaiah’s parallel and the Church’s Student Manual makes of Christ: where the Lord found no man to stand before Him, He came Himself.

## Moses his chosen stood before him in the breach

Before Ezekiel, the breach had a man in it. “Therefore he said that he would destroy them, had not Moses his chosen stood before him in the breach, to turn away his wrath, lest he should destroy them” ([[Ps. 106:23]]). The psalm’s “breach” is Ezekiel’s “gap,” the same noun, and “stood before him” is the same verb and preposition as Ezekiel’s “stand… before me.”[@oshb-wlc] Delitzsch and the Cambridge commentary both send the reader of the psalm to Ezekiel 22:30; Cambridge calls it “a military metaphor. Moses confronted God with intercession like the warrior who stands in the breach of the city wall to repel the enemy at the risk of his life.”[@cambridge-psalms][@delitzsch-psalms] The Church’s heading to the psalm says what the metaphor means in a word: “Moses mediated between Israel and the Lord.”[@lds-scriptures]

The psalm looks back to Horeb, and the Church’s footnote sends the reader there ([[Ex. 32:10]]).[@lds-scriptures] The Lord said, “let me alone, that my wrath may wax hot against them, and that I may consume them: and I will make of thee a great nation” ([[Ex. 32:10]]), and Moses did not let Him alone. He pleaded, and the next day he offered himself: “if not, blot me, I pray thee, out of thy book which thou hast written” ([[Ex. 32:32]]). The Church’s heading to the chapter: “Moses serves as a mediator between God and rebellious Israel… Moses pleads and intercedes.”[@lds-scriptures] The Student Manual draws the line the heading leaves implicit. Moses “became their mediator with God. He pleaded their cause and even offered his own life to appease the divine justice… But Moses, like Christ whom he emulated, loved his people in spite of their hardheartedness and wickedness. He interceded in their behalf and saved them, but only on the condition of their repentance.”[@ot-manual-ex-21-24] The theme page [Speak Thou with Us](/exodus/themes/speak-thou-with-us/) follows that scene to the mediator of the new covenant; here the point is simpler: Moses stood before the Lord for the people, in the breach, and the Lord spared them.

Abraham had stood there too, in his way. When the men turned toward Sodom, “Abraham stood yet before the LORD” ([[Gen. 18:22]]), and bargained the Lord down to ten righteous. The Church’s one cross-reference from that scene to Ezekiel is at the Lord’s first answer, “If I find in Sodom fifty righteous within the city, then I will spare all the place for their sakes” ([[Gen. 18:26]]), whose footnote names Ezekiel 22:30 and Jeremiah 5:1 together.[@lds-scriptures] Keil hears the end of Ezekiel’s verse, “that I should not destroy it,” as “an obvious allusion” to the Lord’s words to Abraham.[@keil-ezekiel] So the figure comes to Ezekiel with a history: two men who stood before the Lord for others, and a Lord who let them plead for others.

## Ye have not gone up into the gaps

Ezekiel’s book shows the breach from the other side, as a place no one goes. The first charge is against the prophets of peace: “Ye have not gone up into the gaps, neither made up the hedge for the house of Israel to stand in the battle in the day of the LORD” ([[Ezek. 13:5]]). The site’s note on [Ezekiel 13](/ezekiel/13/) explains the figure. The commentators agree on what the prophets should have done and say it in different words. Keil: the people’s sins “had made gaps and breaches in the walls of Jerusalem,” and the prophets “had not stood in the way of this decay and its causes, as the calling and duty of prophets demanded, by reproving the sins of the people.”[@keil-ezekiel] Jamieson, Fausset and Brown: “Those who would advise the people to repentance are the restorers of the breach,” and the hedge is “the law of God.”[@jfb-ezekiel] The gap, on both readings, is filled by someone who turns the people back and stands between them and what is coming. Nobody in chapter 13 does either.

The book then closes the other doors. The elders ask whether the righteous in the land might save it, and the answer is no: “Though these three men, Noah, Daniel, and Job, were in it, they should deliver but their own souls by their righteousness” ([[Ezek. 14:14]]); “they shall deliver neither sons nor daughters” ([[Ezek. 14:16]]). Keil reads the chapter as taking away the very hope Abraham had been given at Sodom.[@keil-ezekiel] Joshua Sears, in his study of prophetic intercession, lists these verses among the places where God “explicitly forbids” it.[@sears-intercession] Even Ezekiel, whose charge is to warn ([[Ezek. 3:17]]), pleads twice, and Keil heads the first plea “Intercession of the Prophet, and the Answer of the Lord.”[@keil-ezekiel] “Ah Lord GOD! wilt thou destroy all the residue of Israel?” ([[Ezek. 9:8]]). The Lord answers by describing the extent of the iniquity: “mine eye shall not spare” ([[Ezek. 9:10]]). He asks again, “wilt thou make a full end of the remnant of Israel?” ([[Ezek. 11:13]]), and this time the answer is a remnant among the exiles, “a little sanctuary” ([[Ezek. 11:16]]). The site’s note on [Ezekiel 9](/ezekiel/9/) follows both. The Lord answers Ezekiel’s pleas, but they do not save Jerusalem, and they are not what 22:30 is looking for.

## I sought for a man, but I found none

Chapter 22 reads the law back to Jerusalem as an indictment and ends by class: prophets, priests, princes, people ([[Ezek. 22:25–29]]). Then the search. “And I sought for a man among them, that should make up the hedge, and stand in the gap before me for the land, that I should not destroy it: but I found none” ([[Ezek. 22:30]]). “Among them,” Keil says, is “not merely the prophets, who did not enter into the gap according to Ezekiel 13:5, but to all the classes previously mentioned”; the corruption “is so universal, that not a man is to be found who could enter into the gap as a righteous man, or avert the judgment of destruction by his intercession.”[@keil-ezekiel] He adds a caution worth keeping: the seeking and not finding “cannot be understood in a chronological sense, i.e., as an event belonging to the past”; the verse “merely depicts the existing moral condition of the people.”[@keil-ezekiel] That is why this essay does not claim that the Lord has found no one in every age. He found Moses. Ezekiel 22 says that in Ezekiel’s Jerusalem He found none.

The Cambridge commentary reads the man differently, and the difference matters later. “A ‘man’ here is not a man to intercede, but a man to interpose, to stem the tide of ruin and turn the fortunes of the people. The moral energies of the nation were wholly exhausted; it could no more put forth out of itself a saviour to retrieve its fortunes.”[@cambridge-ezekiel] Keil says intercede; Cambridge says interpose. Moses, in Exodus 32, did both: he prayed, and he went down the mountain and acted. Taken together, the two readings describe someone who pleads before the Lord and acts to repair the breach, and in Ezekiel’s Jerusalem there was no one for either.

The Lord sought, and the Lord poured out His indignation, and the chapter ends.

## I will seek

A repeated verb, on my reading, lets the book answer its own failed search. The word for “I sought” in 22:30 is *bāqaš*, and the Lord uses it in the first person five times in Ezekiel.[@oshb-wlc] Three times it is what He will do to a watchman who sees the sword and does not warn: “his blood will I require at thine hand” ([[Ezek. 3:18]]; [[Ezek. 3:20]]; [[Ezek. 33:8]]). Once it is 22:30, “I sought for a man.” And once, in the chapter against Israel’s shepherds, it is this: “I will seek that which was lost” ([[Ezek. 34:16]]). The shepherds are charged in the same verb, “neither have ye sought that which was lost” ([[Ezek. 34:4]]); “none did search or seek after them” ([[Ezek. 34:6]]).[@oshb-wlc] So the Lord who sought a man and found none, and who seeks blood from the watchman who would not warn, says at last that He will do the seeking Himself. *Scripture Helps* reads the chapter’s end as the Church reads it: the Lord “would deliver the people from these unfaithful shepherds and personally gather and care for His flock,” and the “one shepherd… my servant David” He sets over them “is a reference to Jesus Christ, the Good Shepherd.”[@scripture-helps-ezek] The essay [One Shepherd](/ezekiel/themes/one-shepherd/) follows chapter 34; here I need only the verb.

The connection between 22:30 and 34:16 is mine. No commentary I have read makes it, and the Church’s footnotes send 34:16 to Luke’s lost sheep ([[Luke 15:4]]; [[Luke 19:10]]), not back to chapter 22.[@lds-scriptures] What is sought differs, a man in one verse and lost sheep in the other, and I do not want to make the two verses say one thing. But the pattern is in the book’s own words. A search for a man fails; a charge is laid against those who did not seek; and the Lord says “I will.” That pattern has a name in Isaiah.

## No intercessor, therefore his arm

“And he saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no intercessor: therefore his arm brought salvation unto him” ([[Isa. 59:16]]). Isaiah has the shape of Ezekiel 22:30, a search for a man that fails, and he adds the ending Ezekiel 22 leaves off: the Lord acts in His own person. Jamieson, Fausset and Brown gloss “no man” as “no representative man able to retrieve the cause of fallen men,” and cite Ezekiel 22:30 and Jeremiah 5:1 for the thought;[@jfb-isaiah] the Cambridge commentary on Isaiah cites Ezekiel 22:30 too, and the Cambridge commentary on Ezekiel cites Isaiah 59:16 back, with its twin, “I looked, and there was none to help… therefore mine own arm brought salvation unto me” ([[Isa. 63:5]]).[@cambridge-isaiah][@cambridge-ezekiel] The site’s essay [The Arm of the Lord](/isaiah/themes/arm-of-the-lord/) follows that image through Isaiah.

The Church reads Isaiah’s verse of Christ. Its heading to the chapter says, “The Messiah will intercede,” and the Student Manual: “Isaiah 59:16–21 refers to Jesus Christ, our intercessor with the Father. He came to earth because ‘there was no man’ and ‘there was no intercessor’ for the people.”[@ot-manual-18] The footnote at “intercessor” gives two scriptures, and they are the two that name what He does now. “He ever liveth to make intercession for them” ([[Heb. 7:25]]). And: “Listen to him who is the advocate with the Father, who is pleading your cause before him—Saying: Father, behold the sufferings and death of him who did no sin… Wherefore, Father, spare these my brethren that believe on my name” ([[D&C 45:3–5]]).[@lds-scriptures] Each of those verses footnotes Isaiah 59:16 in return.[@lds-scriptures] The Bible Dictionary on the word: an advocate is “one who pleads another’s cause. Jesus Christ is the Advocate with the Father, on behalf of the righteous.”[@bd-advocate]

Set that beside Ezekiel’s verse. The man the Lord sought was to “stand… before me for the land, that I should not destroy it.” The advocate of the Doctrine and Covenants stands before the Father pleading a cause, and the plea is “spare.” Moses at Horeb stood before the Lord and the Lord turned; this one stands before the Father and does not stop. The Lord of Ezekiel is Jehovah, “the premortal Jesus Christ,”[@bd-jehovah] as the site’s note on [Ezekiel 1:26](/ezekiel/1/) explains. So the one who said “I sought for a man… but I found none” is the one Paul calls “the man Christ Jesus,” the “one mediator between God and men” ([[1 Tim. 2:5]]), and whom Abinadi describes “standing betwixt them and justice” ([[Mosiah 15:9]]). The Cambridge commentary, having sent its reader from Ezekiel 22 to Isaiah 59 and 63, says what happens there: “the Lord himself interposes as saviour, there being none else.”[@cambridge-ezekiel] The Student Manual says it of Christ: “He came to earth because ‘there was no man.’”[@ot-manual-18] The commentators carry the reader from Ezekiel to Isaiah, and the Church’s footnotes carry him from Isaiah to Hebrews and Kirtland. The step from the empty gap in Ezekiel to the advocate who now stands before the Father is the one I am adding, and I add it the way Isaiah does: not by finding a man in Ezekiel 22, where there is none, but by watching what the Lord did when there was none. He did not keep seeking. He came.

Both of the commentators’ words for the man are true of Him. Keil’s man would intercede; Cambridge’s would interpose, “to stem the tide of ruin.” The Savior pleads, and He also stood in the breach in person and took what came through it: “standing betwixt them and justice… taken upon himself their iniquity and their transgressions, having redeemed them, and satisfied the demands of justice” ([[Mosiah 15:9]]). The Church’s heading to Isaiah 59 has the first; Abinadi has the second.

## The repairer of the breach

The gap that mattered has been filled by the only one who could fill it, and what that leaves a disciple to do Isaiah says in the same words, and the Church reads the answer of us. To a people who keep the true fast he promises, “thou shalt be called, The repairer of the breach, The restorer of paths to dwell in” ([[Isa. 58:12]]). “Repairer of the breach” is Ezekiel 22:30’s two words, the wall-builder and the gap, set side by side[@oshb-wlc], and every commentator I have read gives the title to the people, not the Messiah: “Israel restored to its fatherland receives the honourable title of ‘builder of breaches,’” Delitzsch writes.[@delitzsch] The Student Manual quotes Bishop John H. Vandenberg on the verse: it is “a promise to those working with the members of the Church who are in need physically and spiritually… you have helped them overcome their weaknesses, to restore their souls, to bridge the gap.”[@ot-manual-18] Jamieson, Fausset and Brown said the same of Ezekiel 13: “Those who would advise the people to repentance are the restorers of the breach.”[@jfb-ezekiel]

So the breach has been stood in, by the only one who could stand in it to the end. Moses stood there once, “in the similitude” of the Only Begotten, as the Student Manual reads him, and the Lord spared the people on condition of their repentance.[@ot-manual-ex-21-24] In Ezekiel’s Jerusalem no man could stand there, and Ezekiel 14 and 22 are the record of a time when none could. The one who stands there now and does not leave is the Savior, “pleading your cause” ([[D&C 45:3]]). Two scholars at BYU, writing on Exodus 32, set the two side by side: “Jesus Christ, the Great Intercessor, continually stands before God the Father and pleads the cause of the entire human family (see Doctrine and Covenants 45:3–5). Thus, the meekness and intercessory roles of both Moses and Jesus Christ are highlighted in scripture across the ages.”[@belnap-skinner-sinai]

What the sources leave a disciple is the part Jamieson, Fausset and Brown and Bishop Vandenberg describe: to call people to repentance and to restore those in need. The Lord’s own “I seek,” said of the watchman’s blood, tells us how He regards one who sees the sword and will not warn, and Elder Christofferson says the charge is not the prophet’s alone: “While the duty to warn is felt especially keenly by prophets, it is a duty shared by others as well. In fact, ‘it becometh every man who hath been warned to warn his neighbor.’”[@christofferson-warning] And disciples pray for one another before Him, as Ezekiel prayed and was answered. The Lord who sought a man in Jerusalem and found none came Himself, and stands before the Father now. He is seeking, in His own word, the lost; and every man who has been warned is to warn his neighbor ([[D&C 88:81]]).
