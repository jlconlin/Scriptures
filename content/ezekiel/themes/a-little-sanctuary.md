Those left in Jerusalem had a sentence for the exiles: “Get you far from the LORD: unto us is this land given in possession” (11:15). The Lord’s reply accepts the distance and rejects their conclusion. “Although I have cast them far off among the heathen, and although I have scattered them among the countries, yet will I be to them as a little sanctuary in the countries where they shall come” (11:16). “Get you far” and “cast them far off” are one Hebrew root, *rāḥaq*, and the Lord had already used it of Himself, asking Ezekiel in the temple whether he saw the abominations “that I should go far off from my sanctuary” (8:6).[@oshb-wlc]

That verb stands five times in Ezekiel, and in order the five tell the book’s story of the Lord’s presence. He goes far from His house (8:6). Jerusalem tells the exiles they are far from Him (11:15). He says He sent them far and is their sanctuary there (11:16). When the glory has come back to a new house, He asks Israel to put its whoredom “far from me, and I will dwell in the midst of them for ever” (43:9), and judges the Levites who “are gone away far from me” (44:10).[@oshb-wlc] Reading the five as one sequence is mine; the commentaries note the root at 8:6 and 11:15–16 and go no further. The sentence at the center is 11:16. Of Ezekiel’s twenty-eight verses with the word “sanctuary,” all but three name a house, as “my sanctuary” or “the sanctuary,” and two of those three are plurals (21:2; 28:18); 11:16 alone has no article or possessive, “for a sanctuary,” what the Lord Himself becomes to a people who have lost the building.[@oshb-wlc]

What the Church’s sources carry is the shape of it: the glory seen in Babylon before Ezekiel sees it leave Jerusalem, the Lord not confined to His temple, and a temple promised again with His presence in it. What “little” means, and whether the verb is past or future, the commentaries dispute, and the page reports the dispute. The last section sets 11:16 beside the one other verse in the Old Testament where the Lord is a sanctuary, which the Church’s footnote joins to it, and beside the city whose temple is the Lord God and the Lamb, to which Keil looks beyond it.

## “Among the captives by the river of Chebar”

The book opens with the glory far from the temple. Ezekiel is “among the captives by the river of Chebar” in the fifth year of the captivity (1:1–2), and what he sees there is “the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the LORD” (1:28), the glory that belonged in the temple at Jerusalem. He sees it again in the plain, “as the glory which I saw by the river of Chebar” (3:23). Only in the sixth year (8:1), carried “in the visions of God to Jerusalem” (8:3), does he watch it leave: it rises from the cherub to the threshold (9:3; 10:4), goes out to the east gate (10:18–19), and “went up from the midst of the city, and stood upon the mountain which is on the east side of the city” (11:23). Then the vision “went up from me” and Ezekiel is back “to them of the captivity” (11:24–25).[@lds-scriptures]

The Church’s sources read that order. *Come, Follow Me*: Ezekiel saw “‘the glory of the Lord’ (Ezekiel 1:28)—not back in the temple at Jerusalem but there in Babylon among the exiles. The wickedness in Jerusalem, he learned, had become so severe that God’s presence was no longer there.”[@cfm-ot-2026-45] Scripture Helps: the vision “likely deepened his understanding of God’s majesty and provided assurance that the Lord could still be with the people, even though they had been exiled to Babylon.”[@scripture-helps-ezek] Scripture Central: “God’s presence is mobile and not confined to the temple in Jerusalem.”[@sc-knowhy-719] Avram Shannon reads chapters 1 and 10 together: “Ezekiel explains that the reason he could see this vision of Jehovah from his standpoint in exile is that Jehovah has left his house in Jerusalem,” and “The divine presence then traveled to the Jews who were living in the Babylonian exile.”[@shannon-location] Kristian Heal reads the first vision as itself “a sign that the Lord’s presence has departed the Jerusalem temple.”[@heal-abide-ezekiel]

The text says less about the route. Ezekiel 11:23 leaves the glory on the mountain east of the city and says nothing of where it went next; what carries Ezekiel back to Chaldea in the next verse is the Spirit, not the glory.[@oshb-wlc] What the book does say is that the glory was seen among the captives a year before Ezekiel was shown it leaving, and that the Lord says He is a sanctuary to them there.

## “That I should go far off from my sanctuary”

On the reading the commentaries prefer, the first use of the verb is the Lord’s own, and it is a question. In the temple court Ezekiel is shown “the image of jealousy” and asked, “seest thou what they do? even the great abominations that the house of Israel committeth here, that I should go far off from my sanctuary?” (8:6). The Hebrew does not name who goes far off. Ellicott says it “may therefore be understood either of the removing of the people or of the Divine abandonment of the sanctuary,” and judges the second “more probable”; Keil is sure of it, “The subject … is not the house of Israel, but Jehovah”; the Cambridge commentary glosses it “i.e. withdraw,” since the abominations “defiled Jehovah’s sanctuary, the place of his abode, causing him to abandon it.”[@ellicott-ezekiel] Richard Draper draws the lesson: the vision of the Lord’s Spirit lifting from the sanctuary meant that “It was not the temple but righteousness that would be Judah’s only shield.”[@draper-exile]

So when Jerusalem says to the exiles “Get you far from the LORD” (11:15), the word has already been spoken of the Lord Himself, and of His leaving the speakers. The Church’s footnote on “the men of thy kindred” gives the ancient Greek translation’s reading, “thy fellow exiles”; Keil reads the Hebrew word as the duty a kinsman owed to redeem his brother, so that the exiles are “the people of thy proxy,” whom Ezekiel represents.[@lds-scriptures] Jerusalem claims the land and the Lord; the Lord’s answer concedes only the distance, and gives the land to the exiles (11:17).

## “Yet will I be to them as a little sanctuary”

“Although I have cast them far off among the heathen, and although I have scattered them among the countries, yet will I be to them as a little sanctuary in the countries where they shall come” (11:16). The Hebrew of “will I be” is a past narrative form, “and I was,” or “and I became”; the King James Version and the Septuagint translate it as a future, and Keil, the Cambridge commentary, the lexicon and the modern translations as a past.[@oshb-wlc] The NRSV Updated Edition: “yet I have been a sanctuary to them for a little while in the countries where they have gone.”[@nrsvue] Keil’s own rendering: “I have become to them a sanctuary for a little while in the lands whither they have come.”[@keil-ezekiel] Read that way, the verse does not promise what the Lord will do for the exiles but tells them what He has already been.

“Little,” *meʿaṭ*, follows the noun, “for a sanctuary, a little,” and the commentaries divide on it. The Cambridge commentary and the lexicon take it of degree, “for a sanctuary but little”: “he had not been to the exiles in great measure that which a ‘sanctuary’ is, viz. a presence of Jehovah, a sanctification, and a religious joy.”[@cambridge-ezekiel][@bdb] Ellicott takes it of time: “for a little while, during the term of their captivity, God’s presence with them spiritually would be instead of the outward symbolical presence in His Temple.”[@ellicott-ezekiel] Keil finds it “difficult to decide” and leans to time, since the promise to gather them (11:17) makes the substitution “only a provisional one.”[@keil-ezekiel] The page does not choose. On either reading the sentence says that in the countries the Lord Himself has been to the exiles something of what the house was, in measure or for a time.

Keil says it in so many words: “Jehovah is their sanctuary even in their dispersion … the Lord Himself had become their temple.”[@keil-ezekiel] No Church source reads the verse that fully; the Church’s footnote on “sanctuary” gives Isaiah 8:14 and the Topical Guide heading “Refuge,” Gerald Lund notes that with these words the Lord “changes the tone” from judgment to hope, and the Cambridge commentary doubts that the word ever means refuge, since “the sanctuary is the abode of Jehovah, and his presence there sanctifies those in the midst of whom he dwells.”[@lds-scriptures][@lund-ezekiel][@cambridge-ezekiel] Either way, a sanctuary in Ezekiel is where the Lord is; to be one for the exiles is to be where they are.

## “I will dwell in the midst of them for ever”

The house itself is lost: “I will profane my sanctuary, the excellency of your strength, the desire of your eyes” (24:21). What follows the news of the city’s fall (33:21) is a presence promised to the gathered: “Thus shall they know that I the LORD their God am with them” (34:30), and “I will set my sanctuary in the midst of them for evermore. My tabernacle also shall be with them: yea, I will be their God, and they shall be my people” (37:26–27). Keil notes that the sentence is formed on Leviticus 26:11, with “sanctuary” for “tabernacle” and “for ever” added, and that the Hebrew preposition of 37:27 is “over them”; the Cambridge commentary thinks “over” “has little probability” and reads the sanctuary as what sanctifies rather than what shelters.[@keil-ezekiel] The Church reads these verses of a temple. The Student Manual: Ezekiel “prophesied in 37:26–28 about a holy sanctuary or temple that would be part of the great reunification of Israel,” the temple of chapters 40–48.[@ot-manual-27] The Church’s footnote on “sanctuary” at 37:26 gives both headings at once, “God, Presence of; Temple.”[@lds-scriptures]

Then the glory comes back the way it went. “The glory of the God of Israel came from the way of the east,” “into the house by the way of the gate whose prospect is toward the east,” and “filled the house” (43:2, 4, 5); the Church’s footnote at 43:2 leads back to 11:23, where it left, and forward to Revelation 21:11.[@lds-scriptures] The Cambridge commentary: “the prophet sees Jehovah return to it by the E. gate, by which he had seen him leave it (ch. 11).”[@cambridge-ezekiel] The voice from the house names the place “where I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel for ever” (43:7), and the root of chapter 11 returns with it: “Now let them put away their whoredom, and the carcases of their kings, far from me, and I will dwell in the midst of them for ever” (43:9). The distance Jerusalem wished on the exiles is asked of Israel’s sin instead. The book’s last words are the city’s new name, “The LORD is there” (48:35), which the Joseph Smith Translation reads “Holy; for the Lord shall be there,” with the footnote to Revelation 21:3.[@lds-scriptures] The Student Manual: “The temple will be built as a symbol to Israel that the Lord is with His people.”[@ot-manual-27]

## “He shall be for a sanctuary”

The one other verse in the Old Testament where the Lord is a sanctuary is Isaiah’s: “he shall be for a sanctuary; but for a stone of stumbling and for a rock of offence to both the houses of Israel” (Isa. 8:14), with the same Hebrew word and the same preposition.[@oshb-wlc] The Church’s footnote at Isaiah 8:14 reads “security for those who trust Him, but dismay and suffering for unbelievers,” and points to Ezekiel 11:16; the footnote at Ezekiel 11:16 points back.[@lds-scriptures] The LORD of both verses is Jehovah, the premortal Jesus Christ, and the Church’s footnote at Ezekiel 1:26 leads to the Topical Guide heading “Jesus Christ, Appearances, Antemortal.”[@bd-jehovah] The Isaiah footnotes carry the paired verse to Christ in the Church’s own voice: “stumbling” leads to Luke 2:34 and Romans 9:32 and to the heading “Cornerstone.”[@lds-scriptures]

Keil reads the “little” sanctuary of exile as provisional, and the fuller presence it looks to as “fully realized in the heavenly Jerusalem, where the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb will be a temple to the redeemed (Revelation 21:22)”: “I saw no temple therein: for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are the temple of it.”[@keil-ezekiel] Keil and Ellicott join 37:27 to “the tabernacle of God is with men” (Rev. 21:3) and to “the Word … dwelt among us” (John 1:14), where the Greek is “tabernacled,” and the Cambridge commentary joins 48:35 to Revelation 21:3; the Church’s edition makes the Revelation 21:3 join at 48:35 and leaves the John join unmade, and this page reports it as the commentators’.[@ellicott-ezekiel] The Church reads the temple of chapters 40–48 as a house to be built, and Scripture Central adds that Ezekiel’s mobile presence “does not imply that because God’s presence is not limited to one location, temples or promised lands are unnecessary or simply symbolic.”[@sc-knowhy-719]

The same reassurance reaches anyone far from a house of the Lord: “Despite how far the covenant people had fallen, God had not totally abandoned them.”[@cfm-ot-2026-45] The Lord did not deny that He sent them far; He denied that far from the land was far from Him. He says He is a sanctuary to them in the countries, little or for a little while, and the sanctuary He promised was one set in their midst, with Himself dwelling there for ever. Between the two stands the one thing the book asks of Israel in the same word: that it put its sin far from Him (43:9).
