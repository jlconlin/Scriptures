# Ezekiel brief

Book-level decisions for Ezekiel. The site-wide standard is in `STANDARDS.md`; this file records what is particular to Ezekiel. Writers, checkers, and reviewers read both before starting a chapter. Jeremiah's brief (`content/jeremiah/BRIEF.md`) is the model, and its section “What a chapter is for” holds here word for word; the author's rules recorded in Genesis's brief carry over to every book.

The author asked for the book on 2026-10-07 (“Let's do the book of Ezekiel next”) and has not read this brief. The book is in preview (`status: preview` in `book.yaml`). Where a section says “Proposed,” that is the approach the chapters follow; it is a decision of the coordinator's, not a fact from a source, and the author can overturn it.

Related files: `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` at the top of the repository (what waits for the author, for every book); `THEME-CANDIDATES.md` in this folder. Jeremiah, Lamentations, Isaiah, Genesis, and Malachi are on the site.

## What the author has decided

- **Ezekiel is the next book** (author, 2026-10-07), written in the three-agent arrangement (`AUTHORING.md`, “Plan for the next book”): an Opus writer, the ledger script, a Sonnet checker, an Opus reviewer.
- **No focus questions** (author, 2026-10-06). Writers work from the questions a careful reader would ask (`STANDARDS.md` §3).
- **Don't replicate the Gospel Library** (author, 2026-10-03; `STANDARDS.md` §1). The footnotes, chapter headings, and *Scripture Helps* are beside the verse already. A note that only says what they say is not worth writing.
- **Quote the Church's material sparingly** (author, 2026-10-07; `STANDARDS.md` §1): paraphrase and cite; a short phrase where the wording matters; never the same passage in two chapters. Look beyond the Gospel Library first.
- **Newest trumps oldest** (author, 2026-10-03). For Ezekiel the newest is *Scripture Helps: Old Testament* (2025), which treats chapters 1–3, 33–34, 36–37, and 47 and has one paragraph on 40–48. The Student Manual (two chapters, on every part of the book) is older, and the Bible Dictionary is not an official statement of doctrine. Where they differ, *Scripture Helps* is followed (chapter 1's creatures and 34:23 are the cases; see below).
- **Follow *Scripture Helps* to what it cites**; **decide what the standard decides**; **the Student Manual limits apply** (about a third of a chapter's notes at most, and a note that cites it also rests on something from beyond the Church's own publications). As in Malachi's and Lamentations' briefs.

## What a chapter is for here

Ezekiel is long (48 chapters, 1,273 verses) and strange to a first reader: visions described at length, things the prophet is told to act out, extended parables, exact dates, and nine chapters of measurements. The Church's helps are full on a quarter of it and thin on the rest: thirty-nine chapters have no *Scripture Helps* section, and many have a handful of footnotes (chapter 15 has two; 6, 9, 31, and 35 have few). So a chapter does the plain work first: when this is, by the book's own dates; what Ezekiel sees or is told to do, and what the text itself says it means (most sign-acts and parables are explained a few verses later: say where); who and where the people and places are; what a hard word means. The well-known chapters (1, 3, 18, 33, 34, 36, 37, 47) will be the fullest. Chapters of measurements and boundaries (40–42, 45–46, 48) and the shorter oracles (15, 25, 30, 35) will be short, with few notes: answer what a reader would ask (what a cubit and a reed are, what is being measured and why it matters, where the glory enters) and stop. Don't pad them, and don't walk through every measurement.

## Divisions

The Bible Dictionary divides the book in four: “(1) 1–24, prophecies of judgment against Jerusalem and the nation; (2) 25–32, prophecies against Israel's enemies; (3) 33–39, prophecies of restoration; (4) 40–48, visions of the reconstruction of the temple and its worship.” The Guide to the Scriptures and *Scripture Helps* set chapters 1–3 apart (the vision and the call) and take 33–48 as one. `book.yaml` has six, which keeps all of those lines and adds one of the site's own at chapter 11, where the vision of 8–11 ends. Proposed: keep them; they are only the map on the book page.

| 25–32 | Prophecies against the Nations (`neighbors`) |

## Book-wide conventions

- **Say when, from the book's own dates.** Ezekiel counts from the captivity of Jehoiachin (1:2). The dated headings, from `data/kjv/ezekiel.json`: 1:1–2 (the thirtieth year; the fifth year of the captivity, fourth month, fifth day); 8:1 (sixth year, sixth month); 20:1 (seventh year, fifth month); 24:1 (ninth year, tenth month, tenth day: the day the siege begins, 24:2); 26:1 (eleventh year); 29:1 (tenth year, tenth month); 29:17 (twenty-seventh year, the latest date in the book); 30:20 and 31:1 (eleventh year); 32:1 and 32:17 (twelfth year); 33:21 (twelfth year, tenth month: word that the city is smitten); 40:1 (twenty-fifth year, “the fourteenth year after that the city was smitten”). Every chapter's `when` gives the nearest date above it in the book's own terms, and says so when a chapter stands under an earlier heading or has none. The dates in 25–32 are not in order; say so where it matters.
- **Years B.C.** Use the Bible Dictionary's and *Scripture Helps*': the exiles were carried away with Jehoiachin “in about 597 BC”; Ezekiel prophesied “from about 592 to 570 BC” (the Bible Dictionary: “a period of 22 years, 592–570 B.C.”); Jerusalem fell in 586 B.C. (the Bible Dictionary's date, used in Jeremiah and Lamentations on the site). A year reached by counting from these is given as “about.” The Student Manual passes on a 601 B.C. date for the captivity (26-2, Sperry); don't use it. Where a source you cite has 587 or 593, keep the dates above and mention the difference only if a note depends on it.
- **“The thirtieth year” (1:1).** The text does not say thirty years from what. Give an answer only from a source read, as that source's; otherwise say it is not explained.
- **Ezekiel's contemporaries.** *Scripture Helps* names Jeremiah (in Judah), Daniel (in the court of Babylon), and Lehi (sent to a promised land) as prophets of the same years. Say it once in chapter 1's setting. Jeremiah and Lamentations on the site tell the siege and fall: link with `[[Jer. 39:1–10]]`, `[[Jer. 52:4]]`, `[[Jer. 29:1]]` (the letter to these same exiles) rather than retell. Use the Book of Mormon where a chapter really meets it (the two sticks; the shepherds; 1 Ne. 1 beside the call), sourced like anything else.
- **“Son of man.”** Ezekiel is addressed so 94 times in the King James text. *Scripture Helps*: the Hebrew “can also be translated as ‘son of a human’” and stresses his mortality; it is “not to be confused with the title ‘Son of Man,’ which refers to Christ.” Explain it once, at 2:1; later chapters don't re-explain it and never read it as a title of Christ.
- **“Ye shall know that I am the LORD.”** The refrain, in its several forms, comes about seventy times. Explain it where it first matters (chapter 6 is the first cluster) and link back; it is a theme candidate, not a note in every chapter.
- **Names of God.** The Church edition prints “the Lord GOD” (Hebrew *adonai* with the divine name) 217 times in Ezekiel, and “Lord” for *adonai* in 18:25 and 18:29. Copy any phrase with the name from `data/kjv/ezekiel.json`. As in the other books, the Bible Dictionary identifies the LORD of the Old Testament as the premortal Christ; say it once where it first matters (the figure on the throne, 1:26–28), with the source.
- **Verse numbers.** The Hebrew numbers 20:45–49 as 21:1–5, and 21:1–32 as 21:6–37 (`scripts/hebrew.mjs` prints both). Keil's and other commentaries' headings may follow the Hebrew; cite the King James verse.
- **Repeated passages are explained once and linked.** The watchman (3:16–21 and 33:1–9); each answers for his own sins (chapter 18 and 33:10–20); the vision of the glory (chapter 1 and chapter 10, where the creatures are named cherubim); the new heart (11:19–20 and 36:26–27); the unfaithful wife (16 and 23); the lament form (19, 26:17, 27, 28:12, 32). The fuller treatment goes in the first of each pair, except the new heart, which goes in 36; the other chapter says briefly what is the same and what is new there.
- **Sign-acts.** Ezekiel is told to do things as signs (4:1–5:4; 12:1–20; 21:6–7, 18–23; 24:15–24; 37:15–20). Say what he does, then what the text says it means, from the verses that explain it. Whether a given act was performed literally or seen in vision is said only from a source read; don't guess.
- **Parables and allegories** (15, 16, 17, 19, 23, 24:3–14, 31): the text usually gives the key (17:11–21 explains 17:1–10). Identify the kings and events from the text and from Kings and Jeremiah, with links (`[[Jer. 37:5–8]]`, `[[2 Kgs. 24:17–20]]`).
- **Graphic passages.** Chapters 16 and 23 describe Jerusalem and Samaria as unfaithful wives in language the Student Manual calls “some of the most scathing in all the scriptures”; it says of chapter 23 that “all the references to whoredoms, to other impure sexual practices, and to various parts of the female anatomy are metaphorical,” the figure of Jehovah as husband and Israel as wife. Describe plainly and briefly; do not quote the explicit lines and do not dwell (`STANDARDS.md` §3). The same for 5:10 (cannibalism in the siege; Jeremiah 19:9 and Lamentations 4:10 on the site), 9:5–7, and 16:20–21 and 20:26, 31 (child sacrifice). The figure condemns a nation's idolatry and alliances; a chapter does not turn it into a statement about women.
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** Five readings are in the Church edition's footnotes: 14:9; 23:17 and 23:22; 36:20; 48:35. (The Student Manual, 26-60, also names 23:28.) Each belongs in its chapter, with a ledger row from the footnote (`lds-scriptures`).
- **Echoes in the book of Revelation** (the four living creatures; the roll eaten; the mark on the forehead; Gog and Magog; the river and the trees; the city with twelve gates). Revelation is not on the site. Name an echo where a Church footnote or a source read joins the two, in a sentence; the explanation of John's vision is not Ezekiel's chapter's work. D&C 77 explains John's beasts; *Scripture Helps* says it is “unclear whether the animals in Ezekiel's vision represent the same thing” (endnote 4).
- **Isaiah, Jeremiah, Lamentations, Genesis, and Malachi are on the site**, with theme pages for Isaiah and Jeremiah. Where Ezekiel takes up something they explain, link rather than re-explain: the shepherds (Jeremiah 23, and Jeremiah's page The Shepherds and the Branch); the sour grapes proverb (`[[Jer. 31:29–30]]`); the new covenant and the new heart (Jeremiah 31–32, and the page The Covenant Written in the Heart); prophets who say “Peace” (`[[Jer. 6:14]]`); the cup (23:31–34; Jeremiah's page The Cup of the Lord's Fury); scattered and gathered; Eden, Noah, Sodom, and the nations of Genesis 10 (Gomer, Magog, Tubal, Meshech, Togarmah); “until Shiloh come” (`[[Gen. 49:10]]`, beside 21:27).
- **Book of Mormon comparison panel: none.** Use the `scripture` kind for Book of Mormon passages.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports what runs across the book; the coordinator adds it to `THEME-CANDIDATES.md`. No theme pages are written until the author chooses them.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that forty-eight chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-07.

- **What the creatures and wheels of chapter 1 mean.** *Scripture Helps* (2025): “While some have attempted to explain what these figures represent, the full meaning of Ezekiel's vision has not been revealed to us by the Lord.” What matters most, it says, is that Ezekiel “beheld ‘the glory of the Lord’ on His throne,” an assurance that the Lord could still be with the people in exile. The Student Manual (26-3 to 26-11) offers meanings for the wings, the feet, and the wheels. Proposed: the newest wins. Chapter 1 describes what Ezekiel saw, explains the words (amber, beryl, firmament, the Hebrew behind them), says where else scripture has such a vision (Isaiah 6 on the site; Revelation 4; 1 Nephi 1:8), and gives the *Scripture Helps* statement as the frame. A meaning for a detail is given only as “has been read as,” from a source read, and never as what the detail means. No speculation about machines.
- **The Lord's hand in hard commands and hard sayings** (the prophet struck dumb, 3:26; lying on his side, 4:4–8; “I gave them also statutes that were not good,” 20:25; “if the prophet be deceived,” 14:9). For 14:9 the Joseph Smith Translation settles it (“I the Lord have not deceived that prophet”). For 20:25 find a source; if only Keil answers, give his as one reading and say the verse is hard. Do not soften the wording and do not explain it from memory.
- **The 390 and 40 days (4:4–6).** The Student Manual (26-17) treats them. Give what a source read says, as that source's; the note may say the numbers are not finally explained. The Septuagint has a different number; mention it only from Brenton's page and only if it helps.
- **Each answers for his own sins (chapter 18; 33:10–20; 14:12–20) and “visiting the iniquity of the fathers”** (`[[Ex. 20:5]]`). The Bible Dictionary names chapters 3 and 18 for “the individual responsibility of every person for the consequences of his own behavior.” Proposed: explain the proverb the exiles were using and the Lord's answer from the text; set Exodus 20:5 beside it only with a source that reconciles them (KnoWhy #712 touches it; the second article of faith is joined only if a source joins it). “The soul that sinneth, it shall die” is about a person's own accountability; what “die” means there is said from a source.
- **Daniel in 14:14, 20 and 28:3.** The Student Manual (26-41) takes him as the Daniel then in Babylon. Follow it. An identification with another, older figure is from scholarship not readable here and is left out.
- **“Until he come whose right it is” (21:27).** The Student Manual (26-59): the king is deposed “until He comes who has the right to reign over Israel and all flesh: Jesus Christ the King.” This is the chapter's Witness of Christ, with Genesis 49:10 on the site.
- **The death of Ezekiel's wife (24:15–24).** Tell it plainly and with restraint: what he was told, what it was a sign of (the text says, 24:21–24). The Student Manual (26-62) treats it. Do not explain why the Lord took her beyond what the text and a source say, and make no application to a reader's bereavement.
- **Tyre (26–28) and “a remarkable fulfillment.”** The Student Manual (27-6) gives eighteen paragraphs to how 26:1–14 was fulfilled, and (27-10) says Nebuchadnezzar did not take the island city, which 29:18 itself says (“yet had he no wages”). Proposed: say what the prophecy says, what 29:18 adds, and what the Bible Dictionary's “Tyre” gives of the city's later history; the manual's fulfillment argument is cited for what it prints and in a sentence, not rehearsed, and anything it relays from another book is that book's (`STANDARDS.md` §4 rule 6).
- **The king of Tyrus in Eden (28:11–19).** The lament speaks of the king as in “Eden the garden of God,” “the anointed cherub.” It is often read as a description of Lucifer's fall. Proposed: say first what the text says it is (a lamentation upon the king of Tyrus, 28:12); a reading of Satan in it is given only from a Church source read, worded as that source's. Isaiah 14:12 on the site treats “Lucifer, son of the morning”; link if a source joins them. KnoWhy #726 cites 28:13–16 for Eden as a mountain sanctuary.
- **Egypt (29–32).** “It shall be the basest of the kingdoms” (29:15) and the forty years (29:11–13): say what the text says; a claim about how or when it was fulfilled needs a source read (the Student Manual, 27-9, has one sentence).
- **“My servant David” (34:23–24; 37:24–25).** *Scripture Helps*: “This does not refer to King David, who died about four centuries before Ezekiel's ministry. Rather, it is a reference to Jesus Christ, the Good Shepherd, who was a descendant of David.” The Student Manual (27-17) points instead to its note on Isaiah 11:1, and its introduction to chapter 27 speaks of “a shepherd named David.” Proposed: the newest wins; the notes give the *Scripture Helps* reading, with John 10. Another reading is not set beside it. Jeremiah 23:5 and 30:9 on the site have the same promise: link.
- **The new heart and “clean water” (36:25–27).** The promise is the Lord's gift to gathered Israel. A connection to baptism or to being born again is made only from a source read. The fuller note is here, not at 11:19.
- **The valley of dry bones (37:1–14).** *Scripture Helps*: the bones “represented the house of Israel in their lost and hopeless state”; their new life “can represent the house of Israel being restored to a new spiritual life,” and the vision “can also be understood as a prophecy of the Resurrection of the dead.” The Bible Dictionary names the chapter for the Resurrection. Proposed: both, in that order: the text's own explanation first (37:11–14), then the Resurrection, each from its source. This carries the chapter's Witness of Christ.
- **The two sticks (37:15–22).** *Scripture Helps*: the act represents “the future reunification of Israel and Judah into one nation under a single king,” and “the ‘sticks’ can also represent written records. A latter-day fulfillment of Ezekiel's prophecy is seen in the coming forth of the Bible and the Book of Mormon.” D&C 27:5 calls the Book of Mormon “the stick of Ephraim”; 2 Nephi 3:12 and 1 Nephi 13:40–41 speak of the records growing together. Proposed: give both, the text's own explanation (37:21–22) first and the records as its latter-day fulfillment, in the Church's words; don't argue which is primary. What a “stick” was (the writing-board explanation in the Student Manual, 27-22) is given from the article it comes from, Keith H. Meservy, “Ezekiel's Sticks and the Gathering of Israel,” *Ensign*, Feb. 1987 (readable), as that article's explanation. This will be the book's most-read note: keep it clear and unhurried.
- **Gog and Magog (38–39).** The Bible Dictionary (“Gog”): the prophecy “points to a time when the gentile nations of the north would set themselves against the people of God and would be defeated… All this appears to be at the Second Coming of the Lord. Another battle, called the battle of Gog and Magog, will occur at the end of the 1,000 years” (Rev. 20:7–9; D&C 88:111–16). The Student Manual (27-24 to 27-28) and its Enrichment I (`ot-manual-kings-i`) call the first the battle of Armageddon. In the passage the manual quotes, “the prophecies do not name the modern nations which will be fighting for and against Israel.” Proposed: say who Gog is in the text (“Gog, the land of Magog,” 38:2), where the names come from (Genesis 10 on the site) and where the manual places them in the ancient world, and give the Bible Dictionary's two battles. **No modern nation is named or hinted at, and no present-day event is brought in.** D&C 29:21 (“as it is spoken by the mouth of Ezekiel the prophet”) is joined to 38:22 by the manual (27-27) and treated in KnoWhy #784; use them.
- **The temple of chapters 40–48.** The Bible Dictionary calls 47–48 “the description of the latter-day temple in Jerusalem.” *Scripture Helps* answers “When will the temple… be built?” with Joseph Smith: “Judah must return, Jerusalem must be rebuilt, and the temple, and water come out from under the temple, and the waters of the Dead Sea be healed… and all this must be done before the Son of Man will make His appearance.” The Student Manual (27-29) quotes James E. Talmage: whether Ezekiel took the design as “one to be subsequently realized, or as but a grand yet unattainable ideal, is not declared. Certain it is that the Temple of the vision has not yet been builded,” and that its immediate purpose was to awaken Israel “to a realization of their fallen state.” Proposed: say it once, in chapter 40's setting (a temple not yet built, which the Church's sources place in Jerusalem before the Second Coming) and link back from 41–48. Don't harmonize the measurements with Solomon's temple or with any later one unless a source read does. The guide in 40:3 is “a man… like the appearance of brass”; the manual says he “was probably not the Lord but an authorized messenger.”
- **Sacrifices in that temple (43:18–27; 45–46).** A reader will ask how animal sacrifice can be offered after Christ. The Student Manual (27-32) answers with Joseph Fielding Smith (the sons of Levi will offer again “to complete the restoration”; “afterwards sacrifice will be of some other character”); D&C 13 and 84:31 and Malachi 3:3 on the site (with its note) bear on it. Proposed: answer once, in chapter 43, from those, cited as the manual for what it prints unless the original is read; chapters 44–46 link back.
- **Zadok's sons and the Levites who went astray (44:10–16); the prince (44:3; 45–46).** Who “the prince” is, is not said in the text; give an identification only from a source read. He offers a sin offering for himself (45:22), which a note may point out from the text. Zadok is in the Bible Dictionary.
- **The waters from the temple (47:1–12).** *Scripture Helps* gives the plain sense (the river reaches the Dead Sea and heals it) and Elder Dale G. Renlund's application to the blessings of the temple. The plain sense, the Hebrew, and the links to Joel 3:18, Zechariah 14:8, and Revelation 22:1–2 are the note's work; the application stays Elder Renlund's, in a sentence, since *Come, Follow Me* and *Scripture Helps* both print it.
- **Strangers given an inheritance (47:22–23); the city's name (48:35).** The Student Manual has a paragraph on each (27-35, 27-37), and the Joseph Smith Translation changes 48:35. The book's last words carry the last chapter's Witness of Christ.
- **Prophecies against Edom and the neighbors (25; 35).** Edom is explained at Malachi 1:3–4, Jeremiah 49, and Lamentations 4:21 on the site; link. Explain these as the Lord's judgment on what those nations did when Jerusalem fell (the text says what); no application to a reader's enemies.
- **Tammuz, sun worship, and the “chambers of imagery” (chapter 8).** The Bible Dictionary has “Tammuz”; the Student Manual has 26-25 to 26-29. Describe what Ezekiel is shown, from the text and those; details of Mesopotamian religion beyond them need a source read.
