# Luke brief

Book-level decisions for the Gospel of Luke. The site-wide standard is in `STANDARDS.md`. **What Luke shares with Matthew, Mark and John is in `content/volumes/new-testament/GOSPELS.md`; read it first**: every chapter answers its own reader's questions, how a story's research is shared between the Gospels, Talmage's place, Ellicott as the outside commentary of last resort, the Greek, the themes and the images. This file holds what is Luke's own. Mark's brief is the model for its form, and much of what Matthew's and Mark's briefs settle (the Pharisees and “the Jews,” divorce, the Transfiguration, the genealogy, the virgin birth, Markan priority) holds here unchanged and is pointed to, not argued again.

**Status (2026-11-21):** drafted from a research survey made with the research scripts on 2026-11-21; each source named below was opened then, and a claim about what it says comes from that reading. **The author chose this book** (the Gospels in canonical order, `GOSPELS.md`) **and approved this brief on 2026-11-21** (“I approve the brief”). Where a section says “Proposed,” that is the drafter's decision, not a fact from a source; it stands as approved, and the author can still overturn it. The author decided the sermon on the plain the same day (“Two”: two sermons, below).

Related files: `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` at the top of the repository (“The Gospels” among them); `content/volumes/new-testament/GOSPELS.md`, `stories.yaml` and `stories/`; `UNANSWERED.md` in this folder once there is something to put in it. Theme candidates are not collected book by book for the Gospels (`GOSPELS.md`).

## What the author has decided

- **The four Gospels' shared decisions** are in `GOSPELS.md`, except two rules the writer, the checker and the reviewer must have in front of them:
  - **Talmage is opened last** (2026-11-21). *Jesus the Christ* follows the Gospel story in order, so a writer who opens it early finds an answer to nearly every question and looks no further. List the reader's questions first, look for each answer in the sources that belong to it (the Joseph Smith Translation, the Greek, conference talks, the Religious Studies Center's studies of the passage, the Bible Dictionary), and only then open Talmage, for the questions still unanswered and for what he says that the others do not. The same holds for any work that covers every verse in order: Ellicott, Huntsman and Hatch's chapters for the last week (*Greater Love Hath No Man*), **and Brown's *Testimony of Luke***, which has a note on nearly every phrase.
  - **A source is cited for what it adds** (2026-11-21). Where one source already answers the note's question and a second only agrees, the second is not cited. This holds for every source, not Talmage alone. The checker reports each note where a citation adds nothing the note's other sources don't already give, and the reviewer decides; it is a judgment on the note, never a count.
- **A saying Jesus gave on more than one occasion stays two stories** (`GOSPELS.md`, 2026-11-20). Luke's chapters get the `parallels` lines that answer Matthew's, written by hand and ending “said on another occasion” (`stories.mjs` does not print them). Matthew 5–7, 18 and 24 already point to fifteen passages of Luke 11–17 that way (`grep -rn "another occasion" content/matthew/chapters`); each Luke chapter lists the Matthew passage back in the same words.
- **Matthew 24 links to Joseph Smith—Matthew,** and Luke 21 does the same where the verses meet it (“Luke 21, Joseph Smith—Matthew and D&C 45,” below).
- **The Church's teaching manuals are finders, never cited** (`STANDARDS.md` §1): *Come, Follow Me*, *Scripture Helps: New Testament*, the *New Testament Student Manual*. `sources.yaml` has `nt-student-manual-15` (Luke 1–3) from an older page; it is not a licence for new citations. *Scripture Helps* covers Luke in seventeen chapters (02, 03, 05, 06, 08, 10, 11, 12, 13, 17, 19, 20, 21, 22, 25, 26, 27; chapter 19 covers Luke 12–17, and Luke 13 is in 13 and 19); `footnotes.mjs` finds them.

## What a chapter is for here

The author's purpose for the Gospels: “the purpose is to anticipate questions a reader might have, and answer those questions” (`GOSPELS.md`). Read the chapter, write down what a careful reader would stop and ask, and answer that from sources read in the session (`STANDARDS.md` §3). A note exists because a reader would ask; a source's having something to say is not a reason for a note. A question no source answers goes in the writer's report and then `UNANSWERED.md`.

**Begin with the chapter's stories.** Run `node scripts/stories.mjs luke <n>` and, for each story told in another Gospel, `node scripts/stories.mjs <id> --text`, and follow `GOSPELS.md`, “How a story's research is shared.” Where a research file exists, read it before you research, reopen with `source.mjs` any finding you use (it goes into your own ledger), and correct a finding you find wrong or thin, with your chapter in its `by`. Matthew's and Mark's writers left in the files' `open` lists the questions a reader of Luke will ask: about 130 lines name Luke (`grep -A15 '^open:' content/volumes/new-testament/stories/*.yaml | grep Luke`), among them the census (2:1–2), “as was supposed” and Adam “the son of God” (3:23, 38), the bloody sweat (22:43–44), whom Jesus forgave (23:34), the thief and paradise (23:43), and “the times of the Gentiles” (21:24). Those are Luke's first questions. Add to `findings` what a writer of John will need.

**What the stories show for Luke** (`stories.mjs` run for all 24 chapters): 110 stories fall in Luke's chapters. 74 have a research file; 2 are told elsewhere and have no file yet; 34 are told only in Luke. The stories told only in Luke hold about 480 of its 1,151 verses.

- **Told only in Luke, so the chapter's ledger is its research:** all of chapter 1 and 2:8–52 except the birth itself; the rejection at Nazareth (4:16–30, a separate event in the author's chronology); the sermon on the plain as a whole (6:20–49, below); Nain and the woman in Simon's house (7:11–17, 36–50); the women of 8:1–3; the good Samaritan and Mary and Martha (10:25–42); 11:1–13 and nearly everything from 11:37 to 18:14 (from the woes on the lawyers through the lost sheep, coin and son, the steward, the rich man and Lazarus, the ten lepers, to the Pharisee and the publican); Zacchaeus and the pounds (19:1–27). `stories.mjs luke <n>` names each.
- **Shared, with no research file yet; you write it:** who is greatest and the appointed kingdom ([[Luke 22:24–30]]); the risen Lord appears to the disciples ([[Luke 24:36–49]], told also in Mark 16 and John 20).
- **Luke's own passages inside shared stories,** which the files hold only from Matthew's or Mark's side: the angel and the bloody sweat (22:43–44), the healing of the servant's ear (22:51), Herod (23:6–12), the daughters of Jerusalem (23:27–31), the penitent thief (23:39–43), the fish and honeycomb (24:41–43); the Bible Dictionary lists several of these among what “appear in but one record” (`bd-gospels` [4]).

Questions a reader of Luke asks again and again (a start, not a limit):

- *Why does only Luke tell this?* Luke's special material is the largest of the three (`huntsman-lukes-jesus` [12]). That a story is Luke's alone is what the stories show and needs no row; *why* he has it only from a source, as that source's reading (`GOSPELS.md`).
- *Is this the same saying as in Matthew?* See “Repeated sayings and the sermon on the plain.”
- *What did Joseph Smith change here?* Luke has more changes than any other Gospel. See “The Joseph Smith Translation in Luke.”
- *Who were these people?* Samaritans, publicans, lawyers, Pharisees, the Seventy, Herod Antipas. The Bible Dictionary for the term, then the study of the passage.
- *What does this word mean?* The footnotes gloss many (“GR”): 2:2 “taxing” is “enrollment”; 17:21 “within” is “among” in many translations “because the pronoun ‘you’ is plural here in Greek.” A note goes further than a footnote only where the question needs more (`STANDARDS.md` §1).

**Where answers come from** is `STANDARDS.md` §1's order, with Talmage, Brown's notes by phrase, Ellicott, the *Expositor's Greek Testament*, Meyer, the *Cambridge Bible* and Edersheim last (`GOSPELS.md`). Luke is well served by Latter-day Saint studies: Brown above all, and Huntsman, Keller, Olson and Wayment. **Don't always go to the same source first,** and that includes Brown: his commentary is the place to check a question the studies of the passage left open, not where the search begins. The entries quoted in this brief are leads for the questions they are quoted under.

## Divisions

Luke's own shape. Brown's outline: “Infancy narratives (1:1–2:52)”; “John the Baptist and the Genealogy of Jesus through his father (3:1–38)”; “Galilean Ministry (4:1–9:50)”; “Journey to Jerusalem (9:51–19:48)”; “From the Temple Cleansing to the Ascension (20:1–24:53)” (`brown-testimony-luke`, introduction [128]–[132]). The infancy stories and the journey are “the two most distinctive elements” ([133]); the journey is marked by travel notices (9:57; 10:38; 13:22; 14:25; 17:11; 18:31, 35; 19:1, 28; [134]). Olson runs the trek from 9:51 “until Jesus arrives at the outskirts of Jerusalem in Luke 19:28,” and reads it as “a journey of discipleship” rather than a route (`olson-final-trek` [8]–[9]). The Gospel opens in the temple (Zacharias, 1:9) and closes there (“continually in the temple,” 24:53); a story file leaves that as an open question for Luke 24.

**Verdict: keep the seven provisional divisions.** A division holds whole chapters, so the turn at 9:51 cannot open one: 50 of chapter 9's 62 verses are the Galilean ministry, so chapter 9 stays with Galilee and its blurb ends on the turn, as it does now. Brown's journey (9:51–19:48) is ten chapters, too long for one division; the provisional split at the end of chapter 13 falls at the lament over Jerusalem (13:34–35) and leaves two of about equal length. Brown begins the Galilean ministry at 4:1; the provisional `lukeprep` keeps chapter 4 with 3 instead, so that the baptism, the genealogy, the temptation and the sermon at Nazareth (the ministry announced, 4:18–21) open it together and chapter 5 begins the calling of disciples (the drafter's reading; either is defensible). Splitting Brown's last section at the end of chapter 21 separates the teaching in the temple from the Passion. The keys are used by no other book (checked against every `book.yaml`); the blurbs are in `book.yaml`.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks were copied from the page named. The rest is the drafter's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **Long chapters: save in pieces.** Matthew's writers of long chapters were cut off with “Output blocked by content filtering policy” before the chapter file was saved; a writer told to save in pieces finished at the first try (`GOSPELS.md`, “State”). **Every writer of Luke 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 18, 19, 20, 22, 23 and 24 (43 verses or more; 1 has 80 and 22 has 71) saves the chapter as a small skeleton first (the YAML frame, `setting`, section titles, no notes), then adds two or three notes at a time by Edit.** That is fifteen of twenty-four chapters. The coordinator gives this in the workflow's `focus` for those chapters; any writer may do it for any chapter.
- **Luke's own wording is the text.** A note quotes the verse it sits on; Matthew's or Mark's wording comes in only where the difference is the question (`GOSPELS.md`).
- **Repeated sayings and the sermon on the plain.** Brown: “An approach with promise holds to a view that Jesus utters similar words to separate audiences and adjusts his teachings to their needs” (introduction [136]; examples [137]–[142], [160]); the Lord's Prayer and the sayings on treasures appear “in a totally different setting, an observation that suggests another source and another occasion” ([157]). This is the `GOSPELS.md` rule above, and Luke's notes follow it. **The sermon on the plain is the exception the stories make.** `stories.yaml` joins Luke 6:20–49 to four Sermon on the Mount stories and also keeps it whole as Luke's own; the author's chronology has one event, “Sermon on the Mount/Plain” (Matthew 5–7 with Luke 6:20–49). Brown reads two sermons in two places ([155]): “the two sermons lack basic links to each other” ([156]); Matthew 7's `explore` calls it “The other sermon.” Luke 6's notes read the shared story files for what the sayings mean, and explain each saying in Luke's words and Luke's setting (the Twelve just chosen, the plain, the woes, 6:17–26); where the reader asks whether it is the same sermon, the note says once that the Gospels set it differently and that readers take it either way (Brown names the view that they are “variants of one another,” [154], and argues against it), with his reading attributed. It does not call Luke 6 “Luke's version of the Sermon on the Mount” in the site's voice. The `parallels` lines `stories.mjs` prints stand. **The author decided, 2026-11-21: two sermons** (asked whether Luke's sermon is the same as Matthew 5–7, “Two”), though the chronology joins them.
- **The text: King James and the Greek.** The text is the King James Version, whose Greek is the Textus Receptus (`greek.mjs luke <c>:<v>`; lexicon `thayer`). Modern editions differ from it in places a reader may ask about: 22:43–44 (below), the seventy or seventy-two at 10:1 (“But the number seventy is original,” `brown-testimony-luke`, ch. 10 [17]), and a few phrases Brown notes as missing from the earliest manuscript, P75 (ch. 22 [145], [307]). **Proposed:** a difference is mentioned only where the reader of the King James verse would ask, read from the SBL text on Bible Hub (`greek-nt`; `biblehub.com/texts/luke/<c>-<v>.htm` sets the editions side by side; it prints 22:43 and 23:34), and stated as what the manuscripts show, never as a claim that the King James verse is not scripture.
- **Dates and places.** The author's chronology is the order the site follows (`GOSPELS.md`). Luke says he writes “in order” (1:3), but his journey is “an extended, meandering journey” (Brown, introduction [134]) and he sets some events otherwise than Mark (the temple cleansed on the day of the entry, [149]). A year is given only from a source read, as that source's.
- **The Pharisees, the lawyers and the chief priests.** Matthew's convention holds: name the group the verse names, and never let a note's wording pass from a group in a scene to a people in every age. Luke has Jesus at table with Pharisees three times (7:36; 11:37; 14:1) and often names “lawyers” (7:30; 11:45–52; 14:3); a note says who they were from the Bible Dictionary or a study read. JST Luke 23:35 bears on whom Jesus forgave (below); no note turns it into a judgment of a people.
- **Luke's attention to women, the poor, Samaritans and Gentiles, prayer and the Holy Ghost.** The Bible Dictionary: Luke presents “Jesus as the universal Savior of both Jews and Gentiles,” is “favorable toward the Gentiles and also gives more stories involving women than do the other records” (`bd-gospels` [3]). Huntsman: Luke “records instances of healing women not preserved elsewhere,” Mary Magdalene and the women of 8:1–3 and the bent woman of 13:10–17 (`huntsman-lukes-jesus` [54]); Luke “consciously downplays the failings of others, particularly his disciples” ([62]). Brown's introduction treats property and money ([168]–[174]) and Gentiles ([91]–[97]); not read. Luke shows Jesus praying where the others do not (3:21; 5:16; 6:12; 9:18, 28–29). **Proposed:** these are what Luke's verses show and need no source to be noticed; what they mean is said only from a source read, and no note makes a theme of one in passing.
- **Titles of Jesus.** Huntsman (`huntsman-lukes-jesus`) reads Luke's Christology through “Savior, the anointed Servant of God, Redeemer, the Lord, and Son of God” ([13]). **Proposed:** a note on a title says what it means where it stands, from a source; the reading of the whole Gospel's Christology is Huntsman's, attributed.
- **Talmage.** *Jesus the Christ* is opened last, for what the other sources have not answered, and cited for what it adds (above). Find the chapter for a passage on its contents page; `talmage-jesus-christ-<chapter>` keys exist for 8–11, 14, 15, 17–24, 26, 27, 29–35 and 37.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each. The writer reads the source again before writing the note and quotes from the page.

- **Who Luke was, the “we” passages and his preface ([[Luke 1:1–4]]).** The Bible Dictionary: named three times (Col. 4:14; 2 Tim. 4:11; Philem. 1:24), writer of the Gospel and Acts; where Acts says “we,” “we can assume that Luke was Paul’s fellow-traveler”; “born of gentile parents”; his identification with a disciple on the road to Emmaus “is picturesque but historically unsupported”; JST 1:1 gives him “a high calling as a ‘messenger of Jesus Christ’” (`bd-luke` [1]). Brown: Theophilus is “likely the patron” (introduction [144]); the “we” passages are Acts 16:10–17; 20:5–21:18; 27:1–28:16 ([200]); Luke is “a second or third generation believer” (1:2; [199]); his medical vocabulary proves nothing, and his interest in medicine “does not push itself prominently into view” ([202]–[205]); a date “between AD 70 and 80 settles most easily within the evidence, all of which is circumstantial,” and the place is unknown ([197]–[198]); Huntsman, “sometime in the seventies or eighties” (`huntsman-lukes-jesus` [12]). The JST opens, “As I am a messenger of Jesus Christ, and knowing that many have taken in hand” (the footnote at 1:1, not in the appendix); by it “the Prophet Joseph emphasizes in clear fashion the value of Luke’s record” (Brown [98]). The title footnote: “The Testimony of St. Luke.” **Proposed:** chapter 1's `setting` says once what the Bible Dictionary says, with JST 1:1 as the answer to who is speaking; the note on 1:1–2 says what Luke says of his sources, with Brown's reading of Mark among the “many” ([146]). The physician is Col. 4:14's word, and nothing is built on it.
- **Luke among the Synoptics, for a Latter-day Saint reader.** Mark's brief's approach holds. Brown: “Most commentators hold that Luke copies much of Mark and a second source, ‘Q’”; he judges “that Luke draws directly from Mark only a little, almost as an afterthought” ([146]); of Q, “One major problem with this view has to do with the lack of copies of such a document from antiquity. None exist” ([153]); of Luke's own material, “we cannot hold that ‘L’ is a single, unified source” ([161]). Wayment argues that the Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi 12–14) challenges the claim that the Sermon on the Mount is a late composition (`wayment-q` [17]). **Proposed:** as for Mark: attributed, where the question needs it; nothing built on Q or L; nothing said in the site's voice about what Luke “did to” Mark; no date drawn from 21:20 (Jesus “can predict the future, even with high precision,” Brown [196]).
- **Gabriel, Elias and Noah ([[Luke 1:11–20]], [[Luke 1:26]]).** Joseph Smith, 1839: the priesthood was given to Adam, “he is Michael the Archangel spoken of in the scriptures— Then to Noah who is Gabriel he stands next in authority to Adam” (`jsp-discourse-1839-a`, p. 2, read on the page). The Bible Dictionary: Gabriel “is identified by latter-day revelation as Noah (HC 3:386)” (`bd-gabriel`); “Elias” has been applied to “Noah or Gabriel (Luke 1:11–20; D&C 27:6–7)” (`bd-elias` [3]). The Lord calls the messenger to Zacharias “Elias” ([[D&C 27:7]]); the footnote at 1:19 gives Dan. 8:16, D&C 27:7 and 128:21. Brown: “Gabriel, who is Noah” (ch. 1 [9]). **Proposed:** the note on 1:19 says who Gabriel is from Daniel, Joseph Smith's 1839 words (quoted from the Joseph Smith Papers, a report by a scribe, so “as reported”) and D&C 27:7, and that the Bible Dictionary explains the title Elias; Brown's reading of Noah's contrast with the Flood ([9]) is not needed.
- **The virgin birth and Mary ([[Luke 1:26–38]]).** Matthew's brief settles the virgin birth and Isaiah 7:14 (Matthew 1's notes link the Isaiah notes); Luke's notes link Matthew 1 for Isaiah rather than repeat it. Luke's own questions are Mary's: “How shall this be, seeing I know not a man?” (1:34; the footnote gives [[1 Ne. 11:18]] and [[Alma 7:10]]). Brown: *parthenos* here emphasizes “both Mary’s virtue and her honorable status” (ch. 1 [148]); the Book of Mormon knew her before her birth (1 Ne. 11; Mosiah 3:8; [188]); Elisabeth “stands as the first witness of the virgin birth” ([214]). **Proposed:** the note states the conception by the power of the Holy Ghost as scripture teaches it (1:35; Alma 7:10; 1 Ne. 11), and says no more about the manner than scripture or a prophet read on the page says. Mary is written of as Luke shows her (1:38, 1:46–55, 2:19, 2:51); the Magnificat's echoes of Hannah's song (1 Sam. 2:1–10) are stated from a source (Brown ch. 2 [168]; Huntsman [20]).
- **The census and Quirinius ([[Luke 2:1–2]]).** Brown: Quirinius “serves as legate of Syria from AD 6 to AD 7, too late for Jesus’ birth, which Matthew places during the reign of Herod—who dies in 4 BC”; “most scholars dismiss the notation here as historically inaccurate. But this view does not take into account that Herod … most likely adopts the Roman approach to census-taking” (ch. 2 [11]). The footnote glosses “taxing” as “enrollment.” The story file for the birth leaves the question open and points to Skinner (`skinner-life-jesus` [30]). **Proposed:** the note says what the decree and the enrollment were, states the difficulty plainly (Quirinius's known census was later), and gives the explanations a Latter-day Saint source offers as explanations, without settling it in the site's voice. It does not say Luke erred, and it does not say the problem is solved.
- **Jesus's growth ([[Luke 2:40]], [[Luke 2:46]], [[Luke 2:52]]).** The footnotes join 2:40 to JST Matthew 3:24–26 and 2:52 to D&C 93:13 (12–14): “he received not of the fulness at first, but continued from grace to grace, until he received a fulness.” The Joseph Smith Translation makes the doctors the listeners: “they were hearing him, and asking him questions” (JST Luke 2:46; appendix); Brown: “Jesus is not the learner but the teacher, even at a young age” (introduction [104]). **Proposed:** chapter 2 holds the two together, as Restoration scripture does: the boy taught the doctors (JST 2:46), and He grew “from grace to grace” (D&C 93:13). Mark's brief's approach to Jesus's humanity holds: explain the verse plainly, and bring D&C 93 in where it answers the verse's question.
- **The genealogy ([[Luke 3:23–38]]).** Matthew's brief settles it (`bd-genealogy`: two lines, “Matthew gives a legal descent … while Luke gives a natural descent,” followed as “probable”); Luke's notes follow it and link Matthew 1. Luke's own questions are in the story file's `open` list: “as was supposed” (3:23), the line through Nathan, Cainan (3:36), and Adam “the son of God” (3:38), which the Joseph Smith Translation expands (“who was formed of God, and the first man upon the earth,” JST Luke 3:45 in the footnote). Brown calls it “the Genealogy of Jesus through his father” (introduction [129]). **Proposed:** a note answers each from a source read; it does not reconcile the two lists name by name beyond what a source says.
- **The Seventy ([[Luke 10:1–24]]).** Only Luke tells their sending (`bd-gospels` [4]). Brown: the Seventy are “to be especial witnesses unto the Gentiles and in all the world” (D&C 107:25; ch. 10 [17]); in the modern Church they act “under the direction of the Twelve” (D&C 107:34) and Joseph F. Smith in 1907 called them “assistants to the twelve apostles” ([57], quoted by Brown: read the original before citing it as his). **Proposed:** chapter 10 explains the sending from the text; the latter-day Seventy are one sentence, from D&C 107:25, 34, and nothing about today's quorums is said from memory.
- **Hard sayings: “hate not his father” ([[Luke 14:26]]) and the unjust steward ([[Luke 16:1–13]]).** Brown: Jesus speaks “by hyperbole,” and “to hate” (*miseō*) “has to do chiefly with turning against whatever distracts a person from his or her primary loyalties” (ch. 14 [108]); the Joseph Smith Translation adds “or husband” and “or in other words, is afraid to lay down his life for my sake” (the footnote at 14:26; Brown [137]); Matthew 10:37 is the footnote's cross-reference. Of the steward, Brown: “the lord does not call the steward good, but wise or clever” (ch. 16 [53]); the parable turns to faithfulness with “the true riches” (16:10–11; [54]); the footnote at 16:9 gives D&C 82:22, which the writer reads on the page. **Proposed:** each note answers the question the hard words raise (does Jesus command hatred? does He praise dishonesty?) from the Greek, the JST and D&C 82:22, and gives an interpretive reading as its author's. Divorce at 16:18 follows Matthew's brief.
- **The rich man and Lazarus and the spirit world ([[Luke 16:19–31]]).** Brown: the story “casts an important and unexpected light on the workings of the world to come” (ch. 16 [10]); “in hell” is “in Hades,” “a place where the disembodied spirits temporarily await the resurrection and judgment,” corrected the same way in Alma 40:11–14 ([110]); the footnotes give [[Alma 40:11]] (11–21) at 16:22 and [[1 Ne. 15:28]] at 16:26. The Joseph Smith Translation adds a preface (JST Luke 16:16–23, appendix) that ends “I will liken you unto the rich man,” so the parable answers Pharisees who “have the law, and the prophets.” Joseph Smith, 11 June 1843, as reported: “Hades shaole paradise, spirits in prision is all one it is a world of spirits, the righteous & the wicked all go to the same world of spirits” (`ehat-cook-words-1843-06-11` [26]; Brown quotes a later form, [110]). **Proposed:** chapter 16 explains the parable as a parable (Brown's note that its frame “circulated in Egypt,” [10], only if the reader would ask), reads its world of spirits with Alma 40 and D&C 138 where the verse meets them, and, where the reader asks whether the “great gulf” is final, reads D&C 138 on the page (Brown sets 138:20–22, 29–32, 37 beside it, [118]) rather than leaving the parable as the last word. The JST preface is used where it answers why the parable is told.
- **“The kingdom of God is within you” ([[Luke 17:21]]).** The footnote: many translations read “among” “because the pronoun ‘you’ is plural here in Greek”; the JST reads “the kingdom of God has already come unto you” (appendix). Brown: *entos* is an adverb of place; “Jesus nowhere says that the kingdom resides inside a person, but that individuals reside in the kingdom”; “one of the preferred senses is ‘among you,’ that is, Jesus’ presence brings the kingdom,” with which the JST agrees (ch. 17 [103]); the footnote also gives [[D&C 65:2]]. **Proposed:** the note follows the JST and the plural: the kingdom had come among them in Jesus; it does not teach an inward kingdom as the verse's meaning. JST Luke 17:36–40 (the eagles as the gathering of the Saints, a new earth) is chapter 17's other JST question.
- **The angel and the bloody sweat ([[Luke 22:43–44]]).** The evidence: the verses are not in “the earliest manuscript (P75) or other important manuscripts”; a few manuscripts place them after Matthew 26:39; P69 omits 41–44; Justin, Irenaeus and probably Tatian knew them (`brown-testimony-luke`, ch. 22 [206]); the two verses always stand together ([226]). The KnoWhy: the passage's status “cannot be fully resolved at the present time,” but there are “enough reasons for one to cautiously accept their factual authenticity” (`sc-knowhy-520` [13]); it quotes Wayment that they “may be original to Luke’s Gospel but were perhaps omitted over doctrinal concerns” ([12]); *hōsei* (“as it were”) may mean real blood or a simile, and scholars are divided ([7]). Restoration scripture: “blood cometh from every pore” ([[Mosiah 3:7]]); “to bleed at every pore” ([[D&C 19:18]]); the footnote at 22:44 gives both, and the JST keeps “as it were great drops of blood.” Neither, the KnoWhy notes, does “explicitly say Jesus would sweat blood in Gethsemane” ([15]). Brown: “For Latter-day Saints, Jesus’ bleeding in Gethsemane is a fact” ([225]). The Gethsemane story file's `open` list has this question, with Huntsman and Hatch (`huntsman-thursday` [303]–[306]) as the lead. **Proposed:** chapter 22's notes treat 22:43–44 as scripture, as the Church's edition, the JST and Restoration scripture do. The note says once, plainly, that some early manuscripts lack the verses and that Latter-day Saint scholars judge they may well be original (Brown, Wayment through the KnoWhy), and then answers what the reader asks: what the angel and the agony mean, with Mosiah 3:7 (the angel's prophecy to Benjamin) and D&C 19:18 (the Savior's own words). It does not settle the medical question in the site's voice.
- **“Father, forgive them” ([[Luke 23:34]]).** The JST adds “(Meaning the soldiers who crucified him,)” (JST Luke 23:35, appendix; the footnote at 23:34). Brown: “Jesus’ forgiveness extends to the soldiers alone, not yet to ‘the rulers’” (ch. 23 [136]; introduction [105]); he also reads in Moses 7:38–39 a door left open for those who repent in the next life ([157]). The SBL text prints the prayer (Bible Hub, `texts/luke/23-34.htm`); no source read for this brief discusses its manuscripts, so a note says nothing about them unless a writer reads one that does. **Proposed:** the note gives the verse, the JST's gloss as Joseph Smith's answer to the reader's question whom He forgave, and Brown's reading of it, attributed; it does not draw from the gloss a judgment that the Jewish leaders, or the Jews, are beyond forgiveness (the convention above).
- **“To day shalt thou be with me in paradise” ([[Luke 23:43]]).** Joseph Smith, 11 June 1843, as reported: “what is Paradise it is a modern word it does not answer at all to the original that Jesus made use of … But it was this day I will be with thee in the world of spirits & will teach thee” (`ehat-cook-words-1843-06-11` [25]); another report has “for this day you will be with me in the world of Spi[ri]ts. & then I will teach you all about it” ([14]). The footnote gives John 20:17 and Alma 40:21 (11–14). Brown: *paradise* is “A Persian loanword that comes to mean a ‘garden’” (ch. 23 [178]); the promise is “immediate fellowship with him in the next world,” as D&C 138:11–19 illustrates ([177]). **Proposed:** the note quotes Joseph Smith's teaching (read in the Joseph Smith Papers transcript of the report if it loads; otherwise as Ehat and Cook print it), says what “paradise” means in Alma 40 and D&C 138, and does not say what became of the thief beyond that: he would be with the Lord in the world of spirits and be taught there. The story file for the crucifixion has this as an open question.
- **The risen body ([[Luke 24:39–43]]).** Brown: the Risen Jesus “offers, in negative terms, the proper nature of a resurrected body, that is, it consists of flesh and bones and is not a disembodied spirit” (ch. 24 [147]); Huntsman: Luke's resurrection narrative “is more effective in demonstrating the reality of an actual, bodily resurrection” (`huntsman-lukes-jesus` [82]). The footnotes give [[3 Ne. 11:14]] and [[D&C 129:2]]. **Proposed:** the note says what Luke shows (hands and feet, “handle me,” the fish and honeycomb) and what Restoration scripture adds (3 Ne. 11:14–15; D&C 129:1–3; D&C 130:22 for the Father and the Son, read on the page); the setting may say the KnoWhys on the resurrection and Emmaus (`sc-knowhy-665`, `sc-knowhy-677`) are where to go further.
- **Luke 21, Joseph Smith—Matthew and D&C 45.** Luke's discourse is set in the temple (21:5, 37) and has its own words: Jerusalem “compassed with armies” (21:20) and “the times of the Gentiles” (21:24). The JST divides it: “Now these things he spake unto them, concerning the destruction of Jerusalem. And then his disciples asked him, saying, Master, tell us concerning thy coming?” (JST Luke 21:24–26, appendix), and “this generation, the generation when the times of the Gentiles be fulfilled” (JST 21:32). Brown: Jesus gave some of these warnings earlier too (19:41–44; [140]–[142]); D&C 45 is the Lord's own report of the discourse, “I stood before them in the flesh” (D&C 45:16; [116]). **Proposed:** chapter 21's `setting` says that the Lord's fuller account is in Joseph Smith—Matthew and D&C 45:16–59, and links them; a note on a verse Luke shares with Joseph Smith—Matthew answers Luke's reader briefly and links that note. What is Luke's own (the armies, the times of the Gentiles, the JST division at 21:24) is written in full, with D&C 45:25–30 for the times of the Gentiles, read on the page.

## The Joseph Smith Translation in Luke

- **How heavy it is.** Joseph Smith changed 563 verses of Luke, more than any book but Genesis (`millet-js-matthew` [11], as Matthew's brief cites it). For Matthew 1 through John 5 he dictated the whole text, so the Synoptics have “many more small, clarifying, textual changes” than the rest of the New Testament (`jackson-jst-new-testament` [11]–[12]). Brown: “the most important alterations in Luke either add to or change the words of Jesus”; others fill in the background, interpret, or bridge one scene to the next (introduction [99]–[103]); his commentary discusses “all the important changes.” Brown numbers the JST verses as the Inspired Version and the Church's appendix do ([107]); quote them in that numbering.
- **What the reader already has.** The appendix has entries for chapters 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17, 18, 21, 23 and 24 (`jst-luke-<n>`, `…/scriptures/jst/jst-luke/<n>?lang=eng`; all fourteen read for this brief; the other ten chapters have no page). Among them: John's mission to the Gentiles and the poor from the treasury (3:4–11, 19–20); “the key of knowledge, the fullness of the scriptures” (11:53); the disciples' fear and the Lord coming in the first, second and third watch (12:9–12, 41–57); “Ye know not Moses, neither the prophets” (14:35–37); the eagles as the gathering (17:36–40); and those used under the questions above (2:46; 16:16–23; 17:21; 18:27; 21:24–26, 32; 23:35; 24:2–4). The footnotes give many more, among them 1:1, 3:45, 9:31 (“spake of his death, and also his resurrection”), 10:22–23, 11:4–6, 14:26, 22:16 and 23:31–32 (“signifying the scattering of Israel”).
- **How to use it.** Judiciously, where the change answers the reader's question (`STANDARDS.md` §1). It is “not the official Bible” of the Church but “an invaluable aid to biblical interpretation” (`bd-jst`). Where Brown or another study discusses a change, that is the source for what the change means; the change itself is cited from the appendix or the footnote (`lds-scriptures`). An unprinted reading is quoted from the manuscript (New Testament Revision 1 and 2, `jsp-nt1`, `jsp-nt2`) or a transcription read on a page; never argue from the Greek that a change is or is not what Luke wrote (the rule of Joseph Smith—Matthew's brief).
