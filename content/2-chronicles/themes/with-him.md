The second book of Chronicles begins, “And Solomon the son of David was strengthened in his kingdom, and the LORD his God was with him, and magnified him exceedingly” ([[2 Chr. 1:1]]). It ends with the proclamation of Cyrus king of Persia: “Who is there among you of all his people? The LORD his God be with him, and let him go up” ([[2 Chr. 36:23]]). “The LORD his God with him” is the same three Hebrew words in both, *YHWH ʾĕlōhāyw ʿimmô*, and neither sentence has a verb: “was” and “be” are the translators’, supplied from the setting, a narrator reporting in the one and a king proclaiming in the other.[@oshb-wlc] The phrase is not only at the two ends. It stands once between, of Asa ([[2 Chr. 15:9]]), seven verses after a prophet states the terms on which the LORD is with anyone at all.[@oshb-wlc]

What differs is who it is said of. At the start a narrator says it of Solomon, David’s son, on his throne. At the end a Persian king wishes it over “who is there among you of all his people,” and no son of David is named. The presence the book began by reporting has become a wish.

I read 1:1, the terms of 15:2, Hezekiah’s “with us is the LORD our God” (32:8) and 36:23 as one line: the presence stated of David’s son, the terms on which it is kept, its confession under siege, and its return as a wish over a people who had lost it. The terms themselves leave a way back, and the word of Jeremiah that the book’s last verses say was being accomplished puts the LORD’s visit before the people’s seeking. The book ends with the wish, spoken at the LORD’s own stirring, and I read that open wish as answered in Jesus Christ, Immanuel, “God with us,” in whom the LORD did not wait for His people to be with Him but came to be with them and save them from their sins. That reading is mine. In the Church’s materials and the Latter-day Saint scholarship I searched, I found none that joins 1:1, 15:2 and 36:23 or reads the book’s last wish as answered in Immanuel. Others have come near it: God is said to be “with” David’s house, the name Immanuel fits that house, and Matthew’s Gospel begins with “God with us” and ends with “I am with you alway.”[@rsc-combs-ahaz-sign] Nor do I say that Christ set aside the terms of 15:2. They stand; what changes is who moves first. (The notes at [15:2](/2-chronicles/15/) and [36:23](/2-chronicles/36/) treat each verse in its chapter.)

<h2 id="while-ye-be-with-him">“While ye be with him”</h2>

The first verse does not come from nowhere. David had spoken a wish over the son who would build the house: “Now, my son, the LORD be with thee; and prosper thou, and build the house of the LORD thy God” ([[1 Chr. 22:11]]). There the Hebrew does have the verb, *yĕhî*, “let [him] be.”[@oshb-wlc] The first verse of 2 Chronicles reports the wish granted (David’s charge is treated at [1 Chronicles 22](/1-chronicles/22/)).

Then the terms. The prophet Azariah meets Asa as he comes back from battle: “The LORD is with you, while ye be with him; and if ye seek him, he will be found of you; but if ye forsake him, he will forsake you” ([[2 Chr. 15:2]]). The sentence runs both ways, and “with” is on both sides of it: the LORD with them, they with Him. Its first half is seen at once. Strangers out of Ephraim, Manasseh and Simeon came over to Asa, “for they fell to him out of Israel in abundance, when they saw that the LORD his God was with him” ([[2 Chr. 15:9]]). The three words of 1:1 return, and this time the presence is something others can see.

The book measures its kings by these terms. Most of its “with him,” “with you” and “with us” is everyday, a congregation or a troop with a king;[@lds-scriptures] the places where the LORD is said to be with someone are fewer, and every one of them uses the preposition *ʿim*. “The LORD was with Jehoshaphat, because he walked in the first ways of his father David” ([[2 Chr. 17:3]]); “the LORD shall be with the good” ([[2 Chr. 19:11]]). And the other side has something else with it. Abijah tells the north, “there are with you golden calves” ([[2 Chr. 13:8]]), and “God himself is with us for our captain” ([[2 Chr. 13:12]]). A man of God sends Amaziah’s hired army home, “for the LORD is not with Israel” ([[2 Chr. 25:7]]), a verse that gives no reason (the note at [25:7](/2-chronicles/25/)).

The second half of 15:2 is carried out in the book’s own words. To Rehoboam: “Ye have forsaken me, and therefore have I also left you” ([[2 Chr. 12:5]]). To Judah under Joash: “because ye have forsaken the LORD, he hath also forsaken you” ([[2 Chr. 24:20]]). In Chronicles the LORD’s presence is real, it answers to the people’s, and it can be lost. (The “seek” in the middle of 15:2 is the subject of [“Seek My Face … and I Will Heal”](/2-chronicles/themes/seek-my-face/).)

<h2 id="with-us-is-the-lord-our-god">“With us is the LORD our God”</h2>

A father and a son faced the same empire. When Edom and the Philistines pressed him, “did king Ahaz send unto the kings of Assyria to help him” ([[2 Chr. 28:16]]), and the king of Assyria “came unto him, and distressed him, but strengthened him not” ([[2 Chr. 28:20]]). Chronicles never says that the LORD was with Ahaz: among the places where the LORD is said to be with someone, his reign has none. That is an observation from the list, not a sentence of the text.

What Chronicles does not tell of Ahaz, Isaiah does, and I bring it in from Isaiah, as the [chapter’s note on Christ](/2-chronicles/28/) does. The LORD offered Ahaz a sign, “Ask thee a sign of the LORD thy God” ([[Isa. 7:11]]). Ahaz answered, “I will not ask, neither will I tempt the LORD” ([[Isa. 7:12]]). Isaiah replied, “Therefore the Lord himself shall give you a sign; Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel” ([[Isa. 7:14]]). The name means “With us is God.”[@lds-scriptures][@bowen-ominous-onomastics] It is a fitting name in David’s house, of which God is often said to be “with.”[@rsc-combs-ahaz-sign] The sign of God’s being with them was given to a king who had not been with Him and would not ask for it.

Then Hezekiah, with Assyria at the walls: “there be more with us than with him: With him is an arm of flesh; but with us is the LORD our God to help us, and to fight our battles” ([[2 Chr. 32:7–8]]). He does not say the name Immanuel. His words are *wĕʿimmānû YHWH ʾĕlōhênû*, “and with us the LORD our God”; Isaiah’s are *ʿimmānû ʾēl*, “with us God.”[@oshb-wlc] “With us” is the shared word, and the names of God differ. But Isaiah had spoken of this same Assyrian flood, that it would “fill the breadth of thy land, O Immanuel” ([[Isa. 8:8]]), and that the nations’ counsel “shall not stand: for God is with us” ([[Isa. 8:10]]); and under Ahaz’s son Hezekiah the land of Judah was indeed invaded by Assyria, as 2 Chronicles 32 tells.[@rsc-combs-ahaz-sign] There are reasons to think that Isaiah’s Immanuel was Hezekiah himself: the sign was spoken to Ahaz and his house, 2 Kings says of Hezekiah that “the Lord was with him” ([[2 Kgs. 18:7]]), and Isaiah 8:8 gives the land of Judah to Immanuel in the very invasion that came in Hezekiah’s reign.[@rsc-combs-ahaz-sign] Yet no definitive conclusion can be reached about who Immanuel was in Isaiah’s own day,[@rsc-combs-ahaz-sign] and nothing here is built on making Hezekiah that child. I read his speech as Isaiah’s confidence on a king’s lips in the event Isaiah spoke of: the son says of the LORD what the father would not ask to be shown. (The arm of flesh is treated in the note at [32:8](/2-chronicles/32/).)

<h2 id="who-is-there-among-you">“Who is there among you of all his people?”</h2>

The presence was never the dynasty’s possession. When Josiah went out against Pharaoh Necho, Necho warned him to “forbear thee from meddling with God, who is with me, that he destroy thee not” ([[2 Chr. 35:21]]), and the narrator calls the message “the words of Necho from the mouth of God” ([[2 Chr. 35:22]]; the note at [35:21](/2-chronicles/35/)). Josiah is the book’s reforming king, and this does not undo him. It shows only that God could be with a pharaoh on a given day and that a faithful son of David could stand on the wrong side of it.

Then the forsaking comes. Huldah’s word in Josiah’s day was “they have forsaken me” ([[2 Chr. 34:25]]), and in the last reign the people “mocked the messengers of God, and despised his words, and misused his prophets, until the wrath of the LORD arose against his people, till there was no remedy” ([[2 Chr. 36:16]]; see [“Till there was no remedy”](/2-chronicles/themes/seek-my-face/#no-remedy)).

But Azariah’s terms never ended in forsaking. In the verses after 15:2 he told what had happened when Israel was without God: “But when they in their trouble did turn unto the LORD God of Israel, and sought him, he was found of them” ([[2 Chr. 15:4]]). And the book shows the LORD keeping David’s house through a king who had forsaken Him. Of Jehoram it says, “he had forsaken the LORD God of his fathers” ([[2 Chr. 21:10]]), and yet “the LORD would not destroy the house of David, because of the covenant that he had made with David” ([[2 Chr. 21:7]]). Forsaking was a judgment, not the last word.

The book ends where Jeremiah’s word pointed: “The LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia,” that the word spoken by Jeremiah might be accomplished ([[2 Chr. 36:22]]). That word is the seventy years of exile ([[Jer. 25:11]]; [[Jer. 29:10]]),[@cambridge-chronicles] and Jeremiah’s letter to the exiles gives its order. First the LORD acts: “after seventy years be accomplished at Babylon I will visit you, and perform my good word toward you, in causing you to return to this place” ([[Jer. 29:10]]). Then the people: “Then shall ye call upon me, and ye shall go and pray unto me, and I will hearken unto you. And ye shall seek me, and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart. And I will be found of you, saith the LORD” ([[Jer. 29:12–14]]). The last words are Azariah’s promise, “he will be found of you,” in the LORD’s own voice. The terms stand, and seeking is still how He is found; but the visit and the return come first.

The book’s last sentence is the wish. Three things in it come from the text. First, the words are those of 1:1, the same three Hebrew words, with the divine name. Ezra, which gives the same decree, has “his God be with him” ([[Ezra 1:3]]), with the verb *yĕhî* and without the name;[@oshb-wlc] the difference has been put down to an error in copying,[@cambridge-ezra] and nothing here rests on which wording came first. Second, the one the words are spoken over is no longer the king but anyone of the people who will go. Third, the move is the LORD’s. He stirred up Cyrus, and the chapter says nothing of the people turning first. In the Hebrew order of the books, these are the last words of the Old Testament.[@cambridge-chronicles]

I take this as mercy that moves first, in the order Jeremiah’s word sets, and it is the order Isaiah shows at Ahaz’s court: the sign of “God with us” given to a king who would not ask, and the wish for the LORD’s presence spoken over exiles who had not yet gone anywhere.

A wish is not yet a presence, and a Persian king cannot grant what he wishes. The LORD did answer it in His own voice, “I am with you, saith the LORD” ([[Hag. 1:13]]; see [“Shall It Be Holy?”](/haggai/themes/shall-it-be-holy/)), but He spoke it to the remnant who had already gone up, after they “obeyed the voice of the LORD their God” ([[Hag. 1:12]]), and then they “came and did work in the house” ([[Hag. 1:14]]). That answer was given to people already with Him, on the terms of 15:2. (Haggai’s “with” is the other preposition, *ʾittĕkem*,[@oshb-wlc] so nothing here is built on the word.) What I read in Immanuel is the LORD coming to a people not yet with Him.

<h2 id="god-with-us">“God with us”</h2>

The Gospel of Matthew begins with “Jesus Christ, the son of David” ([[Matt. 1:1]]) and gives His names within its first chapter. The angel says, “thou shalt call his name Jesus: for he shall save his people from their sins” ([[Matt. 1:21]]), and Matthew adds Isaiah’s name: “they shall call his name Emmanuel, which being interpreted is, God with us” ([[Matt. 1:23]]). Immanuel is one of the names of Jesus Christ,[@gs-immanuel] and it signifies that “Christ as God will be born into mortality of a virgin and will be among the people to save and redeem them.”[@bd-immanuel]

Read with Chronicles, the name says of this Son of David what the book said of David’s sons, and says more. The LORD was with Solomon. Jesus Christ is the LORD with us. And He comes on the far side of the forsaking, to a people whose need is to be saved from their sins. Abinadi taught “that God himself shall come down among the children of men, and shall redeem his people” ([[Mosiah 15:1]]), and Paul that “God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us” ([[Rom. 5:8]]). The order of 15:2 is not undone. It is preceded: He is with us first.

At the dedication of the temple Solomon asked, “But will God in very deed dwell with men on the earth?” ([[2 Chr. 6:18]]). His “with” is *ʾēt*, not the *ʿim* of the book’s sentences of presence,[@oshb-wlc] but the question is the book’s own, and I read John as answering it: “the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us” ([[John 1:14]]).

Matthew ends his Gospel as he began it.[@rsc-combs-ahaz-sign] The risen Lord says, “lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world” ([[Matt. 28:20]]). What Cyrus could only wish, He says in the first person, because He is the one wished for. The two endings are alike in their words as well: a king to whom the LORD had given “all the kingdoms of the earth” sends whoever will to go up, with a wish for God’s presence; the Lord to whom “all power is given” sends His disciples with “Go ye therefore” and promises His own ([[Matt. 28:18–19]]). The likeness is in the words; I claim nothing about what Matthew drew on, and Cyrus is no figure of Christ.

<h2 id="always-have-his-spirit">“That they may always have his Spirit to be with them”</h2>

I read the sacrament prayer as keeping the terms Azariah gave, with the condition put first. Those who are willing to take upon them the name of the Son, and “always remember him and keep his commandments which he has given them,” are prayed for “that they may always have his Spirit to be with them” ([[D&C 20:77]]). The gift of the Holy Ghost “includes the right to constant companionship,” and partaking of the sacrament renews “our qualification for the promise.”[@oaks-always-have-his-spirit] The right is real, and so is the condition: “Even though we have a right to His constant companionship, the Spirit of the Lord will dwell with us only when we keep the commandments.”[@oaks-always-have-his-spirit] I read the Savior’s own words as the same two-sided form: “If a man love me, he will keep my words: and my Father will love him, and we will come unto him, and make our abode with him” ([[John 14:23]]).

So a disciple is with Him by covenant and has Him by promise. He came first, to people who were yet sinners, and the being-with that the prayer asks for answers a presence already given. A disciple who has not been with Him stands where the people of 36:23 stood, over whom the words were spoken before they had gone anywhere. The way back to His company is open at His doing, and it is still a going up.
