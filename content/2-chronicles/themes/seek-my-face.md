After the temple was dedicated, the LORD appeared to Solomon by night and gave him a promise in one sentence: “If my people, which are called by my name, shall humble themselves, and pray, and seek my face, and turn from their wicked ways; then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land” ([[2 Chr. 7:14]]). Four things the people do, three things the LORD does (the note at [7:14](/2-chronicles/7/)). One of the things asked, seek, runs through both books of Chronicles. The last thing promised, heal, is the second book’s word alone: its Hebrew root does not occur in 1 Chronicles.[@oshb-wlc]

They do not travel at the same pace. Chronicles tells of seeking often, and in the second book those who seek the LORD find Him. Healing is rare. The Hebrew root of “heal,” *rpʾ*, occurs six times in 2 Chronicles, and only once is the LORD said to heal: at Hezekiah’s Passover, “the LORD hearkened to Hezekiah, and healed the people” ([[2 Chr. 30:20]]).[@oshb-wlc] The people He healed had humbled themselves, turned and set their hearts to seek Him, but they had not been cleansed as the law required, and the king prayed that “the good LORD” would pardon them, with a verb the book uses elsewhere of making an atonement. With God as its subject the verb means “cover, i.e. treat as covered”: God “may himself provide an atonement or covering for men and their sins which could not be provided by men.”[@bdb] The last time the root appears, there is “no remedy” ([[2 Chr. 36:16]]).

I read that one scene as showing where the healing of 7:14 comes from. It does not come from the seeking, though the seeking is asked and is given. It comes from the LORD who provides the atonement, and scripture names Him: Jesus Christ, with whose stripes we are healed. The sequence is my reading. In the Church’s materials and the Latter-day Saint scholarship I searched, I found none that reads 7:14, the Passover and 36:16 together or ties them to Christ as healer. The nearest are the observation that the mercy of that Passover is “of a piece with the way Jesus healed on the Sabbath,”[@davis-bended-rules] and the listing of the Passover under healing beside “with his stripes we are healed” and the “Sun of righteousness” who rises “with healing in his wings.”[@tg-heal]

Nor do I say the promise was kept only once. When Rehoboam and his princes humbled themselves, the LORD granted “some deliverance” ([[2 Chr. 12:7]]); He heard Manasseh’s supplication ([[2 Chr. 33:13]]) and Josiah’s humbling ([[2 Chr. 34:27]]). The promise’s “hear” is kept more than once. Its “heal”, on the reading given here, is kept once.

<h2 id="the-due-order">“We sought him not after the due order”</h2>

The first book of Chronicles begins its story with a death explained by seeking. Saul died “for asking counsel of one that had a familiar spirit, to inquire of it; And inquired not of the LORD: therefore he slew him” ([[1 Chr. 10:13–14]]). Both “inquire” and “inquired” are the Hebrew verb *dāraš*,[@oshb-wlc] which the King James Version gives as “inquire” or “seek.” Saul sought, but not the LORD.

David proposes to bring back the ark, “for we inquired not at it in the days of Saul” ([[1 Chr. 13:3]]), again *dāraš*.[@oshb-wlc] The first attempt ends with Uzza dead beside the cart, and when David tries again he names what went wrong in the same verb: “the LORD our God made a breach upon us, for that we sought him not after the due order” ([[1 Chr. 15:13]]). “After the due order” is one Hebrew word, *kammišpāṭ*.[@oshb-wlc] The text names the act and the order, not a motive in Uzza (the notes at [13:10](/1-chronicles/13/) and [15:13](/1-chronicles/15/)), and I add nothing to them. What matters here is David’s diagnosis: the LORD was sought, and sought wrongly, and the result was a breach.

The verb’s other uses in the book run the same way. *Dāraš* occurs eleven times in ten verses of 1 Chronicles.[@oshb-wlc] It is used of seeking not done (10:14; 13:3), of seeking turned to a familiar spirit or done out of order (10:13; 15:13), and of seeking David did not dare: at the threshing floor he “could not go before it to inquire of God: for he was afraid because of the sword of the angel of the LORD” ([[1 Chr. 21:30]]). One use is a census (26:31). The rest are commands or conditions (16:11; 22:19; 28:8–9). In no verse of 1 Chronicles does the narrator say that anyone did seek the LORD, with this verb or with the other verb for seeking, *bāqaš*.[@oshb-wlc]

That is a pattern of one verb, not of a silent heaven. Twice before battle “David inquired of God,” and God answered him ([[1 Chr. 14:10]]; [[1 Chr. 14:14]]); the verb there is *šāʾal*, to ask.[@oshb-wlc] And on the same threshing floor David “called upon the LORD; and he answered him from heaven by fire upon the altar of burnt offering” ([[1 Chr. 21:26]]). The God of 1 Chronicles answers. What the book does not narrate, with either verb of seeking, is a seeking of Him done as He ordained.

What it hands on instead is the command. In David’s psalm: “Seek the LORD and his strength, seek his face continually” ([[1 Chr. 16:11]]). The first “seek” is *dāraš*, the second *bāqaš*,[@oshb-wlc] and “seek his face” is the phrase the LORD will use to Solomon. The verb of 7:14 is *bāqaš*, not *dāraš*;[@oshb-wlc] the two are different words, and Chronicles sets them side by side, here and again in Asa’s reign, where the promise “if ye seek him, he will be found of you” has *dāraš* ([[2 Chr. 15:2]]) and the report that the people “sought him … and he was found of them” has *bāqaš* ([[2 Chr. 15:15]]).[@oshb-wlc] David tells the princes, “set your heart and your soul to seek the LORD your God” ([[1 Chr. 22:19]]), and tells Solomon, “the LORD searcheth all hearts, and understandeth all the imaginations of the thoughts: if thou seek him, he will be found of thee” ([[1 Chr. 28:9]]). “Searcheth” is *dāraš* too, with the LORD as its subject;[@oshb-wlc] the clause that follows shows what it means, that He examines hearts.

So the first book leaves three things standing: seeking the LORD is commanded, it has an order, and the one time the book says the order was missed, the LORD made a breach.

<h2 id="the-physicians">“Not to the LORD, but to the physicians”</h2>

In 2 Chronicles people do seek the LORD, and He is found of them. Asa commands Judah “to seek the LORD God of their fathers” ([[2 Chr. 14:4]]); the prophet Azariah tells him, “if ye seek him, he will be found of you” ([[2 Chr. 15:2]]); and the people “sought him with their whole desire; and he was found of them” ([[2 Chr. 15:15]]). Jehoshaphat, Uzziah, Hezekiah and Josiah are all said to seek (17:4; 26:5; 31:21; 34:3). Seeking is not what fails in this book.

The healing word is another matter. After 7:14 it comes back three times before the Passover, and in none of them does the LORD heal. Asa, the king of the seeking chapters, ends this way: “in his disease he sought not to the LORD, but to the physicians” ([[2 Chr. 16:12]]). “Physicians” is the participle of *rpʾ*, the healers.[@oshb-wlc] What the verse faults is the seeking Asa left undone (the note at [16:12](/2-chronicles/16/)), not the physicians. Of Jehoram, “the LORD smote him in his bowels with an incurable disease” ([[2 Chr. 21:18]]): in Hebrew, a disease *lĕʾên marpēʾ*, “to no healing,”[@oshb-wlc] where *marpēʾ* is the noun “healing, cure.”[@bdb] And Joram of Israel “returned to be healed in Jezreel because of the wounds” of battle ([[2 Chr. 22:6]]), an ordinary use.[@oshb-wlc]

Four of the six uses of the root are now behind us, and the LORD has healed no one.

<h2 id="the-good-lord-pardon">“The good LORD pardon”</h2>

Hezekiah sends letters through all Israel, and their word is 7:14’s “turn”: “turn again unto the LORD … and he will return to the remnant of you” ([[2 Chr. 30:6]]); “the LORD your God is gracious and merciful, and will not turn away his face from you, if ye return unto him” ([[2 Chr. 30:9]]). Both are *šûb*.[@oshb-wlc] Most of the north “laughed them to scorn, and mocked them” ([[2 Chr. 30:10]]). “Nevertheless divers of Asher and Manasseh and of Zebulun humbled themselves, and came to Jerusalem” ([[2 Chr. 30:11]]), with 7:14’s verb for “humble themselves.”[@oshb-wlc]

Then the scene itself: “a multitude of the people … had not cleansed themselves, yet did they eat the passover otherwise than it was written. But Hezekiah prayed for them, saying, The good LORD pardon every one That prepareth his heart to seek God, the LORD God of his fathers, though he be not cleansed according to the purification of the sanctuary. And the LORD hearkened to Hezekiah, and healed the people” ([[2 Chr. 30:18–20]]).

The verbs of 7:14 are here, but not all of them, and not all in the same hands. The people were called to turn, and those who came had humbled themselves. “Prayed” is 7:14’s verb, but it is Hezekiah who prays, “for them.”[@oshb-wlc] “Seek” is *dāraš*, not 7:14’s *bāqaš*.[@oshb-wlc] “Hearkened” is 7:14’s “hear,” and at the end of the feast the priests’ prayer “came up to his holy dwelling place, even unto heaven” ([[2 Chr. 30:27]]).[@oshb-wlc] “Healed” is *rpʾ*.[@oshb-wlc] Where 7:14 has “forgive,” Hezekiah’s word is *yĕkappēr*, translated “pardon.” It is the verb of the chapter before, where the priests killed the offerings and “made reconciliation with their blood upon the altar, to make an atonement for all Israel” ([[2 Chr. 29:24]]), and these are its only two uses in 2 Chronicles.[@oshb-wlc] There the priests make the atonement with blood. Here the one asked to make it is “the good LORD.” With God as its subject the verb means “cover, i.e. treat as covered, view propitiously,” and 30:18 is among the verses read so: God “may himself provide an atonement or covering for men and their sins which could not be provided by men.”[@bdb] This is the one place in the book where the LORD is said to heal,[@oshb-wlc] and I take it as the place where the healing promised in 7:14 is given.

Read beside 1 Chronicles 15:13, the scene answers the breach. Both are seeking with *dāraš* that was not done by the rule: “we sought him not after the due order”; “otherwise than it was written,” “not cleansed according to the purification of the sanctuary.” One ended in a breach and the other in healing. I do not explain Uzza’s death by this; the text gives no reason beyond the act. But the Passover names three things the ark’s first journey does not. The people had hearts “prepared” to seek God. A king prayed for them. And what they lacked, the LORD Himself was asked to cover. “Prepared” is the measure Chronicles uses of Rehoboam in the negative, who “prepared not his heart to seek the LORD” ([[2 Chr. 12:14]]), and hearts are what the LORD “searcheth” ([[1 Chr. 28:9]]).

The order did not stop mattering. Uzziah, who went into the temple to burn incense, was struck at the altar: “the leprosy even rose up in his forehead before the priests in the house of the LORD, from beside the incense altar” ([[2 Chr. 26:19]]). And this same Passover was kept by priests and Levites who “were ashamed, and sanctified themselves” ([[2 Chr. 30:15]]), while the Levites killed the lambs “for every one that was not clean, to sanctify them unto the LORD” ([[2 Chr. 30:17]]). Nor did the crowd come with nothing: they humbled themselves, came and sought. What they lacked was the purification, and that is the gap the prayer asks the LORD to close. “The Lord honored Hezekiah’s request and ‘healed the people.’”[@scripture-helps-2-chronicles]

What “healed” means here is read more than one way, since the people are called unclean, not sick (the note at [30:20](/2-chronicles/30/)). One Latter-day Saint reading takes it as the healing of “spiritual wounds—most of them no doubt known only to God” left by years of estrangement.[@davis-bended-rules] An older reading supposed that sickness and then death would otherwise have followed; another, rejecting it, takes sin “as a spiritual disease,” so that the word means “healing the soul.”[@keil-chronicles] Nothing here turns on choosing among them. On any of them, the healing is the LORD’s act, done in answer to a prayer for atonement on behalf of people who sought Him.

<h2 id="no-remedy">“Till there was no remedy”</h2>

The book’s end names two of the four conditions as refused. Zedekiah “humbled not himself before Jeremiah the prophet speaking from the mouth of the LORD” ([[2 Chr. 36:12]]) and “hardened his heart from turning unto the LORD God of Israel” ([[2 Chr. 36:13]]), 7:14’s “humble” and “turn.”[@oshb-wlc]

The LORD’s side is still there: He “sent to them by his messengers, rising up betimes, and sending; because he had compassion on his people, and on his dwelling place” ([[2 Chr. 36:15]]). “But they mocked the messengers of God, and despised his words, and misused his prophets, until the wrath of the LORD arose against his people, till there was no remedy” ([[2 Chr. 36:16]]). “No remedy” is *ʿad lĕʾên marpēʾ*, “until there was no healing,” Jehoram’s phrase now spoken of the nation.[@oshb-wlc] It is the root’s last use in the book.

The promise of 7:14 was to heal “their land”; the Passover healed “the people.” No verse of Chronicles says the land was healed. It lay desolate “until the land had enjoyed her sabbaths” ([[2 Chr. 36:21]]), and the book closes with a summons to the exiles, “let him go up” ([[2 Chr. 36:23]]). That is my observation of the text; I found no source that makes it.

Jeremiah, whom 36:12 and 36:21 both name, says both things. He laments, “why hast thou smitten us, and there is no healing for us?” ([[Jer. 14:19]]), with the same noun (the note at [36:16](/2-chronicles/36/)). And of the besieged city the LORD says through him, “Behold, I will bring it health and cure, and I will cure them, and will reveal unto them the abundance of peace and truth” ([[Jer. 33:6]]). “Cure” is *marpēʾ* again, and “I will cure them” is the verb of 7:14.[@oshb-wlc] The chapter is the one in which the LORD promises, “will I cause the Branch of righteousness to grow up unto David” ([[Jer. 33:15]]), and whose heading reads, “The Branch of Righteousness (the Messiah) is promised.”[@lds-scriptures]

I read Jeremiah 33:6 as the answer to 36:16. It does not soften the judgment on the generation that mocked the messengers. What it shows is that when Chronicles closes on “no healing,” the healing is still promised, and promised by the LORD as His own act, in the same breath as the Messiah.

<h2 id="with-his-stripes">“With his stripes we are healed”</h2>

The LORD who promised Solomon to hear, forgive and heal is Jehovah, and “Jehovah is the premortal Jesus Christ.”[@bd-jehovah] His voice, speaking out of the darkness to the Nephites spared in the destruction, before He came to them,[@lds-scriptures] made the same offer, in 7:14’s terms of turning and healing: “will ye not now return unto me, and repent of your sins, and be converted, that I may heal you?” ([[3 Ne. 9:13]]).

Scripture says how He heals. “He was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed” ([[Isa. 53:5]]). Malachi’s “Sun of righteousness” rises “with healing in his wings” ([[Mal. 4:2]]), and Nephi places that rising after the cross: “they will crucify him; and after he is laid in a sepulchre for the space of three days he shall rise from the dead, with healing in his wings” ([[2 Ne. 25:13]]).

Read together with the Passover, these verses show what I take the scene to mean. Hezekiah asked the LORD Himself to provide the atonement for people who had sought Him and were not clean, and the LORD healed them. What Hezekiah asked the LORD to do for them, Jesus Christ did in His own person: He made the atonement, and the healing came through His wounds. That joining is mine; what the verses say of Him is theirs. It explains why the one healing the book gives to the LORD comes where He is asked to atone, and why the book can end at “no remedy” with the remedy still promised. The healing was never in the seeking. It was in the One sought.

He also moves first. The LORD of Chronicles “sent to them by his messengers, rising up betimes” ([[2 Chr. 36:15]]), and “the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost” ([[Luke 19:10]]; see [The Law and the Lost Sheep](/psalms/themes/the-law-and-the-lost-sheep/)).

<h2 id="him-that-seeketh">“Him that seeketh so to do”</h2>

The four conditions of 7:14 are still how one comes to Him, and they are still asked: “Draw near unto me and I will draw near unto you; seek me diligently and ye shall find me” ([[D&C 88:63]]). They do not do the healing. He does.

A disciple who comes with a heart set to seek Him, and without everything yet in order, is not turned away for it. The Lord gives His gifts “for the benefit of those who love me and keep all my commandments, and him that seeketh so to do” ([[D&C 46:9]]), and “the Lord requireth the heart and a willing mind” ([[D&C 64:34]]). He knows how to heal such a person because He took the wound Himself: He would “take upon him the pains and the sicknesses of his people,” and “take upon him their infirmities, that his bowels may be filled with mercy, according to the flesh, that he may know according to the flesh how to succor his people according to their infirmities” ([[Alma 7:11–12]]).
