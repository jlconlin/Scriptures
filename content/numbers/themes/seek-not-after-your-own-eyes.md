At Kadesh the LORD told Moses, “Send thou men, that they may search the land of Canaan” (13:2), and Moses told the twelve, “see the land, what it is” (13:18). Ten of them came back with a report that ended on themselves: “we were in our own sight as grasshoppers, and so we were in their sight” (13:33). The forty days of searching became forty years. The chapter of laws that follows the sentence closes with a tassel on the corner of a garment, “that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them; and that ye seek not after your own heart and your own eyes, after which ye use to go a whoring” (15:39).

The law of the fringe answers the spies in their own words: its “look upon,” “seek” and “eyes” are the words of 13:2, 18 and 33, and its “go a whoring” is the root of “your whoredoms” in the sentence (14:33).[@oshb-wlc] The reading is not new. Rashi glosses the verb by the spies’ “searching” (13:25) and calls the heart and the eyes the “spies” of the body, and a modern rabbinic study argues that the fringe answers the spies’ seeing in their own words.[@rashi-numbers][@bazak-explore] No Latter-day Saint source I read makes it, and the Church’s edition has no footnote at 15:39.[@lds-scriptures] What I add is to read the fringe with the one other thing in Numbers the LORD gives all Israel to look upon, the serpent of brass (21:8–9). Read together, they are the two looks the LORD gives all Israel in the book: the first so that Israel may remember, the second so that the bitten may live. Scripture says what the second asked: “the labor which they had to perform was to look” (1 Ne. 17:41). Jesus named Himself the thing lifted up as the serpent was (John 3:14–15). Eyes that had measured by themselves are given, by command, something to look at.

## “See the land, what it is”

The searching was the LORD’s command before it was anyone’s fault: “Moses by the commandment of the LORD sent them” (13:3). The verb, *tûr*, is the word of these chapters; twelve of its fourteen uses in Numbers are in chapters 13 and 14.[@oshb-wlc] Nor was looking the fault. Joshua and Caleb were “of them that searched the land” (14:6), and they reported in the same verb: “The land, which we passed through to search it, is an exceeding good land” (14:7). All twelve, in Elder Kevin R. Duncan’s words, “had witnessed the same benefits from and the same obstacles,” but “ten members of the group saw only the problems, while the other two placed their trust in God.”[@duncan-rebel-not]

What the ten got wrong was the measure. Their last sentence is about how they looked to themselves, and the Hebrew says “in our eyes … in their eyes,” with no word for “own.”[@oshb-wlc] Keil renders it “we seemed to ourselves and to them as small as grasshoppers.”[@keil-numbers]

The punishment is described in the same language of sight. Israel had not lacked things to see: the glory of the LORD appeared “before all the children of Israel” that day (14:10), and the LORD’s complaint is that they do not believe Him “for all the signs which I have shewed among them” (14:11). So it falls on those who had seen: “all those men which have seen my glory, and my miracles … Surely they shall not see the land which I sware unto their fathers” (14:22–23). The Church’s footnote at “see” leads to Moses, who “sought diligently to sanctify his people that they might behold the face of God; But they hardened their hearts and could not endure his presence” (D&C 84:23–24).[@lds-scriptures] And the length of the sentence is counted in the spies’ verb: “After the number of the days in which ye searched the land, even forty days, each day for a year” (14:34). The notes on [Numbers 13:33](/numbers/13/) and [Numbers 14:20](/numbers/14/) carry the rest of the story.

## “That ye seek not after your own heart and your own eyes”

The chapter of laws that follows the sentence closes with the fringe (15:37–41), and four of its words come back from the story of the spies. “That ye may look upon it” is *ûrĕʾîtem*, the same form as Moses’ “see” in 13:18, and the form stands in Numbers only in those two verses. “That ye seek not” is *lōʾ tāturû*, the spies’ *tûr*, and its last use in Numbers. “Your eyes” is *ʿênêkem*; the King James supplies “own” here as in 13:33. And “ye use to go a whoring” is *zōnîm*, from the root of *zĕnûtêkem*, “your whoredoms,” which the children were to bear for forty years (14:33); in Numbers the root stands only there, at 15:39 and at 25:1.[@oshb-wlc]

The echo should not be pressed beyond the text. *Tûr* is not only the spies’ verb. Its first use in Numbers is the ark’s, which went before Israel “to search out a resting place for them” (10:33), and the LORD Himself is its subject when He goes before Israel “to search you out a place to pitch your tents in” (Deut. 1:33). The lexicon gives three senses: “seek out, select” for the ark and the LORD, “spy out, explore” for the spies, and puts 15:39 under a third, “go about, figurative.”[@bdb] The law does not forbid searching. It forbids searching “after your own heart and your own eyes,” and it names the eyes the ten measured themselves by.

In place of the forbidden search the law gives a thing to look at that turns the eyes to the LORD’s words. The fringes were commanded “so that when they looked upon the fringes they would be reminded of the commandments of the Lord,” and a fringe on clothing “symbolized that an individual is clothed, or covered, with the commandments of God.”[@ot-manual-numbers-13-36] The next verses say whose commandments they were: “That ye may remember, and do all my commandments, and be holy unto your God. I am the LORD your God, which brought you out of the land of Egypt” (15:40–41). The note on [Numbers 15:39](/numbers/15/) has the older readings of the verse.

## “When he looketh upon it, shall live”

The second of these looks comes later in the book and after a different sin. On the way around Edom the people “spake against God, and against Moses,” and the LORD sent fiery serpents, “and much people of Israel died” (21:5–6). They confessed and asked Moses to pray; the page [Pray unto the LORD](/numbers/themes/pray-unto-the-lord/) follows that prayer. The answer did not take the serpents away. “Make thee a fiery serpent, and set it upon a pole: and it shall come to pass, that every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live” (21:8). “When he looketh upon it” is *wĕrāʾâ ʾōtô*, the verb and the “it” of the fringe’s *ûrĕʾîtem ʾōtô*, “that ye may look upon it”; in 21:9 “beheld” is another verb, *wĕhibbîṭ*.[@oshb-wlc] No commentary I read remarks on the change.

Numbers does not say what the look required. Keil supplies it: whoever “looked at the brazen serpent with faith in the promise of God, lived.”[@keil-numbers] The Book of Mormon says it outright. Nephi: “he prepared a way that they might be healed; and the labor which they had to perform was to look; and because of the simpleness of the way, or the easiness of it, there were many who perished” (1 Ne. 17:41). Alma: “there were many who were so hardened that they would not look, therefore they perished. Now the reason they would not look is because they did not believe that it would heal them” (Alma 33:20). The brass did not heal; the symbol “itself was powerless,” and “it was the Lord’s power that was manifest in the healing.”[@sc-knowhy-721] A Scripture Central study suggests that Israel would likely have understood that in looking to the serpent they “were actually looking upward to the Lord.”[@sc-knowhy-657]

On my reading the fringe and the serpent are one lesson given twice. They are the two things in Numbers the LORD makes for all the people to look upon (the spies were sent to see the land, 13:18, and Moses is told to see it, 27:12), and in Hebrew both are “look upon it,” with the same verb and the same pronoun. What changes after the punishment is what Israel is told to look at. The spies were commanded to see a land and weigh it (13:18), and they weighed it by themselves; the fringe and the serpent are things the LORD has made to be looked at, each with His word attached, to remember and to live. Read together, the second shows what the first was asking for. The fringe turned eyes that had searched after their own judgment toward words the LORD had spoken. The serpent shows that the look He asks for is trust, since those who would not look “did not believe that it would heal them” (Alma 33:20). The ten believed what they were in their own eyes; at the pole the only labor was to look where the LORD had said life was. Elder Glen O. Jenson comes nearest. In the same account he quotes the Lord’s plea at 15:39 to “remember all the commandments of the Lord, and do them,” and of the serpent he writes, “We show our desire to be healed by remembering Him always and obeying His commandments.”[@jenson-look-and-live] He does not set the fringe and the serpent side by side as two looks. The notes on [Numbers 21:8–9](/numbers/21/) discuss the serpent and the pole.

Numbers has other sights after these, each in its own story: Balaam, whose eyes the LORD opened (22:31; 24:3, 17), and Moses, shown the land from Abarim (27:12). The notes on [Numbers 22:28](/numbers/22/), [Numbers 24:3](/numbers/24/), [Numbers 24:17](/numbers/24/) and [Numbers 27:12](/numbers/27/), and [Let It Suffice Thee](/deuteronomy/themes/let-it-suffice-thee/), take them up.

## “Even so must the Son of man be lifted up”

The serpent is where Numbers reaches Christ in His own words: “And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up: That whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have eternal life” (John 3:14–15). “Jesus Christ taught that this incident symbolized His atoning sacrifice”; He referred to it “as a symbol of Himself being lifted up on the cross.”[@scripture-helps-num][@gs-serpent-brazen] Nephi the son of Helaman draws out the look: “as many as should look upon the Son of God with faith, having a contrite spirit, might live, even unto that life which is eternal” (Hel. 8:15). The risen Lord said it in the first person: “Look unto me, and endure to the end, and ye shall live” (3 Ne. 15:9).

Read with the fringe, the serpent completes what the law of 15:39 began. The fringe gave eyes that had measured themselves the LORD’s commandments to look upon; the serpent gave them a thing lifted up at His command, and life for the look; and Jesus says what that thing was. Eyes like those that saw giants and grasshoppers are not closed but given Him to look at. The work is His: He is lifted up, He heals, and the look is the trust that He will.

## “Look unto me in every thought”

The command reaches a disciple now in the Lord’s words to Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery: “Look unto me in every thought; doubt not, fear not” (D&C 6:36). Elder Kim B. Clark calls it “a call to trust Him completely, surrender our will and yield our hearts to Him.”[@clark-look-unto-jesus] Elder D. Todd Christofferson gives the look its content from Alma: “to look to God is to keep His commandments, cry unto Him continually for His support, counsel with Him in all your doings, and let your heart be full of thanks unto Him day and night.”[@christofferson-look] Elder Christofferson comes to that from the serpent, which Alma calls a symbol of Christ; I also read his words alongside the fringe, looking and remembering the commandments, now turned to the One who gave them.[@christofferson-look]

A disciple’s own eyes still report the walls and the giants and measure the self against them, and what the Lord gives such eyes is not a better estimate of the odds but something to look at. Elder Carlos E. Asay, as Elder Jenson quotes him: “Our looks must not be allowed to wander across the way or to become fixed upon the perishable things of the world. … We must look to God and live!”[@jenson-look-and-live] And the look is easy to refuse because it is plain. Elder Jeffrey R. Holland sets the Israelites “refusing to look at the serpent on the staff” beside Naaman: “how easy it is for us to dismiss the source of our redemption because the ingredients and the instruments seem embarrassingly plain.”[@holland-and-now-i-see]
