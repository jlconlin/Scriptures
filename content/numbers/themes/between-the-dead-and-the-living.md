Numbers is not where one expects to learn about atonement. Leviticus is the book of the altar; Numbers is the book of the march, the censuses and the quarrels. Yet the verb behind “make an atonement,” *kipper*, is used sixteen times in Numbers[@oshb-wlc], and the book uses it in ways Leviticus does not. In Leviticus the word belongs to the priest and his offering. In Numbers atonement is made by a tribe's service, by a censer of incense carried into a crowd, by a javelin, and by a heap of gold. Daniel Belnap, setting the altar beside the three narratives of chapters 16, 25 and 31, counts “at least four recognized ways of enacting atonement,” and in those three narratives, he says, “blood played no role whatsoever.”[@belnap-dwell-among] The Levites of chapter 8 are my addition to his list.

Then the verb is used one last time, and used differently. It is passive, it is negated, and its subject is not a person but the ground: the land “cannot be cleansed of the blood that is shed therein, but by the blood of him that shed it” ([[Num. 35:33]]). Two verses earlier the law has refused, twice, to take any ransom for a murderer's life.

I want to argue that the book sets two limits on atonement, and that they belong together. The first is shown, not stated. Every atonement Numbers makes without an altar is made to stop or prevent a plague, for people still alive, and when Aaron stood “between the dead and the living” the dead stayed dead. The second is stated in the law: for blood shed, no ransom can be brought. Amulek, in the Book of Mormon, reasons from that second law to the conclusion that the atonement for the world must be infinite, and not the sacrifice of a man. The join between the two limits is my own reading. But an infinite atonement, as scripture describes it, reaches past the line Aaron held. On my reading, the law Amulek reasons from and the line Aaron stood on answer each other.

## Four atonements and no altar

Begin with the first. When the Levites are given to Aaron, the Lord says they are “to do the service of the children of Israel in the tabernacle of the congregation, and to make an atonement for the children of Israel: that there be no plague among the children of Israel, when the children of Israel come nigh unto the sanctuary” ([[Num. 8:19]]). No animal is named. The Levites atone by serving, and by standing where the people would otherwise come too near. The Cambridge commentary puts it exactly: this is “not in this case propitiation for past sins, but the averting of God's anger,” and the Levites are “both substitutes for the laity, and at the same time a cordon to keep them at a distance.”[@cambridge-numbers] The lexicon files the verse under atonement made “by the substitution of the Levites for the firstborn.”[@bdb] The result the verse names is a negative one: no plague.

The second atonement is the one this essay takes its title from. The day after Korah's company died, the whole congregation turned on Moses and Aaron, and the Lord's answer was to begin consuming them. Moses said to Aaron, “Take a censer, and put fire therein from off the altar, and put on incense, and go quickly unto the congregation, and make an atonement for them: for there is wrath gone out from the LORD; the plague is begun” ([[Num. 16:46]]). Aaron ran. He “put on incense, and made an atonement for the people. And he stood between the dead and the living; and the plague was stayed” ([[Num. 16:47–48]]).

Nothing died for this atonement except the people who were already dying. There is fire from the altar, but no blood, and the Cambridge commentary calls incense “an unusual way of making atonement; the shedding of blood was generally required.”[@cambridge-numbers] Keil sees in it the high priest's prayer made visible. Moses had exhausted every plea he had used before, and what remained was “the offering of incense which embodied the high-priestly prayer.”[@keil-numbers] The Seminary manual says plainly what a Latter-day Saint reader sees in the scene: Aaron's actions “represented Jesus Christ's Atonement for us.”[@seminary-2018-num-67] For now notice two things. The verb is in verses 46 and 47; verse 48 tells us where Aaron was standing when it took effect.[@oshb-wlc] And the chapter counts the cost on the other side of him: “fourteen thousand and seven hundred, beside them that died about the matter of Korah” ([[Num. 16:49]]).

The third is harder to read as atonement at all. At Peor, when Israel had joined itself to Baal and a plague was again running, Phinehas took a javelin into a tent and killed an Israelite man and a Midianite woman, and the text says, “So the plague was stayed from the children of Israel” ([[Num. 25:8]]). The Lord's verdict on the deed uses our verb: Phinehas “was zealous for his God, and made an atonement for the children of Israel” ([[Num. 25:13]]). The lexicon groups this with Aaron's incense, with Moses' intercession after the golden calf, and with David's question over the bloodguilt of Saul's house, as atonement “without sacrifice.”[@bdb] (The notes on [chapter 25](/numbers/25/) carry what the Church says of the violence; nothing here applies Phinehas to anyone now.)

The fourth is gold. After the war with Midian the officers counted their men and found that “there lacketh not one man of us,” and they brought the gold they had taken “to make an atonement for our souls before the LORD” ([[Num. 31:49–50]]). The Church's edition sends the reader from this verse back to Exodus 30, where every man numbered in a census gave “a ransom for his soul… that there be no plague among them” ([[Ex. 30:12]]), and from Exodus 30 forward to Numbers 31.[@lds-scriptures] I read the two passages as one transaction, and the text supports the reading: “have taken the sum” in Numbers 31:49 is the census idiom of Exodus 30:12, “when thou takest the sum.”[@oshb-wlc] Men have been counted; silver or gold is given for their souls; and the purpose, stated in Exodus, is that no plague fall.

So there are four, and they share something beyond the absence of an altar. Each is done against a plague, named in the text or, for the gold, implied by the census law it answers. The Levites serve “that there be no plague.” Aaron's incense is sent because “the plague is begun.” Phinehas's javelin ends with “the plague was stayed,” and the Hebrew behind that phrase, a verb meaning *restrained* with the noun for *plague*, is the same at 16:48, 16:50 and 25:8, and in the psalm that retells what Phinehas did.[@oshb-wlc] The officers' gold, on this reading, is the ransom of Exodus 30, given at a numbering so that no plague would follow. In every case the atonement is made for people who are alive and in danger of not staying so. It holds a line.

## What the line could not cross

Aaron “stood between the dead and the living.” The Hebrew is as plain as the English: between the dead ones, and between the living ones.[@oshb-wlc] That is where the atonement of Numbers works, and it is also where it stops. The plague was stayed. The fourteen thousand seven hundred were not raised. The incense reached the living and did nothing for the dead.

I do not think the text is embarrassed by that. It is simply what *kipper* does in this book: it is for the living. That is the first limit, and Numbers never states it as a rule; it shows it, in a body count. The second limit it does state, in its last chapter, and the second is about a different death. Not the dead of the plague, but a life taken by another man's hand. For that, the law says, nothing can be brought.

## The last use of the verb

The law of the cities of refuge separates the man who killed by accident from the murderer, and then sets two limits:

> Moreover ye shall take no satisfaction for the life of a murderer, which is guilty of death: but he shall be surely put to death. And ye shall take no satisfaction for him that is fled to the city of his refuge, that he should come again to dwell in the land, until the death of the priest. ([[Num. 35:31–32]])

“Satisfaction” is the Hebrew *kōper*, which the Church's footnote renders “ransom, fine”[@lds-scriptures] and the lexicon defines as “the price of a life, ransom.”[@bdb] Israel knew the word from the other side. When an ox known to be dangerous killed a man, its owner's life was forfeit, but a *kōper* could be laid on him and he would live ([[Ex. 21:29–30]]). And at the census every man gave “a ransom for his soul” ([[Ex. 30:12]]). That phrase and this one share their two key words. Exodus has *kōper* for a man's *nefesh*, his soul; Numbers has *kōper* for the *nefesh* of a murderer.[@oshb-wlc] The ransom every Israelite paid for his own life at a numbering is the ransom no one may pay for a murderer's. The law does not say the murderer's life is worth less than the others'. It says it cannot be bought. John Welch, reading these verses beside the Nephite law, notes that whatever the earlier practice may have been, Numbers 35 bans the *kōper*.[@welch-punishments]

Then comes the reason, and with it the verb's last appearance in the book:

> So ye shall not pollute the land wherein ye are: for blood it defileth the land: and the land cannot be cleansed of the blood that is shed therein, but by the blood of him that shed it. ([[Num. 35:33]])

“Cannot be cleansed” is *kipper* in a passive form, and the Church's footnote gives the literal sense: “atonement cannot be made for blood shed in the land except by the blood of him who shed it.”[@lds-scriptures] The lexicon renders it the same way[@bdb], and Keil adds the legal point: there was no expiation for the land “except through the blood of the man who had shed it, i.e., through the execution of the murderer, by which justice would be satisfied.”[@keil-numbers]

Set this beside the fifteen uses before it and the shape of the whole comes into view. Fifteen times the verb is active and someone makes atonement: Moses, the Levites, a priest, Aaron, Phinehas, the officers. In the sixteenth the verb is passive, the land is what would need atoning, and the sentence is built to say that it cannot be done. It is the only time Numbers negates the verb. The one exception it allows is not a ransom and not a rite. It is the shedder's own blood. The book has set a limit once before without the verb: the man who sins “presumptuously” has no offering, and “his iniquity shall be upon him” ([[Num. 15:30–31]]). But here the limit is written into the word for atonement itself. For a life taken there is nothing to bring, because the only thing that cleanses the land of it is the life that took it.

That is my reading of the sequence, and I should say that I know of no commentary that follows *kipper* through Numbers to this verse. The lexicon sorts the uses by sense, not by order, and Belnap, who notices the three bloodless atonements, does not go on to chapter 35.[@belnap-dwell-among] But every step of it is in the text. The verb is where it is. The plague is there at each of the four, in the verse or in the law behind it. And the last verse says what it says.

## Where the law bends, and where it does not

Honesty requires a look at the places where the law seems to allow what Numbers 35 forbids.

Deuteronomy provides for a murder when the murderer cannot be found. If a man is found slain in a field and no one knows who did it, the elders of the nearest city break a heifer's neck in a valley, wash their hands over it, and pray: “Be merciful, O LORD, unto thy people Israel, whom thou hast redeemed, and lay not innocent blood unto thy people of Israel's charge. And the blood shall be forgiven them” ([[Deut. 21:8]]). “Be merciful” is *kipper* once more; the Church's footnote reads it “Let atonement be made for”[@lds-scriptures], and “forgiven” is the same verb in another form.[@oshb-wlc] So bloodguilt is lifted here without the shedder's blood. Keil reads the rite as Israel's own act: the heifer is a substitute, its death “a symbolical infliction of the punishment that should have been borne by the murderer,” and in this way Israel wipes the innocent blood from its midst, though if the murderer were later found the penalty “would still fall upon him.”[@keil-deuteronomy] But notice the prayer. The verb is an imperative addressed to God, and the lexicon files this verse, with Deuteronomy 32:43, where the Lord “will be merciful unto his land,” under *kipper* with God as its subject, drawing the conclusion that “God in his sovereignty may himself provide an atonement or covering for men and their sins which could not be provided by men.”[@bdb] Those are the only two verses in which Deuteronomy uses the verb at all.[@oshb-wlc] As I read the two books together, Numbers says what men cannot do about shed blood, and Deuteronomy, where the shedder cannot be found, has Israel ask the one who can.

The harder case is not in the law but in the history. In David's day a famine was traced to “Saul, and for his bloody house,” and David asked the Gibeonites, “wherewith shall I make the atonement?” ([[2 Sam. 21:1–3]]). They answered, “We will have no silver nor gold of Saul,” and asked for seven of his sons, and David gave them ([[2 Sam. 21:4–6]]). The lexicon lists this among the atonements made without sacrifice, and glosses the answer as “a death penalty of seven sons of the guilty house.”[@bdb] Here *kipper* is used of bloodguilt and the answer is the death of men who did not shed the blood. I note two things and claim no more. The Gibeonites refused money, as Numbers 35:31 does. And this is a king's settlement in a narrative, not the law, which says in the same breath as Numbers that “every man shall be put to death for his own sin” ([[Deut. 24:16]]).

## Amulek’s law

Centuries later a Nephite named Amulek stood before people who doubted “whether there shall be no Christ” ([[Alma 34:5]]), and he answered them from the law of Moses:

> Now there is not any man that can sacrifice his own blood which will atone for the sins of another. Now, if a man murdereth, behold will our law, which is just, take the life of his brother? I say unto you, Nay. But the law requireth the life of him who hath murdered; therefore there can be nothing which is short of an infinite atonement which will suffice for the sins of the world. ([[Alma 34:11–12]])

The Church's edition sends the reader from “our law, which is just” to Deuteronomy's rule that “every man shall be put to death for his own sin” ([[Deut. 24:16]])[@lds-scriptures], and that is the right first stop: no brother may die in the murderer's place. Scripture Central reads the verse the same way, as resting on “the symbolism of the law of Moses which prohibited the taking of one mortal life in punishment for the wrongdoing of another.”[@sc-knowhy-142] Welch reads Alma's sentence on Nehor against Numbers 35:31, and cites Amulek's words as showing that Nephite law still required “the life of him who hath murdered.”[@welch-nehor] Brant Gardner draws the inference Amulek draws: the murderer “died for his own sin,” and his death “did not absolve any other person.”[@gardner-bom-minute-alma]

What Amulek has done is to take the one case where the law of Moses allows no substitute at all, and reason from it. Numbers 35 refused money for the murderer's life; Deuteronomy 24 refused another person's life; the murderer's own blood was the only thing that answered. Amulek takes this as justice. His conclusion is that justice of that kind cannot be met for “the sins of the world” by any sacrifice a man could make. If no one may give his life for one murderer, no one can give his life for everyone. An atonement that reaches all must be of another order. “It shall not be a human sacrifice; but it must be an infinite and eternal sacrifice” ([[Alma 34:10]]), and “that great and last sacrifice will be the Son of God, yea, infinite and eternal” ([[Alma 34:14]]). Russell Rasmussen states the hinge well: “only the life of the man who committed the murder can atone for his crime, though even that falls short.”[@rasmussen-dilemma] Falls short, I take it, because the murderer's death cleansed the land, as Numbers says, and did nothing for his own soul or for anyone else's.

The step from Numbers 35:31–33 to Alma 34:11–12 is my own; no source I have found joins them as premise and conclusion. But Amulek's law and Numbers' law rest on the same ground, and he says in a sentence what the verb's last use says in a clause. Nothing a man can bring answers for a life.

## One who could

How, then, is the Son of God not “a sacrifice of man,” when Paul calls Him “the man Christ Jesus” ([[1 Tim. 2:5]])? President Nelson, quoted in the Church's Book of Mormon manual, answers from the Savior's birth, and his answer is why both are true: “Jesus was the only one who could offer such an infinite atonement, since He was born of a mortal mother and an immortal Father. Because of that unique birthright, Jesus was an infinite Being.”[@bom-student-manual-30] The Book of Mormon says it in its own terms: “God himself atoneth for the sins of the world” ([[Alma 42:15]]). The Bible Dictionary says He “was the only one capable of making an atonement for mankind.”[@bd-atonement]

And the New Testament, describing what He gave, reaches for the word of Numbers 35. The Son of man came “to give his life a ransom for many” ([[Mark 10:45]]). “Ransom” there is the Greek *lytron*, which, Thayer says, the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, uses again and again for the Hebrew *kōper*, naming Exodus 21:30 and Numbers 35:31.[@thayer] Paul's word is the compound *antilytron*, “what is given in exchange for another as the price of his redemption”:[@thayer] Christ “gave himself a ransom for all” ([[1 Tim. 2:6]]). The Church's footnote at that verse sends the reader to Elihu's words in Job, of the one who says, “Deliver him from going down to the pit: I have found a ransom” ([[Job 33:24]])[@lds-scriptures], and that word too is *kōper*.[@bdb] What the law would not take for a murderer, Christ is said to have given for all.

There is a last thing to notice, and it returns to Aaron. Hebrews says the priests of the old covenant “were many priests, because they were not suffered to continue by reason of death,” and sets against them one who “ever liveth to make intercession” ([[Heb. 7:23–25]]). Aaron stood between the dead and the living once, for an afternoon, with incense, and in time he died like every high priest after him. The Seminary manual is right that his run into the plague “represented Jesus Christ's Atonement”;[@seminary-2018-num-67] the Church's own picture of the Advocate is of one who stands before the Father and pleads, “behold the blood of thy Son which was shed… spare these my brethren that believe on my name” ([[D&C 45:4–5]]). But Christ's standing between differs from Aaron's at the very point this essay began with. Aaron's atonement reached the living and stopped at the dead. Christ's is infinite, and when Jacob says why it had to be, he says it in terms of the dead: “save it should be an infinite atonement this corruption could not put on incorruption,” and “this flesh must have laid down to rot and to crumble to its mother earth, to rise no more” ([[2 Ne. 9:7]]). The Church's footnote at Amulek's “infinite” points to that very verse[@lds-scriptures], and President Nelson, listing what “infinite” means, includes that “all humankind would be saved from never-ending death.”[@bom-student-manual-30] “Because of the Atonement, all people will rise from the dead with immortal bodies.”[@gs-atone-atonement] The dead on the far side of Aaron are among those it reaches. That is the join I promised at the start: the law Amulek reasons from says no man can bring a ransom for a life, and the atonement he reasons to is the one that gives the dead back theirs.

## Where we stand

This essay has followed a limit set in a law about murder, but the limit reaches further than murder. A psalm says so in the law's own word: “None of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him: (For the redemption of their soul is precious, and it ceaseth for ever:)” ([[Ps. 49:7–8]]). The ransom is *kōper*.[@oshb-wlc] What Numbers says of one man's blood, the psalm says of every man's soul: no one has the price. (Psalms' [Who Can Ransom a Life?](/psalms/themes/who-can-ransom/) follows that psalm to its answer.)

President Packer told the matter as a parable, of a debtor who could not pay and a creditor who would not forgive, and of a friend who “stepped between them, faced the creditor,” and paid.[@packer-mediator] “Because there was a mediator, justice had claimed its full share, and mercy was fully satisfied.”[@packer-mediator] Then he drew the law of it: “by eternal law, mercy cannot be extended save there be one who is both willing and able to assume our debt and pay the price and arrange the terms for our redemption.”[@packer-mediator] Elder Christofferson says what “redeem” means: “to pay off an obligation or a debt,” or “to rescue or set free as by paying a ransom,” and the Savior's suffering redeems us “by satisfying the demands that justice has upon us.”[@christofferson-redemption]

So the first thing the arc of Numbers says to a disciple is that his own repentance is not a ransom. Nothing he brings, serves or gives is the *kōper* for his soul; the law refused that price long before he was born, and the psalm says no one has it. The Lord said as much in His own voice: “I, God, have suffered these things for all, that they might not suffer if they would repent; But if they would not repent they must suffer even as I” ([[D&C 19:16–17]]). The payment to justice is His. In President Packer's parable the mediator, having paid, turns to the debtor: “you will pay the debt to me and I will set the terms. It will not be easy, but it will be possible.”[@packer-mediator] The terms are His to set.

The second is that someone still stands where Aaron stood, and He does not die. Amulek finishes his argument there: the sacrifice brings about “the bowels of mercy, which overpowereth justice,” and “only unto him that has faith unto repentance is brought about the great and eternal plan of redemption” ([[Alma 34:15–16]]). The Guide to the Scriptures names the terms: an accountable person receives the Atonement's blessings “only if he has faith in Jesus Christ, repents of his sins, receives the ordinances of salvation, and obeys the commandments of God.”[@gs-atone-atonement] None of those is a payment. They are how one comes under the payment already made.

Numbers taught Israel that atonement could be made in many ways, and then that for a life taken it could be made by nothing a man had. Both lessons were true. The second is the one that points to Christ.
