title: Pray unto the LORD, That He Take Away the Serpents
blurb: Under the judgments of Numbers the people never pray to the LORD themselves. They come to Moses and he prays, and the Hebrew verb translated “pray” appears in only two verses, at the first of his pleas and the last. At Taberah his prayer alone puts out the fire. At the serpents the answer to his prayer is a figure lifted up, and the bitten live only by looking at it. Hebrews names Christ the one who “ever liveth to make intercession,” and John names Him the one lifted up like the serpent.

Twice in Numbers the people come to Moses under a judgment and Moses prays. At Taberah “the people cried unto Moses; and when Moses prayed unto the LORD, the fire was quenched” (11:2). Near the end of the wilderness years the LORD sent fiery serpents, and the people said to Moses, “We have sinned, for we have spoken against the LORD, and against thee; pray unto the LORD, that he take away the serpents from us. And Moses prayed for the people” (21:7).

The King James Version has “pray” in thirteen verses of Numbers, but in eleven of them it is the “I pray thee” or “I pray you” of a polite request.[@lds-scriptures] The Hebrew verb for praying to God, *hitpallēl*, stands in the book only in these two verses, once in the first and twice in the second, in the same stem each time.[@oshb-wlc] The book begins and ends its account of Moses praying for the people with the same word.

In Numbers the people never ask the LORD in words to lift a judgment: under the plague of Baal-peor they weep before the tabernacle (25:6), but no plea is recorded, and the one time the LORD is said to hearken to Israel’s own voice is a vow made in battle (21:2–3).[@lds-scriptures] Between the two prayers Moses pleads four more times under a judgment. I read these six pleas as one sequence. At Meribah he and Aaron also fall on their faces over the people’s thirst (20:6), but no judgment has fallen. No source I have read does; the Bible Dictionary lists among “his intercessions” Taberah, the return of the spies and the rebellion of Korah, and lists the brazen serpent as a separate item.[@bd-moses]

Read in order, the pleas show a change, and it is not in Moses. It is in what the answer asks of the people. At Taberah it asks nothing. At the serpents the answer is a serpent on a pole, and “every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live” (21:8). At the serpents, Moses’ prayer is heard, and each bitten person receives healing by looking. That is where Hebrews and John meet: the one who “ever liveth to make intercession” (Heb. 7:25) is the one who must “be lifted up” as Moses lifted up the serpent (John 3:14).

| Plea | Who brings it to Moses | What is asked | What the LORD answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 11:1–3 | the people cry to him | no words given | “the fire was quenched” |
| 12:11–15 | Aaron | “Heal her now” | seven days outside the camp, then received in |
| 14:11–23 | no one | “Pardon, I beseech thee” | “I have pardoned … But” |
| 16:20–26 | no one | “wilt thou be wroth with all the congregation?” | “Get you up from about the tabernacle of Korah” |

<h2 id="quenched">“When Moses prayed unto the LORD, the fire was quenched”</h2>

The first plea has no words. The people “cried,” with a different verb, *ṣāʿaq*; Moses “prayed,” and the text says nothing of what he said.[@oshb-wlc] Keil reads the verse as a judgment cut short: the fire broke out at one end of the camp “and sank down again, i.e., was extinguished very quickly, at the intercession of Moses.”[@keil-numbers] The Pulpit Commentary: “they knew that their only hope was in their mediator, who had already saved them by his intercession from a worse destruction (Exodus 32:30-34).”[@pulpit-numbers]

That is where the sequence starts. The judgment is lifted entirely, at once, by the prayer of one man, and nothing is asked of the people but the cry that brought them to him. (The note “Fire at the edge of the camp” on [Numbers 11:1](/numbers/11/) gives the setting.)

<h2 id="another-shape">“Heal her now” and “Pardon, I beseech thee”</h2>

When Miriam is struck with leprosy, Aaron goes not to the LORD but to Moses: “Alas, my lord, I beseech thee, lay not the sin upon us” (12:11). “Moses, although directly appealed to himself,” the Pulpit Commentary observes, “can only appeal to God.”[@pulpit-numbers] He “cried unto the LORD, saying, Heal her now, O God, I beseech thee” (12:13). The LORD hears him and answers on His own terms: “let her be shut out from the camp seven days, and after that let her be received in again” (12:14). Keil reads this as healing promised “after the endurance of seven days’ punishment,” with the purification that followed “as a matter of course, from Leviticus 13 and 14.”[@keil-numbers] Then the camp waited: “the people journeyed not till Miriam was brought in again” (12:15).

At Kadesh, after the spies’ report, no one asks Moses to plead. The congregation “bade stone them with stones” (14:10), and the LORD said, “I will smite them with the pestilence, and disinherit them, and will make of thee a greater nation” (14:12). The Church’s footnotes at 14:12 and 14:13 send the reader to the same offer and the same plea after the golden calf (Ex. 32:10, 12).[@lds-scriptures] Keil says Moses “stood in the breach on this occasion also (Psalm 106:23), with a similar intercessory prayer to that which he had presented at Horeb, except that on this occasion he pleaded the honour of God among the heathen.”[@keil-numbers] Moses argues that “the Egyptians shall hear it” (14:13). He recites the words in which, Keil says, God “had revealed His inmost being to him at Sinai,”[@keil-numbers] “The LORD is longsuffering, and of great mercy” (14:18). He asks: “Pardon, I beseech thee, the iniquity of this people according unto the greatness of thy mercy” (14:19). The Student Manual calls it “a great intercessory prayer.”[@ot-manual-numbers-13-36]

The answer comes in two parts: “I have pardoned according to thy word: But as truly as I live … Surely they shall not see the land which I sware unto their fathers” (14:20–23).

Each of these answers can be read as fitting its own case, and I do not press them further. In these two pleas, the LORD’s answer differs from the request, and the people bear consequences: a sister kept outside, a camp that waits, a generation that will die in the wilderness. (See “Seven days outside, and a camp that waited” on [Numbers 12:14](/numbers/12/) and “Pardoned, and still sworn out of the land” on [Numbers 14:20](/numbers/14/). Moses’ pleading at Sinai is traced in [Speak Thou with Us](/exodus/themes/speak-thou-with-us/).)

<h2 id="get-you-up">“Shall one man sin?”</h2>

At Korah’s rebellion the LORD told Moses and Aaron, “Separate yourselves from among this congregation, that I may consume them in a moment” (16:21). “They fell upon their faces, and said, O God, the God of the spirits of all flesh, shall one man sin, and wilt thou be wroth with all the congregation?” (16:22). This plea takes the form of a question. The answer narrows the judgment: “Get you up from about the tabernacle of Korah, Dathan, and Abiram” (16:24). In the Cambridge Bible’s summary, “at Moses’ intercession He relents.”[@cambridge-numbers] To be spared, the people must move: “Depart, I pray you, from the tents of these wicked men … lest ye be consumed in all their sins” (16:26).

The next morning the congregation accused Moses and Aaron, “Ye have killed the people of the LORD” (16:41), and the LORD spoke again: “Get you up from among this congregation, that I may consume them as in a moment. And they fell upon their faces” (16:45). This time no words follow. On one reading, Moses has exhausted his earlier pleas: “All the motives which he had hitherto pleaded, in his repeated intercession that this evil congregation might be spared, were now exhausted.” He could not ask that all be spared for the sin of a few, “seeing that the whole congregation had taken part with the rebels.”[@keil-numbers]

So Moses sends Aaron: “Take a censer, and put fire therein from off the altar, and put on incense, and go quickly unto the congregation, and make an atonement for them” (16:46). Aaron “put on incense, and made an atonement for the people. And he stood between the dead and the living; and the plague was stayed” (16:47–48). The plea has become an act. The Student Manual notes that “the burning of incense was a symbol of prayer.”[@ot-manual-numbers-13-36] Ellicott calls incense “an emblem of prayer, and a figure of the intercession and mediation of Christ,” and Aaron between the dead and the living “a striking type of Christ.”[@ellicott-numbers]

Keil says one thing more about the incense, and it matters for what follows. It “embodied the high-priestly prayer,” and its power was “not dependent upon the sincerity and earnestness of subjective faith, but had a firm and immovable foundation in the objective force of the divine appointment.”[@keil-numbers] At the height of the intercessions the people do nothing at all. They murmur, the plague begins, and the high priest runs among them and stops it. (What Aaron’s atonement reached, and what it did not, is the subject of [Between the Dead and the Living](/numbers/themes/between-the-dead-and-the-living/); see also “The God of the spirits of all flesh” on [Numbers 16:22](/numbers/16/).)

<h2 id="not-taken-away">“That he take away the serpents from us”</h2>

The last plea is the one the people bring in words. The Pulpit Commentary calls it “the first and only (recorded) occasion on which the people directly asked for the intercession of Moses,” with a glance back at 11:2.[@pulpit-numbers] It is also the only time in Numbers that the people confess and ask in one breath: “We have sinned … pray unto the LORD, that he take away the serpents from us” (21:7).[@lds-scriptures]

Moses prays with the verb of Taberah.[@oshb-wlc] The answer is not “I will take it away” but “Make thee a fiery serpent, and set it upon a pole: and it shall come to pass, that every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live” (21:8). They ask that the LORD “take away” the serpent, *nāḥāš*; the LORD had sent *nĕḥāšîm śĕrāpîm*, “fiery serpents” (21:6), and tells Moses to make a *śārāf* (21:8), which Ellicott explains as “saraph (a seraph), or burning one”; Moses makes a *nĕḥaš nĕḥōšet*, a “serpent of brass” (21:9).[@oshb-wlc][@ellicott-numbers] The people asked that the thing biting them be removed, and were given an image of it to look at. Keil calls the image “a figurative representation of the poisonous serpents, rendered harmless by the mercy of God,” a sign “that the deadly poison of the fiery serpents was overcome in this brazen serpent.”[@keil-numbers] Then: “if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived” (21:9).

Were the serpents taken away? Numbers does not say. The record “tells nothing but the bare facts,” says the Pulpit Commentary, and the Student Manual: “Nothing more is told of the story.”[@pulpit-numbers][@ot-manual-numbers-13-36] Two of the Church’s retellings read the silence as a no. *Old Testament Stories*: “The Lord did not take the serpents away. But He did make a way for the people to be saved if they were bitten.”[@ot-stories-brass-serpent] Elder Glen O. Jenson: “Rather than simply taking the serpents away, the Lord chose to teach His people about faith and salvation through Christ.”[@jenson-look-and-live] The text itself commits only to this: the answer it records is a remedy for the bitten.

That is not because the LORD will not remove an affliction. Pharaoh had asked, “Entreat the LORD, that he may take away the frogs from me” (Ex. 8:8), with the same form of the verb “take away” as Israel at 21:7, and the frogs died (Ex. 8:13); he asked “that he may take away from me this death only,” and a wind “took away the locusts” (Ex. 10:17, 19).[@oshb-wlc][@lds-scriptures] Pharaoh had asked Moses’ intercession, the Pulpit Commentary recalls, “several times, and never in vain.”[@pulpit-numbers] Numbers itself has the fire quenched and the plague stayed. The difference at the serpents is not in what the LORD can do but in what He did.

Keil names what He did: the Lord helped them, “in such a way, however, that the reception of help was made to depend upon the faith of the people.”[@keil-numbers] Keil does not set this beside what he said of Aaron’s incense, but I do. At chapter 16 the incense worked, in Keil’s words, by “the objective force of the divine appointment” and not by anyone’s faith; at chapter 21 the reception of help “was made to depend upon the faith of the people.” Aaron’s censer stayed the plague in a congregation that did nothing. The serpent of brass saved no one who would not look. The Book of Mormon tells it the same way: “the labor which they had to perform was to look; and because of the simpleness of the way, or the easiness of it, there were many who perished” (1 Ne. 17:41); “the reason they would not look is because they did not believe that it would heal them” (Alma 33:20).[@lds-scriptures]

(The notes “Why an image of the thing that bit them” and “Lifted up on a standard” on [Numbers 21:8](/numbers/21/) take up the serpent itself.)

<h2 id="intercessor">“He ever liveth to make intercession”</h2>

Scripture calls Jesus Christ an intercessor. He “made intercession for the transgressors” (Isa. 53:12), and “he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them” (Heb. 7:25). In latter-day revelation He speaks as “the advocate with the Father, who is pleading your cause before him,” saying, “Father, spare these my brethren that believe on my name, that they may come unto me and have everlasting life” (D&C 45:3, 5).[@lds-scriptures] A mediator, in the Guide to the Scriptures, is “an intercessor or intermediary,” and “Jesus Christ is the mediator between God and man.”[@gs-mediator]

The Church reads Moses’ pleading as pointing to that work. “Moses’s pleading with the Lord on behalf of his people can remind us of Jesus Christ, who is our advocate with the Father,” *Scripture Helps* says of the plea at Sinai,[@scripture-helps-ex-19] and of Numbers 14, that his “intercession for his people was similar to Jesus Christ’s intercession for us.”[@scripture-helps-num] The Student Manual says Moses, “like Christ whom he emulated,” “interceded in their behalf and saved them, but only on the condition of their repentance.”[@ot-manual-ex-21-24] Joshua Sears writes of reading “prophetic intercession as typological for the role of Jesus.”[@sears-intercession]

The serpent points to Him in His own words: “as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up: That whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have eternal life” (John 3:14–15). The Lord refers to that lifting up “as a type of His own ‘lifting up’ upon the cross.”[@bd-serpent-brazen] And Nephi the son of Helaman: “as many as should look upon the Son of God with faith, having a contrite spirit, might live, even unto that life which is eternal” (Hel. 8:15).[@lds-scriptures]

These are usually two figures: Moses praying, a type of the Intercessor, and the serpent, a type of Christ crucified. Scripture joins them itself: “he hath poured out his soul unto death … and he bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors” (Isa. 53:12); “It is Christ that died … who also maketh intercession for us” (Rom. 8:34); and in His own plea the Advocate points the Father to His death: “Father, behold the sufferings and death of him who did no sin … Wherefore, Father, spare these my brethren that believe on my name” (D&C 45:4–5).[@lds-scriptures] What I add is that Numbers 21 enacts the pair in three verses: the answer to Moses’ prayer is the thing lifted up. Elder Dale G. Renlund says in a single paragraph that Christ “is our Advocate with the Father” and the “source [to whom we should] look for a remission of [our] sins,” and that we must, in Alma’s words, “cast about [our] eyes and begin to believe in the Son of God.”[@renlund-treasure]

Read together, Numbers 21 and these passages say that the one who pleads for us and the one we must look to are the same Person. He “shall make intercession for all the children of men; and they that believe in him shall be saved” (2 Ne. 2:9). The everlasting life His plea asks is for “my brethren that believe on my name, that they may come unto me” (D&C 45:5), and Hebrews says He saves “them … that come unto God by him.”[@lds-scriptures] His plea never ceases, and it rests on His having been lifted up.

<h2 id="every-one-bitten">“Every one that is bitten”</h2>

The Lord still answers prayers for relief in more than one way. Sometimes the fire goes out; sometimes the answer is strength to live with what was not removed.[@oaks-2006-heals] President Dallin H. Oaks sets two such answers side by side. When Paul asked to be healed of his thorn in the flesh, “the Lord declined to heal him” and said, “My grace is sufficient for thee.” When Alma’s people prayed for relief, “the Lord told them He would deliver them eventually, but in the meantime He would ease their burdens”; they “did not have their burdens removed, but the Lord strengthened them.”[@oaks-2006-heals] The deliverance came (Mosiah 24:16–17).[@lds-scriptures] I read these accounts together as prayers for relief answered with a way to live. (Deuteronomy’s [Let It Suffice Thee](/deuteronomy/themes/let-it-suffice-thee/) follows a prayer of Moses’ own that was refused.)

Elder Jose L. Alonso finds the lesson in the serpents themselves: “the snakes were not the problem. The real problem was the Israelites’ lack of faith and tendency to constantly complain.”[@alonso-look-to-god]

The Savior’s intercession for His disciples does not cease; He “ever liveth” to make it (Heb. 7:25). What it obtains reaches a disciple as the healing reached a bitten Israelite, by looking. As Elder D. Todd Christofferson tells the story, “Many did look and live, but others were, in Alma’s words, ‘so hardened’ that they simply would not look and perished.”[@christofferson-look]
