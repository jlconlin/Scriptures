Jeremiah's brief (`content/jeremiah/BRIEF.md`) is the model, and its section “What a chapter is for” holds here; the habits to avoid that Ezekiel's and Hosea's briefs list hold here too and are repeated below in short.

## What the author has decided

## What a chapter is for here

Joel is 73 verses in three chapters, and one continuous prophecy: ruin (1:2–2:11), a call to turn (2:12–17), and the Lord's answer (2:18–3:21). A reader meets a vivid text with no king, no year, and no named enemy, and asks plain questions: *Were the locusts real? Is the army of chapter 2 locusts or soldiers? When is “the day of the LORD”? Has the promise of the Spirit been kept, and when? Where is the valley of Jehoshaphat?* A chapter answers those from the text first.

**Say what the text describes before saying what it foretells.** Chapter 1 describes a land after locusts and drought in detail a farmer would recognize (the vine, the fig tree stripped white, the grain offering stopped, the cattle with no pasture). Chapter 2:1–11 describes something that comes “as horsemen” and “like men of war”; the comparisons are the text's own. The Church's helps read both as pointing to the last days. Give the picture, then the reading, and keep them apart.

**Three chapters share almost everything.** The locusts (1:4; 2:25), the day of the Lord (1:15; 2:1, 11, 31; 3:14), the fast and solemn assembly (1:14; 2:15), Zion and the holy mountain (2:1, 32; 3:16–17, 21), the darkened sun and moon (2:10, 31; 3:15), wine and the vats (1:5, 10; 2:24; 3:13, 18). Each is explained once, at the place this brief names, and the other chapters point to it with a `[[reference]]`.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, how a modern translation prints a verse, which later scripture takes up which line) is the coordinator's direction of where to look, some of it from memory. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **When.** The book gives no date. The Bible Dictionary: “The date of his prophecy is uncertain; it may have been spoken as early as the reign of Joash, before 850 B.C., or even so late as after the Return.” *Scripture Helps*: “It is unclear when his ministry took place.” The Student Manual (7-2) prefers the reign of Joash; a survey in the *Religious Educator* thinks it “likely” that Joel prophesied with Haggai and Zechariah; Scripture Central's *Old Testament Minute* reports that some date the Hebrew to about 500 B.C. and ends, “the best we can say is that we don't know.” **Proposed: every chapter's `when` is “Undated; Judah, sometime between the 800s and the 400s B.C.”** or the like. Chapter 1's setting gives the Bible Dictionary's range in a sentence and says the Church's newest help leaves it open; no chapter argues a date, and no argument for one (the order of the Twelve, the Grecians of 3:6, no king named, the temple standing) is made except from a source read, as that source's.
- **Who.** “Joel the son of Pethuel” (1:1) and nothing more. A prophet of Judah (the Bible Dictionary): the book speaks of Zion, Jerusalem, the priests, and the house of the Lord, and never of the northern kingdom. What the name means comes from a lexicon page or it is left out.
- **The locusts.** Four words in 1:4 and again, in another order, in 2:25 (palmerworm, locust, cankerworm, caterpiller in the Church edition's spelling: copy from `data/kjv/joel.json`). An RSC study says the etymologies “is generally unknown, although theories exist for each,” so “the English translations are only approximate.” The Student Manual (7-4) gives Clarke's glosses and says the words are stages of one insect's growth. Proposed: one note at 1:4, with the Hebrew from `hebrew.mjs` and a lexicon page, saying that whether the four are kinds, stages, or four names for one swarm is not known; 2:25 points back.
- **The day of the Lord.** In five verses (1:15; 2:1, 11, 31; 3:14). *Scripture Helps*: “a time when the Lord brings judgment to the wicked and deliverance to the righteous.” Dana M. Pike's article (`pike-great-dreadful-day`, already in `sources.yaml`) studies the phrase in 2:31 and Malachi 4:5: “great” there means momentous, not pleasant, and the Hebrew of the two verses is identical. The full note is at 1:15, its first use (with “as a destruction from the Almighty,” a wordplay in Hebrew: give it only from the Hebrew and a lexicon page; `[[Isa. 13:6]]` on the site has the same line). Pike belongs at 2:31. The note on `[[Mal. 4:5]]` on the site should agree.
- **Verse numbers.** The Hebrew Bible has four chapters: 2:28–32 is its chapter 3, and chapter 3 is its chapter 4 (`scripts/hebrew.mjs` prints both). Commentaries may follow the Hebrew; cite the King James verse. That the five verses Moroni quoted are a chapter of their own in Hebrew is worth one sentence at 2:28.
- **Names of God.** “LORD” throughout; copy any phrase with the name from `data/kjv/joel.json`. “The Almighty” (1:15) is *Shaddai*. The Bible Dictionary identifies the LORD of the Old Testament as the premortal Christ; the place to say it is 2:32, where the Church's footnote on “the LORD” reads “HEB Jehovah” and Paul applies the line to Jesus (`[[Rom. 10:13]]`).
- **Other books on the site.** Joel shares lines with books already written; link, and don't say who borrowed from whom except as a source read says (the Student Manual, 7-2, says the evidence is “not conclusive”). 1:15 and `[[Isa. 13:6]]`; 2:2 and `[[Zeph. 1:15]]` (not on the site); 2:3 and `[[Ezek. 36:35]]` (Eden, reversed); 2:13 and `[[Ex. 34:6]]`; 2:20 and Ezekiel 38–39; 2:27 and Ezekiel's “ye shall know that I am the LORD”; 2:28 and `[[Ezek. 39:29]]`; 2:31 and `[[Mal. 4:5]]`; 3:10 and `[[Isa. 2:4]]` (swords and plowshares, reversed); 3:13 and `[[Isa. 63:3]]`; 3:16 and `[[Amos 1:2]]`; 3:18 and `[[Ezek. 47:1]]`. Hosea on the site was written just before this book: `[[Hosea 6:6]]` beside 2:13 only if a source joins them.
- **Modern revelation.** The Doctrine and Covenants takes up Joel's signs in the Lord's own voice: the Church's footnotes give D&C 29:14, 34:8, 43:18, 45:40–42, 88:87 and Joseph Smith—Matthew 1:33. D&C 45:40–42 follows 2:30–31 nearly word for word. A cross-reference that only repeats the footnote is not a note; one that shows the Lord quoting Joel is. **No comparison panel**, and nothing moves to a Book of Mormon chapter.
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** One change, in the footnotes at 2:13–14: it is the people who are to repent, “and he will turn away the evil from you.” It answers the question a reader asks of “repenteth him of the evil,” so it belongs in a note (ledger row from the footnote, `lds-scriptures`). The site already has notes on the Lord “repenting” (`[[Gen. 6:6]]`; `[[Hosea 11:8]]`): link.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports what runs across the book; the coordinator adds it to `THEME-CANDIDATES.md`. No theme page is written until the author chooses it.

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter; don't re-explain what another chapter explains, point with a `[[reference]]`; a Witness of Christ does not open by saying what the chapter lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer, since the site shows it alone as the note's card; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; “In plain words” adds nothing to the verse; Hebrew only where it changes what the reader understands; don't join two verses yourself and call it the text's meaning; no personal application.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that three chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-09.

- **Were the locusts real, and is the book about Joel's day or ours?** The Church's sources do not speak with one voice. The Bible Dictionary: “The occasion of the prophecy was a severe visitation of drought and locusts.” *Scripture Helps* (2025): the prophecies “were likely made after the land of Judah was afflicted with a severe drought and a plague of locusts. These prophecies tell of many signs preceding the Second Coming of Jesus Christ.” Sidney B. Sperry: “most of the book of Joel has reference to the latter days and has little or nothing to do with events of Joel's own day, as so many scholars have assumed,” the locusts being “idioms and allusions peculiar to his own day” for a future he saw. Kent P. Jackson, as KnoWhy #651 quotes him: “every word in the book refers to the latter days.” The Student Manual (7-3, 7-4) calls the prophecies “dualistic” and passes on a scheme in which the four insects are Assyria and Babylon, Persia, Greece, and Rome. **Proposed: the newest wins, and it holds both.** A real plague is the occasion (the Bible Dictionary, *Scripture Helps*); through it Joel sees the day of the Lord, which the Church's chapter headings and *Scripture Helps* place before the Second Coming. Sperry's and Jackson's reading, that the whole book looks to the latter days, is given once, in chapter 1, as theirs. **The four-empires scheme is not used**: no chapter assigns an insect to a nation.
- **The army of 2:1–11: locusts or soldiers?** The text compares what comes to horses, chariots, and men of war (2:4–7), which is how one describes something that is not an army; 2:25 calls the locusts “my great army which I sent among you”; 2:11 has the Lord at the head of it. The chapter heading: “War and desolation will precede the Second Coming.” The Student Manual (7-8) reads “the battle of Armageddon.” *Old Testament Minute* reads the locusts as “a metaphor for an invading army.” Revelation 9:7–9 takes Joel's locusts up (the footnote at 2:5). Proposed: say what the verses picture and that their comparisons run from insect to army; give the heading's reading as the Church's; **the word “Armageddon” is used only as the manual's**, since neither Joel nor the heading uses it. “When they fall upon the sword, they shall not be wounded” (2:8) and “the northern army” (2:20) are each a reader's question: answer from the Hebrew, a modern translation, and a source read, as readings.
- **“I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh” (2:28–29): fulfilled when?** Peter at Pentecost: “this is that which was spoken by the prophet Joel” (`[[Acts 2:16]]`). Moroni in 1823, of the same five verses: “this was not yet fulfilled, but was soon to be” (Joseph Smith—History 1:41). *Scripture Helps* settles how the site reads it: “Joel's prophecy, like many in the Old Testament, has multiple fulfillments.” President Hinckley in 2001: “The vision of Joel has been fulfilled.” KnoWhy #651 and Lynne Hilton Wilson's chapter (RSC) say the same of Peter and Moroni; Jackson, in the KnoWhy, puts the complete fulfilment in the Millennium. Sperry wrote that if Peter meant fulfilment “we shall have to differ with him”: **don't set Moroni against Peter**; a note may say that Sperry felt the difficulty and that the Church's newer help answers it with more than one fulfilment. The note's own work: what “afterward” follows (the rain and the restored years of 2:23–27), where Peter's quotation differs (“in the last days”; read Acts 2:17–21 beside the verses and Brenton's page), who is named (sons and daughters, old and young, servants and handmaids: the promise goes down the household to those with no standing in it), and Moses's wish in `[[Num. 11:29]]` if a source read joins them (Wilson does). President Joseph Fielding Smith's remark that the fulfilment began with the Renaissance and Reformation is quoted in a 1999 *Ensign* article on Scripture Central's archive: use it only from a page read, and it is not needed.
- **“Whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered” (2:32).** Peter ends his quotation here (`[[Acts 2:21]]`) and Paul quotes it of confessing Jesus as Lord (`[[Rom. 10:13]]`); the Student Manual (7-11): “Verse 32 is a reference to Jesus Christ.” The footnotes: “HEB Jehovah”; the Topical Guide, “Jesus Christ, Savior.” This is chapter 2's Witness of Christ. “In mount Zion and in Jerusalem shall be deliverance” has the footnote D&C 76:66 and the like line in `[[Obad. 1:17]]`; *Old Testament Minute* reads two places, the New Jerusalem and the old: give that only as its reading, and see whether a Church source read says it.
- **The signs in heaven (2:10, 30–31; 3:15).** The sun dark and the moon blood. The Savior uses the language (`[[Matt. 24:29]]`), John sees it (`[[Rev. 6:12]]`), and the Lord repeats it in D&C 29:14 and 45:42. Peter includes these verses in what he quotes at Pentecost, which is itself a reason Moroni's “not yet fulfilled” makes sense: say that only if a source read does. No note explains the signs as natural events or dates them.
- **“Rend your heart, and not your garments” (2:12–14).** *Scripture Helps* has a section (tearing clothes as mourning; the broken heart). The footnote gives 3 Nephi 9:20 and D&C 64:34. The note does what those don't: “even now” (2:12, the turn of the book); the Lord's character quoted from `[[Ex. 34:6]]`; “who knoweth if he will return and repent” with the Joseph Smith Translation; and the fast that includes infants and a bridegroom (2:16), with the priests weeping “between the porch and the altar” (2:17; `[[Ezek. 8:16]]` on the site has the same spot).
- **“The former rain moderately” (2:23).** The Hebrew can be read “the teacher for righteousness”; some translations and old readers take it so. A lead from memory: a note only if the Hebrew from `hebrew.mjs`, a lexicon page and a translation's own page show it, and then as a rendering, with no step from it to a person.
- **“I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten” (2:25).** Much loved and often applied to a person's lost years. In the verse it is harvests, promised to a people who have fasted. Say that first; the Student Manual (7-10) reads the years as “generations of oppression for scattered and rejected Israel,” which is its reading.
- **The valley of Jehoshaphat and the valley of decision (3:2, 12, 14).** The Bible Dictionary places it “East of Jerusalem, between the city and the mount of Olives.” *Old Testament Minute*: “there is no valley or place called Jehoshaphat. The name means ‘Jehovah judges,’ and the ‘valley of Jehoshaphat’ probably symbolizes God's place of judgment.” The Student Manual (7-12) calls it “literally, the ‘Valley of Decision’ in Hebrew,” which runs two different names together, and thinks it “most likely” the Kidron. Proposed: the name's meaning from the Hebrew and a lexicon page, with the wordplay in 3:12 (“there will I sit to judge”); then the Bible Dictionary's place as the traditional one; and say that Joel is the only book to use the name. **“Decision” in 3:14 is the Lord's, not the multitudes'**: the Hebrew word and a lexicon page decide whether that can be said (the verse is often preached as people making up their minds). Whether King Jehoshaphat's victory in 2 Chronicles 20 lies behind the name is said only from a source read.
- **The nations judged (3:1–8, 19).** “Parted my land,” “cast lots for my people,” a boy given for a harlot and a girl sold for wine; Tyre, Zidon and “the coasts of Palestine” (Philistia: the footnote or a lexicon page); the Grecians (the Hebrew is *Javan*, on the site at `[[Gen. 10:2]]` and `[[Ezek. 27:13]]`) and the Sabeans; Egypt and Edom. Explain who each was briefly from the Bible Dictionary; which capture or sale is meant is not known, and the chapter says so. The sale of children is described, not dwelt on.
- **“Beat your plowshares into swords” (3:10).** The reverse of `[[Isa. 2:4]]` (on the site) and `[[Micah 4:3]]`: here it is a taunt to the nations, called to a battle they will lose. A good note; who is speaking in 3:9–13, and who the “mighty ones” of 3:11 are, come from the text and a source read.
- **The sickle and the winepress (3:13).** Taken up in `[[Rev. 14:14–20]]` and D&C (the footnotes); `[[Isa. 63:3]]` on the site has the winepress. Chapter 3's Witness of Christ can rest here or on 3:16–17, the Lord who roars from Zion and is “the hope of his people”; the Student Manual (7-12) joins the chapter to Zechariah 12–14 and D&C 45:48–53, where the Lord stands on the Mount of Olives. Use what a source read joins.
- **The fountain from the house of the Lord (3:18).** `[[Ezek. 47:1]]` on the site, `[[Zech. 14:8]]`, `[[Rev. 22:1]]`. The valley of Shittim: the Bible Dictionary gives “Acacias” and the district east of Jordan. Link to Ezekiel 47 and its theme page; don't retell it.
- **“I will cleanse their blood that I have not cleansed” (3:21).** Obscure in the King James. Give the footnote if there is one, the Hebrew, and what one or two modern translations do; don't call the text corrupt from memory.
- **The Spirit, and “the Spirit of Christ.”** The Student Manual (7-11) says the Spirit poured out “is not only the Holy Ghost but also the Spirit of Christ.” President Hinckley applied the verse to scientific discovery as well as revelation. These are their readings; a note gives them as such and does not build a doctrine of which Spirit the verse means.
