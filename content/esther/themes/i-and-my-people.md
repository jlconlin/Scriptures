The book of Esther turns on a law that cannot be taken back. Vashti’s removal is written “among the laws of the Persians and the Medes, that it be not altered” ([[Esth. 1:19]]), and Haman’s letters against the Jews are “written, and sealed with the king’s ring” ([[Esth. 3:12]]). So when Haman is dead and Esther asks “to reverse the letters devised by Haman” ([[Esth. 8:5]]), the king cannot: “the writing which is written in the king’s name, and sealed with the king’s ring, may no man reverse” ([[Esth. 8:8]]). The decree “could not be undone,”[@scripture-helps-esther] and Haman’s death ends nothing. What saves the Jews is not a repeal but a second writing set beside the first, letting them “stand for their life” ([[Esth. 8:11]]). The notes on [8:8](/esther/8/) and [Daniel 6:8](/daniel/6/) explain the law itself.

Inside that story the book follows one person’s relation to the sentence. Esther enters it hidden, her people and kindred unspoken. She says “if I perish, I perish” in the decree’s own verb, then “we are sold, I and my people,” and then pleads for “my people” and “my kindred” alone. I read that movement beside what scripture says of Jesus Christ as Advocate, and the comparison is my own. No Church source I have read calls Esther a type of Christ or compares her pleading to His. The nearest is a 2014 message on Christ as Advocate, which quotes her among the “female disciples of Jesus Christ” who “have followed His example”;[@ensign-2014-advocate] a chapter title, “Esther: Queen of Persia and Advocate for Her People,” is left unexplained.[@ot-manual-31]

So I do not call her a type, and the comparison works by its differences. Esther, whose people were hidden, names herself among those sentenced to perish and pleads for them before the throne. Three things her story cannot do: she does not perish, what she pleads first is the king’s favour, and it is the enemy, not the advocate, who hangs on the tree. Those three measure what Jesus Christ did, the Advocate who took His brethren’s condition on Himself, died on the tree, and pleads that death.

<h2 id="her-people-nor-her-kindred">“Her people nor her kindred”</h2>

Esther comes into the book hidden. “Esther had not shewed her people nor her kindred: for Mordecai had charged her that she should not shew it” ([[Esth. 2:10]]). After she is made queen the narrator says it again ([[Esth. 2:20]]). Mordecai’s charge is the only reason the text gives; it does not say why he gave it or what she thought of it (the note on [2:10](/esther/2/)).

Then the decree: letters “to destroy, to kill, and to cause to perish, all Jews, both young and old, little children and women, in one day” ([[Esth. 3:13]]). Mordecai charges her to go to the king “and to make request before him for her people” ([[Esth. 4:8]]), and she answers with the law of the inner court: whoever comes uncalled, “there is one law of his to put him to death, except such to whom the king shall hold out the golden sceptre” ([[Esth. 4:11]]).

Mordecai’s reply removes whatever shelter the hiding might have seemed to give. “Think not with thyself that thou shalt escape in the king’s house, more than all the Jews” ([[Esth. 4:13]]); if she holds her peace, “thou and thy father’s house shall be destroyed” ([[Esth. 4:14]]). She was a Jew all along, and the decree named all Jews. The palace gave her concealment, not exemption. Her act, then, is not to take on a sentence that belonged to others but to own one that was already hers.

She answers: “so will I go in unto the king, which is not according to the law: and if I perish, I perish” ([[Esth. 4:16]]). “Perish” is *ʾābad*, the verb of the decree’s “to cause to perish” and of Mordecai’s “shall be destroyed.”[@oshb-wlc] It occurs thirteen times in the book, in eleven verses, and this is the only verse where its subject is “I”; she says it twice.[@oshb-wlc] The decree uses one form of the verb (the intensive infinitive), Mordecai and Esther another (the simple form),[@oshb-wlc] so in form her words stand nearest to his. (The verb is not kept for the Jews’ suffering: Mordecai’s letters and chapter 9 use it of what the Jews do to their enemies, [[Esth. 8:11]]; [[Esth. 9:6]].[@oshb-wlc])

I read the three as a sequence. The decree said what would be done to all Jews; Mordecai told her it would be done to her; she says it of herself, and goes in. Her words are “an expression not of despair but of resignation in light of what she had determined she had to do.”[@ot-manual-31] The sentence that was already hers she now speaks in the first person.

<h2 id="we-are-sold">“We are sold, I and my people”</h2>

What she asks for changes. When the king holds out the sceptre ([[Esth. 5:2]]), she asks for a banquet for “the king and Haman” ([[Esth. 5:4]]), and at it for a second ([[Esth. 5:8]]). Nothing yet is said of her people.

At the second banquet she answers the king: “let my life be given me at my petition, and my people at my request: For we are sold, I and my people, to be destroyed, to be slain, and to perish” ([[Esth. 7:3–4]]). Her life comes first, then “my people,” then one pronoun for both. The three verbs are the decree’s, and the last is in the letter’s own form, the intensive infinitive of 3:13.[@oshb-wlc] Here the hiding ends, before the king and before Haman.

The same verse holds a smaller echo. Mordecai had warned her against holding her peace ([[Esth. 4:14]]); now she says that had they only been sold as slaves, “I had held my tongue” ([[Esth. 7:4]]). It is one verb, *ḥrš*, found in the book only in these two verses.[@oshb-wlc] The silence Mordecai warned her against is the silence she now says she would have kept only had they been sold as slaves.

Then chapter 8. Haman has been hanged; the king gives Esther his house, and Mordecai comes before the king, “for Esther had told what he was unto her” ([[Esth. 8:1]]), words that reach back to 2:10, where she had told nothing.[@lds-scriptures] She comes again: she “fell down at his feet, and besought him with tears to put away the mischief of Haman the Agagite” ([[Esth. 8:3]]). She asks that the letters be reversed, and says why: “For how can I endure to see the evil that shall come unto my people? or how can I endure to see the destruction of my kindred?” ([[Esth. 8:6]]).

Her own life is not in this plea. It belongs to the task of “saving the rest of the Jews after Esther and Mordecai had been saved.”[@ot-manual-31] The text does not say whether she came uncalled this time, and I do not say she risked her life again.

The word “kindred” is *môledet*, and in Esther it stands only in 2:10, 2:20 and 8:6,[@oshb-wlc] the only verses where “people” and “kindred” stand together. In chapter 2 they are “her” people and kindred, in the narrator’s voice, unspoken. In 8:6 they are “my people” and “my kindred,” in her own voice, and the whole of her plea. (The “destruction” of 8:6 is a different word, a noun, not the verb of 4:16.[@oshb-wlc])

Read in order, the four passages make a development, and the reading is mine; no source I found traces it. She is first silent about her people, then speaks the sentence of herself, then names herself and them as one, and last pleads for them when she is no longer in the plea. The two nouns she was charged not to show are the two she ends by claiming.

The arc lies in what she says, not in a count of audiences. The sceptre is held out twice ([[Esth. 5:2]]; [[Esth. 8:4]]); the plea of chapter 7 is made at her own table when the king asks. Nor is 8:6 her last request: after the fighting she asks for a second day in Shushan and that Haman’s ten sons “be hanged upon the gallows” ([[Esth. 9:13]]).

<h2 id="took-part-of-the-same">“He also himself likewise took part of the same”</h2>

Scripture has already read one Hebrew at a foreign court in this way. Moses “refused to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter; Choosing rather to suffer affliction with the people of God, than to enjoy the pleasures of sin for a season; Esteeming the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures in Egypt” ([[Heb. 11:24–26]]). Hebrews does not mention Esther, and I set her beside him because hers is a like choice, one of God’s people in a king’s house choosing to stand with that people under its affliction, and Hebrews calls that choice by Christ’s name. It is not the same choice. Moses left the court, while Esther keeps the crown and uses it; and Moses could have kept the pleasures of Egypt, while Esther, by Mordecai’s word, could not have escaped ([[Esth. 4:13–14]]).

What Hebrews says of Christ Himself goes further. “He is not ashamed to call them brethren” ([[Heb. 2:11]]). “Forasmuch then as the children are partakers of flesh and blood, he also himself likewise took part of the same” ([[Heb. 2:14]]). “He took on him the seed of Abraham” ([[Heb. 2:16]]); “in all things it behoved him to be made like unto his brethren” ([[Heb. 2:17]]). An advocate is “one who pleads another’s cause,” and “Jesus Christ is the Advocate with the Father.”[@bd-advocate]

I set “I and my people” beside “made like unto his brethren.” The likeness is that each stands with the condemned and pleads for them. The difference is where the comparison begins to measure Him. Esther disclosed what she already was: she was under the sentence before she said a word, and her courage lay in owning it. He became what He was not. He was “him who did no sin” ([[D&C 45:4]]), under no sentence of His own, and took part of flesh and blood in order to die. She moved from hiding to confession; He moved from outside to within.

<h2 id="if-i-perish">“If I perish”: what the story cannot do</h2>

*She does not perish.* “If I perish, I perish” ([[Esth. 4:16]]) is a condition, and it is not met: “the king held out to Esther the golden sceptre that was in his hand” ([[Esth. 5:2]]). Her courage was real because she did not know he would. But her people’s deliverance does not come by her death. The Advocate’s plea begins where her story cannot go: “Father, behold the sufferings and death of him who did no sin” ([[D&C 45:4]]). He took part of flesh and blood “that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death” ([[Heb. 2:14]]).

*She pleads favour.* Three of her petitions rest on “if I have found favour” ([[Esth. 5:8]]; [[Esth. 7:3]]; [[Esth. 8:5]]), and the last adds “and I be pleasing in his eyes.” She argues other things too, the king’s own loss ([[Esth. 7:4]]) and that “the thing seem right before the king” ([[Esth. 8:5]]), but what she pleads first is the king’s favour, and she pleads it with “if,” to a king whose sceptre may or may not be held out ([[Esth. 4:11]]). The contrast is not that Christ’s plea lacks favour; He is the one “in whom thou wast well pleased” ([[D&C 45:4]]). It is that He does not plead favour alone: “behold the blood of thy Son which was shed… Wherefore, Father, spare these my brethren that believe on my name” ([[D&C 45:4–5]]). Esther pleads the king’s pleasure in her; He pleads His death.

*The enemy hangs on the tree.* The King James “gallows” of Esther is, in Hebrew, a tree: the verb “hang” and the noun “tree” stand together in eight verses of the book, and the King James Version prints “gallows” in seven of them.[@oshb-wlc] “So they hanged Haman on the gallows that he had prepared for Mordecai” ([[Esth. 7:10]]); “him they have hanged upon the gallows, because he laid his hand upon the Jews” ([[Esth. 8:7]]); “he and his sons should be hanged on the gallows” ([[Esth. 9:25]]). In Esther the tree is where the king’s enemy ends, and the advocate lives.

The law of Moses uses the same two Hebrew roots:[@oshb-wlc] “thou hang him on a tree… (for he that is hanged is accursed of God;)” ([[Deut. 21:22–23]]). And Paul writes, “Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree” ([[Gal. 3:13]]). In Esther the advocate stands before the king and the enemy hangs. In the gospel the Advocate is the one on the tree. That is all the comparison says: Haman is not a figure of Christ or of anyone else.

<h2 id="pleads-that-death">He pleads that death</h2>

Scripture says what the Advocate does now. “He ever liveth to make intercession for them” ([[Heb. 7:25]]). Abinadi describes Him “standing betwixt them and justice; having broken the bands of death, taken upon himself their iniquity and their transgressions, having redeemed them, and satisfied the demands of justice” ([[Mosiah 15:9]]). “If any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous” ([[1 Jn. 2:1]]). He is the one “who is pleading your cause” before the Father ([[D&C 45:3]]), and what He pleads is His sufferings and death.

Here the opening returns, once, as my reading and as a likeness of structure only. In Esther the first writing is not repealed; it is met by a second. In the gospel mercy does not repeal justice either: “do ye suppose that mercy can rob justice? I say unto you, Nay; not one whit” ([[Alma 42:25]]). Instead “God himself atoneth for the sins of the world, to bring about the plan of mercy, to appease the demands of justice” ([[Alma 42:15]]). The differences belong in the same breath. Haman’s decree was wicked, and God’s law is just. Esther’s second writing only let the condemned fight for their lives ([[Esth. 8:11]]); the Atonement satisfies the demand itself.

<h2 id="come-boldly">“Come boldly unto the throne of grace”</h2>

Christ’s advocacy is not a kind word spoken from a safe place. He made His brethren’s condition His own before He pleaded for them, and what He pleads is His death, not their standing. So a disciple does not come to God as Esther came to Ahasuerus, uncalled, not knowing whether the sceptre would be held out ([[Esth. 4:11]]), and then pleading first “if I have found favour” ([[Esth. 5:8]]). “For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities; but was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin. Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need” ([[Heb. 4:15–16]]). The contrast is between two approaches to a throne; the Persian king is not a figure of the Father, nor his sceptre of grace.

The Lord calls Himself “your advocate, who knoweth the weakness of man and how to succor them who are tempted” ([[D&C 62:1]]). Elder D. Todd Christofferson has spoken of the confidence that follows: “I may at any moment and in any circumstance approach through prayer the throne of grace, that my Heavenly Father will hear my petition, that my Advocate, him who did no sin, whose blood was shed, will plead my cause.”[@ensign-2014-advocate] That advocacy has its own terms. He asks, “spare these my brethren that believe on my name” ([[D&C 45:5]]); the Joseph Smith Translation reads, “if any man sin and repent, we have an advocate”;[@lds-scriptures] He is the Advocate “on behalf of the righteous.”[@bd-advocate]

The one Church message that sets Esther beside the Advocate places her among the disciples who “have followed His example,” and draws from His advocacy what can “inspire us to extend mercy and forgiveness to others.”[@ensign-2014-advocate]
