## What a chapter is for here

Esther is ten chapters (167 verses): a story told at the Persian court, with banquets at its turning points, a decree that cannot be revoked, a fast, a reversal, a day of battle and a feast instituted to remember it. A reader asks: *Who was Ahasuerus, and when is this? Why would a queen refuse a king, and why does a royal decree follow about every husband ruling his house? How did a Jewish orphan come to be in the king's house, and was she willing? Why hide that she was a Jew? Why did Mordecai sit in the king's gate, and why would he not bow to Haman? What is an Agagite, and why would one man's wounded pride become a plan to kill a whole people? What are “Pur” and the lot, and why a date eleven months away? Why sackcloth and ashes? Why could the queen not simply ask to see her husband? What did Mordecai mean by “another place” and “such a time as this”? Why fast and not pray, or is the fast a prayer? Why two banquets? Why could the king not sleep, and is that God at work? Why could the first decree not be cancelled? Did the Jews kill women and children, and why a second day and the ten sons hanged? Why do they keep refusing the spoil? What is Purim, and do Jews still keep it? Why is God never named, and why is the book in the Bible at all? What has any of it to do with a Latter-day Saint, who fasts, believes in providence, and is promised that the Lord's hand is in the small details?* A chapter answers those from the text first.

**The book's threads.** The Bible Dictionary: the book “contains no direct reference to God, but He is everywhere taken for granted, as the book infers a providential destiny (Esth. 4:13–16) and speaks of fasting for deliverance,” and it has value “as containing a most striking illustration of God's overruling providence in history and as exhibiting a very high type of courage, loyalty, and patriotism” (`bd-esther-book`). *Come, Follow Me* (4) asks whether the book's chances are “coincidences, or were they part of a divine plan,” with Elder Rasband's “divine design”; the institute manual's two activities are God placing people where they can help (1–4) and courage (4–8). The text itself turns on reversal: the day the enemies “hoped to have power over them” was “turned to the contrary” (9:1), “the month which was turned unto them from sorrow to joy” (9:22); Haman is hanged “on the gallows that he had prepared for Mordecai” (7:10); Haman's ring and Haman's house go to Mordecai and Esther (8:2). **Proposed: two threads.** (1) **Providence without a name**: what Mordecai trusts (4:14), what the fast asks (4:16), what happens on the night the king cannot sleep (6:1), and what Haman's own household concludes (6:13). (2) **The great reversal**, from the decree of 3:13 to the decree of 8:11 and the feast of 9:20–28, carried by banquets (1; 2:18; 5; 7; 8:17; 9:17–19), garments (sackcloth, 4:1; royal apparel, 6:8–11; 8:15) and the king's ring (3:10; 8:2). A chapter's `thread` says which its chapter carries; it does not restate the book.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-11-12. The rest (which verse echoes which, what a Hebrew word can mean, the year arithmetic) is the coordinator's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

### When: one chronology for all ten chapters

The guide [Persia and the Return](/old-testament/guides/persia-and-the-return/#rulers) already gives “486–465 | Xerxes (Ahasuerus) | Esther is set in his reign,” and the timeline guide puts Esther “under Xerxes” (`#return`). A chapter cites the verse and links `#rulers`; it does not retell the Persian history.

- **Which king.** Bible Dictionary, Ahasuerus (`bd-ahasuerus`): “Esth. 1:1, where Ahasuerus is certainly to be identified with Xerxes” (and the Ahasuerus of Ezra 4:6 is “probably the same”; the one of Dan. 9:1 is another). *Scripture Helps*: “commonly identified as Xerxes I, who ruled Persia from 486 to 465 BC.” Bible Chronology (`bible-chron-ot`, 75): “486 | Ahasuerus (Esth. 1:1). | =Xerxes, 486–465.” The Student Manual (31-2): the name is the Hebrew form of the Persian *Khsyayarsha*, Greek Xerxes, and “Most scholars place the events recorded in the book of Esther between about 482 B.C. and 478 B.C.”. The Greek version calls the king Artaxerxes (Brenton, 1:1): a clause in chapter 1 at most, if a reader would meet it.
- **The years the text gives** are the king's regnal years: the feast “In the third year of his reign” (1:3); Esther taken to the king “in the tenth month, which is the month Tebeth, in the seventh year of his reign” (2:16); the lot cast “In the first month, that is, the month Nisan, in the twelfth year” (3:7), for “the thirteenth day of the twelfth month, which is the month Adar” (3:13); the second decree “in the third month, that is, the month Sivan, on the three and twentieth day” (8:9); the fighting on the thirteenth and fourteenth of Adar and Purim on the fourteenth and fifteenth (9:1, 15–21).
- **Proposed convention:** counting from the accession the Church's sources give (486 B.C.), and saying “about,” as the Student Manual does: **the third year, about 483 B.C.; the seventh year, about 479 B.C.; the twelfth year, about 474 B.C.; the thirteenth of Adar, early in 473 B.C.** The arithmetic is the coordinator's; a chapter gives the regnal year from the verse first, then the approximate date, and cites `bible-chron-ot` for 486. Hull (`hull-true-decision`, n. 88) dates the origin of Purim “in 475 BC”; it does not fit the reckoning from 486 and is not used for the date. The Persian wars with Greece fall between chapters 1 and 2 (Bible Chronology, 76: “Battles of Thermopylae and Salamis, 480”); Keil connects the 180-day assembly of 1:4 with Xerxes' council before the campaign (from Herodotus): a clause in chapter 1, Keil's reading labelled, at most.
- **Where Esther falls among the other books.** Between Ezra 6 (the temple finished, 515 B.C.) and Ezra 7 (458 B.C.): Ezra's brief records the Student Manual's “Nearly 60 years separate 7:1 from 6:22,” the years of Esther. *Scripture Helps*: after Cyrus “many remained in exile, including Esther.” Esther's story is about thirty years before Nehemiah served in the same palace (Neh. 1:1). Chapter 1's setting says so once.
- **Proposed `when`, by chapter:**
  - 1: `'The third year of Ahasuerus (Xerxes), about 483 B.C. (verse 3), at Shushan (Susa).'`
  - 2: `'From after the feast of chapter 1 to the tenth month of the seventh year of Ahasuerus, about 479 B.C. (verse 16).'`
  - 3: `'The first month (Nisan) of the twelfth year of Ahasuerus, about 474 B.C. (verse 7); the decree is for the thirteenth day of Adar, eleven months later (verse 13).'`
  - 4: `'Just after the decree of chapter 3, in the first month of the twelfth year of Ahasuerus, about 474 B.C.'`
  - 5: `'The third day of the fast (verse 1; 4:16), in the twelfth year of Ahasuerus, about 474 B.C.'`
  - 6: `'The night after Esther's first banquet and the next day (5:8; 6:14), about 474 B.C.'`
  - 7: `'The second banquet, the day after chapter 6 (5:8), about 474 B.C.'`
  - 8: `'The twenty-third day of the third month, Sivan (verse 9), in the twelfth year of Ahasuerus, about 474 B.C.'`
  - 9: `'The thirteenth and fourteenth days of Adar, the twelfth month (verses 1, 15), early in 473 B.C.; then the letters that fixed Purim for every year.'`
  - 10: `'Undated: a closing word on Ahasuerus and Mordecai's greatness.'`

### The book's make-up (only what a Latter-day Saint or Church source says)

- **Anonymous, and the book is history.** Breitenstein (`breitenstein-esther-minute`, 4): “The book of Esther was written anonymously; therefore, it cannot be dated. However, the story coincides well with the reign of King Ahasuerus.” *Scripture Helps*, endnote 1: “While Bible scholars hold diverse views about the historical accuracy of the book of Esther… the Prophet Joseph Smith made no changes to Esther during his translation of the Bible, and modern prophets have spoken of Esther as a real historical figure” (it cites Monson 2009 and Oaks 2002). **Proposed:** the chapters tell the story as history, as the Church does; chapter 1's setting says once, from that endnote, that scholars differ on its historical accuracy, without a survey of their arguments (Herodotus's queen, the 127 provinces, the size of the gallows). No other chapter takes it up.
- **No Joseph Smith Translation changes.** Nyman (`nyman-jst-historical`, 5): “Ruth, Ezra, and Esther have no changes”; the footnotes have none. A chapter does not say so unless a reader would ask.
- **The Hebrew book and the canon.** Bible Dictionary: “There have been doubts at times as to whether it should be admitted to the canon of scripture.” The guide How It Came to Us (`#dead-sea-scrolls`) already says copies of every Old Testament book “but Esther” were found at Qumran (`bd-dead-sea-scrolls`); **Proposed:** chapter 1's setting or chapter 9 may say it in a clause and link the guide; no note argues for the book's place.
- **The names.** *Scripture Helps* (2:5–7) explains Hadassah (“myrtle”) and Esther (“star”): no note repeats it.
- **Mordecai's descent** (2:5–6): “the son of Kish, a Benjamite; Who had been carried away… with Jeconiah.” Whether “who” is Mordecai or Kish: the Student Manual (31-4) takes it as his great-grandfather; Keil (2:5–6) shows that Mordecai himself would have been about 125; Breitenstein's reading (Mordecai carried away) is not used. *Scripture Helps* endnote 15: “Mordecai, like Saul, was a descendant of Benjamin” (Kish is also Saul's father's name, 1 Sam. 9:1: *lead*). **Proposed:** one note at 2:5–6 on the line of Kish and Benjamin, which chapter 3 needs.

### Other conventions

- **The king's gate** (2:19, 21; 3:2; 5:9, 13; 6:10). *Scripture Helps* (2:19): a gathering place at the entrance, and sitting there “meant that one held an official position.” One note, at 2:19 or 2:21, saying more than that or none; later chapters point back.
- **Fasting** (4:3, 16; 9:31). Ezra 8's `explore` already sets Esther's fast (4:16) beside Ezra's (`[[Ezra 8:21]]`); KnoWhy #135 sets it beside Nephite practice; *Come, Follow Me* beside the law of the fast and Isaiah 58:6–12. **Proposed:** the fast's main note is at 4:16 (what a fast without a recorded prayer is, and the three days); 9:31's “the matters of the fastings and their cry” points back. The Bible Dictionary's later Fast of Esther (Feasts) goes in chapter 9, labelled as later custom.
- **Sackcloth and rent clothes** (4:1–4): *Scripture Helps* explains them; no note unless it says more (why Esther sent clothes and Mordecai refused them, 4:4; the rule against sackcloth at the gate, 4:2, is the text's own).
- **Hanging and the gallows** (2:23; 5:14; 7:9–10; 9:13–14, 25). Belnap (`belnap-fell-tree`, 18): the “gallows” is a wooden structure, “approximately seventy-five feet high (fifty cubits),” meant “to be visible from a distance,” a public humiliation, for those “in rebellion or who sought to overrule the king's authority”; his note 11 on the Septuagint's verb at 7:9. **Proposed:** one note at 5:14, from Belnap; 7:10 is told from the text; `[[Prov. 21:18]]` already uses Haman and Mordecai: link once, at 7:10.
- **The king's ring** (3:10; 8:2, 8): `[[Gen. 41:42]]` (“Ring, linen, and gold chain”) already cites both verses: link. Mordecai “next unto king Ahasuerus” (10:3) has the footnote Gen. 41:40; Joseph's story is the book's nearest scriptural pattern (the footnote at 4:14 points to Gen. 45:7). **Proposed:** one note, in chapter 6 or 10, on how the text echoes Joseph at Pharaoh's court, from the footnotes; Daniel (10:2's footnote Dan. 3:30; 10:3's Dan. 6:3) in the same note.
- **“Half of the kingdom”** (5:3, 6; 7:2): the footnote gives Mark 6:23; Breitenstein (50): “only a gesture rather than a serious offer.” A clause, not a note.
- **Haman's fall on the couch** (7:8): the Student Manual (31-15) explains the reclining couch; Hebrew lead `hebrew.mjs esther 7:8`. One note if the reader would stop; it is told plainly, not dwelt on.
- **“Became Jews”** (8:17): Rasmussen in the Student Manual (31-16) calls it a “typical psychological phenomenon”; Breitenstein, fear. **Proposed:** the text's own reason, “for the fear of the Jews fell upon them,” and no claim about conversion beyond it.
- **Looking up a Hebrew word.** `hebrew.mjs esther <c>:<v>` (KJV and Hebrew numbering agree in Esther). Quote BDB paragraphs from Bible Hub, never its page title.
- **Theme candidates** (leads only; `THEME-CANDIDATES.md` not yet created): providence without a name (4:14; 6:1; 6:13; 9:1); the great reversal; fasting as the book's only worship; Amalek from Exodus 17 to Esther 9 (it runs across four books already on the site).

**Habits to avoid** are listed in Amos's brief and hold here. In Esther especially: **don't make every chapter a lesson on courage or on “such a time as this”** (4:14 belongs to chapter 4, and the providence thread is carried by the text's turns, not by a refrain); **don't name God where the text does not and then claim the text did** (the notes may say where the Church's sources see His hand, labelled as theirs); **don't psychologize** the characters beyond what the text shows (Ahasuerus's character, Esther's feelings, Haman's pride are told from their words and acts); **don't retell Persia's history** (link the guide).

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that ten chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-11-12.

- **God never named; providence (the whole book; 4:14; 4:16; 6:1).** The Hebrew book never uses a name or title of God. Bible Dictionary: “no direct reference to God, but He is everywhere taken for granted… a most striking illustration of God's overruling providence in history.” *Scripture Helps* quotes the same and adds at 4:14 that “the Lord may have placed Esther in her position as queen so she could be His instrument in saving the Jews,” with Elder Maxwell (the Lord “does not do things by ‘coincidence’ but instead by ‘divine design’”) and Elder Rasband (“By ‘divine design,’ He is in the small details of your life”); at 6:1, “It is implied that the Lord's influence led the king to read this particular part of the record.” KnoWhy #863 (`sc-knowhy-863`, 3): no prayer is recorded in the Hebrew, and later Greek-writing Jewish authors “likely felt the absence of such a prayer in the text and imagined what she might have said.” The footnote at 4:14 (“time”) gives Gen. 45:7, Joseph's “God sent me before you to preserve you a posterity in the earth.” **Proposed.** (1) The book's silence is stated as a fact once, in chapter 1's setting, and **not called “the only book”** that never mentions God (KnoWhy #863's wording; the Song of Solomon is a question the site does not need to raise). (2) The notes show providence where the text implies it and say whose reading it is: 4:14 (“from another place” and “such a time as this,” with the Gen. 45:7 footnote and *Scripture Helps*; one of Maxwell or Rasband, once, here); 4:16 (the fast); 6:1 (the sleepless night, *Scripture Helps*' “implied”); 6:13 (Haman's own wise men and wife: “thou shalt not prevail against him”); 9:1 (“turned to the contrary”). (3) **“Another place” (4:14) is not read as a name for God**: the Hebrew is the ordinary word *maqom*, “place” (H4725); a later Jewish use of “the Place” for God was not found in any source read, so no note says the verse hides God's name. (4) The Greek supplies what the Hebrew leaves unspoken (4:8, “call upon the Lord”; 6:1, “the Lord removed sleep from the king”): one sentence in chapter 4 or 6 may say so, from Brenton, as the Greek translation's reading, which makes the Hebrew's silence the more striking. (5) *Scripture Helps*' “implied” is the measure; the Student Manual's surer statements are not used (below).
- **The Greek additions and the Apocrypha (D&C 91).** Bible Dictionary, Apocrypha (`bd-apocrypha`): “The rest of the chapters of the Book of Esther” are among the books of special value; their object is “to illustrate the hearing of prayer and the deliverances from the Gentiles that God wrought for His people”; the Apocrypha are “useful reading,” and D&C 91 says they are “mostly correct but with many interpolations by man.” KnoWhy #863 reads Esther's Greek prayer (Septuagint Esther C) devotionally; in it she “dislikes having to be married to a non-Jew and hates being queen of a foreign kingdom.” The guide How It Came to Us and `[[Dan. 3:23]]` (“The song the Apocrypha adds”) are the site's models. **Proposed, as in Daniel:** the chapters follow the King James text, which is the Hebrew book. One note, at 4:16, says that the Greek version adds a prayer of Mordecai and one of Esther at this point (KnoWhy #863; `bd-apocrypha`), what the additions are for in the Bible Dictionary's words, and D&C 91's word on the Apocrypha, linking the guide; it may quote a line of the prayer from KnoWhy #863 as the Greek's, never as Esther's own words in the book. **Nothing from the additions is used to explain what Esther thought or felt** (her loathing of the marriage and the crown is the Greek's, not the Hebrew's); no other chapter raises them, except the clause on God's name above. KnoWhy #613 (D&C 91) may be read for the note.
- **Vashti's refusal (1:10–22).** The text (1:12, 17, 22) gives the refusal, the king's wrath, the counsellors' fear for every husband and the decree, but no reason for her refusal. Bible Dictionary (Vashti): “deposed for disobedience”; (Esther, book of): “she refused to show her beauty to the people and the princes.” The Student Manual (31-2, through *The New Bible Commentary*) and Keller (`keller-judaism`, 88) suppose the guests' drunkenness was the reason; the Student Manual (31-3): her refusal “may engender the sympathy of modern readers,” and the counsellors feared “a similar independence of spirit in their wives.” **Proposed.** One note at 1:10–12 that gives what the text gives (seven days of wine, the king “merry with wine,” the summons to show her beauty, the refusal), says the text gives no reason, and gives the drinking as a reading (the Student Manual's, labelled), neither praising nor blaming her in the site's own voice. One note at 1:16–22 on what the counsellors feared and the decree's sweep, from the text: the irony that a decree about husbands' rule is published “to every people after their language” is the text's to show, not the site's to mock. **Not said:** no lesson on marriage or the roles of husbands and wives today (the footnotes' Eph. 5:22 and D&C 93:43 at 1:20–22 are the reader's to follow); Breitenstein's “again refused” (not in the text) is not used.
- **Esther taken into the king's house; her marriage; her hidden people (2:1–20).** The text: “many maidens were gathered together… Esther was brought also unto the king's house” (2:8); “Esther was taken unto king Ahasuerus” (2:16); the twelve months of purification (2:12) and the night with the king (2:14) are told plainly; she “required nothing but what Hegai… appointed” (2:15); “the king loved Esther above all the women… and made her queen” (2:17). The passive verbs are the text's. Bingham and Gardner (`bingham-gardner-role-models`, 26, 31): Esther was “in positions that are not ideal or of their own choosing,” “a position she did not choose for herself,” citing 2:8. Monson (50) and Smoot (5) retell it as Mordecai bringing her to the palace. On hiding her people, the text gives only Mordecai's charge (2:10, 20); *Scripture Helps*: she “kept her Jewish ethnicity hidden at the suggestion of her guardian.” **Proposed.** (1) Chapter 2 tells what happened in the text's words and verbs, the harem and the night described rather than quoted at length (`STANDARDS.md` §3). (2) One note at 2:8 says the text gives Esther no choice in being gathered, with Bingham and Gardner's reading (once in the book); the retellings are not set against the text. (3) **Her marriage to a gentile king is not judged**: no source read faults it or defends it, and Ezra's and Nehemiah's rulings on foreign marriages are neither applied to Esther nor contrasted with her (no note says the books disagree). (4) On hiding her people, one note at 2:10: the text's reason is Mordecai's charge and her obedience (2:20); the motive is not given, and the note does not supply one; the Student Manual's “racial identity” (31-5) is not used. The reveal at 7:4 (“we are sold, I and my people”) is the turn, told in chapter 7. **Not said:** nothing about race; nothing about Esther's chastity or purity beyond what the text says (Bingham's “purity” is her reading and is not repeated).
- **Mordecai refuses to bow (3:2–6).** *Scripture Helps* (3:1–10): “Bowing to a political superior was not prohibited under the law of Moses,” but “Haman as an Agagite… likely means he was a descendent of the Amalekite King Agag,” and the refusal and the plot “may have stemmed from the historic hostility between these two groups”; endnote 15 adds Saul, Samuel and Agag, and Mordecai's descent from Benjamin like Saul. Lane (`lane-worship`, 15): bowing expresses “a relationship of submission,” and Mordecai “refuses to recognize the status and position of Haman.” The text's only stated reason: “he had told them that he was a Jew” (3:4). The Student Manual (31-8, from *The New Bible Commentary*): Haman perhaps “was demanding not mere allegiance but worship.” The footnote at 3:2 gives Mosiah 7:12 (Ammon bowing before King Limhi). **Proposed.** One note at 3:1–2 on the Agagite (BD Agag: “the name was perhaps a title… see Num. 24:7”; link `[[1 Sam. 15:33]]`, `[[Ex. 17:14]]` and `[[Deut. 25:17]]`, whose notes already explain Amalek; don't re-explain), and one at 3:2–4 on the refusal: the text's reason first, *Scripture Helps*' reading as the Church's newest, Lane's on what bowing meant, the worship reading at most as one older reading, labelled. No note claims the text says Mordecai refused worship. Haman's leap from one man to a whole people (3:6, “he thought scorn to lay hands on Mordecai alone”) is the text's own: told, not explained by modern analogies (the Student Manual's comparisons with the twentieth century, 31-8 and 31-17, are not used).
- **The irrevocable law (1:19; 8:8).** `[[Dan. 6:8]]` (“A law the king himself cannot change”) already explains “the law of the Medes and Persians” and cites Esth. 1:19 and 8:8: link it, don't re-explain. *Scripture Helps* (8:3–12): the decree “could not be undone. So Esther persuaded the king to issue another decree allowing the Jews to defend themselves.” **Proposed:** a short note at 1:19 linking Daniel 6, and the main note at 8:8, where the story turns on it: a second decree that answers the first word for word (compare 3:13 with 8:11).
- **The killing in chapter 9: the defense, the ten sons, the second day.** The hardest part of the book. The text: the second decree (8:11) turns Haman's words (3:13) back, letting the Jews “stand for their life” against “all the power of the people and province that would assault them, both little ones and women”; on the day they “laid hand on such as sought their hurt” (9:2) and “did what they would unto those that hated them” (9:5); five hundred and the ten sons in Shushan (9:6–10); at Esther's request a second day there and the dead sons hanged (9:13–15); seventy-five thousand in the provinces (9:16); and three times they laid not their hand on the spoil (9:10, 15, 16), though 8:11 allowed it. *Scripture Helps*: the Jews “receive authority from the king to kill their enemies,” a decree “allowing the Jews to defend themselves before the planned day of their own massacre.” Breitenstein (84): they took no plunder, having “put their focus only on defending themselves”; (86): “Many scholars believe” the hanging of the slain sons was “a public example.” Belnap (18, 35): hanging was a public humiliation for those who opposed the king and Israel. No Church or Latter-day Saint source read comments on the second day or on the numbers. **Proposed.** (1) The chapter tells what the text tells, numbers included, without softening and without relish. (2) It gives the frame the text and *Scripture Helps* give: an irrevocable decree of annihilation still stood, and the second decree let the Jews stand for their lives against those who would carry it out (8:11; 9:1–2, 16). (3) **8:11's “both little ones and women”**: the King James sentence can be read as the Jews' permission or as describing “the people… that would assault them”; *lead*: `hebrew.mjs esther 8:11` and modern translations (NRSVue, NIV, ESV differ); the note says what the Hebrew allows, and that chapter 9 never says women or children were killed; it does not resolve the grammar beyond what a source states. (4) The refusal of spoil is noted once (9:10 or 9:15–16), from the text; whether it answers Saul, who took the spoil of Agag (1 Sam. 15:9, 19), is a *lead* to be cut unless a source read draws it. (5) The ten sons hanged after death and the second day: told from the text, with Belnap's account of what public hanging meant; **no defense of Esther's request and no condemnation of it in the site's own voice**, and no comparison with modern events. The `christ` section of chapter 9 rests on deliverance remembered (9:22, sorrow to joy, gifts to the poor), not on the killing.
- **Purim's origin (3:7; 9:17–32).** *Scripture Helps* (3:7): “The Jewish feast of Purim gets its name from pur, the Persian word for ‘lot’”; (9:20–28) describes the feast as Jews keep it now. Bible Dictionary, Feasts (`bd-feasts`): Purim was “instituted by Mordecai and confirmed by Esther”; “The name Purim (lots) was given in mockery of the lots Haman had cast”; held on the 14th and 15th of Adar; the 13th, “originally a feast to commemorate a Maccabean victory, afterwards became a fast, called the Fast of Esther.” Hull (n. 88): Akkadian *pūru*, “a ‘lot,’ the dice used for lot-casting.” Bible Dictionary, Lots: Prov. 16:33 “expresses a feeling on the subject of the Lord's hand in the matter.” The site has `[[Ezra 6:15]]` (Adar, citing 3:7). **Proposed.** One note at 3:7 on the lot, its word and what it decided (with Prov. 16:33 as the scripture beside it, not as a claim that God guided Haman's lot); one at 9:20–28 on the feast as the text founds it (two days, and why two: 9:17–19), with *Scripture Helps*' and the Bible Dictionary's later customs said in the site's words and labelled as later. The Student Manual's long quotation of Herman Wouk (31-17) is not used. **The site does not say Latter-day Saints should keep Purim**, and does not treat it as one of the law's feasts (it is not in Leviticus 23).
- **Christ in the book.** No Church source read makes Esther or Mordecai a type of Christ, and the book never names God. What the sources do: the institute manual asks teachers to “Emphasize the example of Jesus Christ” and speaks of “Christlike courage” (the Savior's courage in Gethsemane, in *Come, Follow Me*'s children's section); Bingham and Gardner: by sacrifice we “not only save ourselves but be the literal saviors of others”; the footnote at 4:14 points to Joseph, sent before them “to preserve you a posterity in the earth.” **Proposed.** `christ` sections say, in statements, where a chapter's principle reaches a disciple: the Lord's hand in “small details” (Rasband, once), courage that relies on Him (4:16; the Savior's courage as the pattern, from the institute manual), fasting as reliance (*Come, Follow Me*), intercession for others at cost to oneself (7:3–4; Bingham's “saviors,” with `[[Obad. 1:21]]` if a source joins them), sorrow turned to joy (9:22). **Not used:** Esther as a type of Christ the intercessor, Mordecai as a type of the exalted Christ, the scepter as a type of grace, the three days as the Resurrection: no source read draws any of them. A writer who finds a Church or Latter-day Saint source that does may use it as that source's reading.
- **Restoration connections that are real.**

So shall it come to pass with poor Haman in the last days.” **Proposed:** one Restoration note at 7:10, the letter quoted from the Joseph Smith Papers (the writer reads the letter itself; the Papers have several copies, and the quotation follows one named copy). The footnotes at 7:10 (Matt. 7:2; 1 Ne. 14:3; D&C 10:26) are the reader's.
  - **D&C 91** on the Apocrypha, in the one note at 4:16.
  - **Fasting**: Book of Mormon parallels where a source draws them (KnoWhy #135). One `explore` line, not a note per chapter.
