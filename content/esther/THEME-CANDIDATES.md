# Esther: theme candidates

Topics that run across the book and might become theme pages (`STANDARDS.md` §8). **None is written until the author chooses it.** The candidates were Fable's and Codex's lists (`.cache/batch/esther/reports/themes-fable.md` and `themes-codex.md`), shown to the author combined on 2026-11-13; only the author's choice is recorded here.

## Author's selection (2026-11-13)

One page (author: “yes”, to “one page, ‘I and My People,’ with the irreversible decree as its opening”). Both readers proposed it and neither proposed another; Fable's second candidate, “May No Man Reverse,” is not a page of its own and supplies this page's opening.

### 1. “I and My People” (from [[Esth. 7:4]])

**The arc.** Esther begins as the one Jew the decree cannot find, her people and kindred hidden at Mordecai's charge (2:10, 20). Mordecai takes the shelter away (“Think not with thyself that thou shalt escape in the king's house,” 4:13–14), and she answers in the decree's own verb: “to cause to perish” (3:13) becomes “if I perish, I perish” (4:16; *ʾābad*, H6). She goes before the throne three times and the requests change: a banquet (5:4); “my life… and my people… we are sold, I and my people” (7:3–4); and, when Haman is dead and her own life is safe, for them alone, “how can I endure to see the evil that shall come unto my people? or how can I endure to see the destruction of my kindred?” (8:3–6). The two nouns she hid in chapter 2 return in 8:6 with “my” on them (*môledet*, H4138: only 2:10, 2:20, 8:6). **Folded in from “May No Man Reverse”:** the opening, the law no one can revoke (1:19; 8:5, 8), which is why an advocate is needed and why deliverance comes as a second writing that meets the first, not a repeal.

**Thesis.** An advocate is one who could stand outside a sentence and takes it on herself before the throne; the three things Esther's story cannot do (she does not perish, she pleads “if I have found favour,” and the enemy, not the advocate, hangs on the tree) measure what Jesus Christ did, the Advocate who did perish, on the tree, and who pleads that death.

**To Christ:** [[Heb. 2:11–17]] (“took part of the same,” “made like unto his brethren”); [[Heb. 7:25]]; [[1 Jn. 2:1]]; [[D&C 45:3–5]] (“the sufferings and death of him who did no sin”); [[Mosiah 15:8–9]] (“standing betwixt them and justice”); [[Heb. 11:24–26]] (Moses in Pharaoh's house, “choosing rather to suffer affliction with the people of God,” scripture's own reading of a Hebrew at a foreign court); [[John 12:27]] beside 4:14; [[Heb. 4:14–16]] against 4:11 (the sceptre that may or may not be held out; the throne of grace approached boldly); [[Deut. 21:22–23]] and [[Gal. 3:13]] (“hanged on the tree”). For the opening only, and with care: [[Alma 42:13–25]]. **Applies:** Christ's advocacy is not a kind word spoken from a safe place; He first made our sentence His own, so disciples “come boldly unto the throne of grace.”

**Must not claim:** that Esther is a type of Christ, or that any Church source calls her one (none does; the 1982 Student Manual's “Advocate for Her People,” the institute manual's “Christlike courage,” and Bingham and Gardner's “the literal saviors of others” come close, and the page says the comparison is its own); that Haman's decree figures God's law, or that the second decree (a licence to fight) figures the Atonement; that 9:31 commemorates Esther's fast; that the text names God; that Esther's motive in hiding her people was anything the text does not give. Check every Hebrew count with `hebrew.mjs` before the page leans on it (Fable's: H6 thirteen times in the book, H4138 three, H8275 the sceptre at 4:11, 5:2, 8:4; H8518 with H6086 in eight verses).

## Not chosen

Providence without a name; the great reversal; Amalek from Exodus 17 to Esther 9; banquets, fasting to feasting, the royal chronicle, decrees in every language; Haman's pride; Joseph and Daniel at foreign courts; power used for others; deliverance made a memorial. Each is carried by the chapter notes, stays in one place, or does not reach Christ's work.
