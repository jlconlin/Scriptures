Writers, checkers, and reviewers read both, whole, before starting a chapter. Jeremiah's brief (`content/jeremiah/BRIEF.md`) is the model, and its section “What a chapter is for” holds here.

## What the author has decided

Nothing for Exodus in particular. The author's standing rules are in `STANDARDS.md` §1 and §3 and in `AUTHORING.md`, and are not repeated here. How four of them land on this book:

- **Newest trumps oldest.** The newest Church source is *Scripture Helps: Old Testament* (2025), five chapters on Exodus. The Student Manual (five chapters, 1980) is older; where they differ, follow *Scripture Helps* and don't repeat the older wording.
- **Don't replicate the Gospel Library.** *Scripture Helps* answers some sixty questions on Exodus verse by verse (the list is under “Sources”), and the Joseph Smith Translation readings stand in the footnotes and the appendix. A note does not repeat one of those answers; it goes where the help stops, usually to the study the help's endnote cites, or it is not written. For the same reason a chapter on the tabernacle does not reproduce the table of furnishings and their meanings that *Scripture Helps* prints for Exodus 35–40.
- **Hebrew only to answer a reader's question** (`STANDARDS.md` §3). Start from the English verse. Bring in a Hebrew word when the King James word is obscure or misleading (“kill,” “peculiar treasure,” “mercy seat,” “borrow,” “shittim,” “badgers' skins,” “repented”), when a disputed rendering changes the reading, or when the passage turns on one word the English hides. No more than about a quarter of a chapter's notes are Language notes, and a chapter may have none.
- **Quote the Church's modern material sparingly.** Joseph Smith's sentence on Israel being “cursed … with a carnal law,” President Holland on what was on the first tables, and President Nelson on covenants are all printed in *Scripture Helps*; each is quoted at most once on the site, at the place this brief names, from its own page.

## What a chapter is for here

Exodus is three kinds of writing, and a reader's questions differ in each.

**Story (1–20, 24, 32–34).** The best-known narrative in the Old Testament, so the reader arrives with a picture already. The questions are the ones the picture raises: *Who was the Pharaoh? Why would God harden a man's heart and then punish him? Why does the Lord try to kill Moses on the road? Did all the firstborn die? Which sea? Why a calf? How can Moses speak with God face to face in one verse and be told no man can see Him in another?* Latter-day revelation answers several of these outright, and where it does the note says so plainly.

**Law (20:22–23:33).** The reader's question is rarely “what does this require” and usually “why would God command this”: servants, an eye for an eye, the death penalty for cursing a parent. A chapter of law gets a `thread` that says how its judgments hang together and what they protect, notes on the laws a reader would stop at, and no note per statute.

**The tabernacle (25–31, 35–40).** Thirteen chapters, a third of the book, and the place readers stop reading. The question is always *what is this thing and why does it matter*. Every furnishing and garment is explained once, where the Lord commands it (25–31), and the chapters where it is made (35–40) point back. See “The tabernacle, told twice.”

**Short chapters are right for some of this.** Chapters 36–39 repeat 25–28 nearly word for word; three or four notes and a short `thread` is a full chapter. `setting` may then fall under the checker's 120 words, and the warning is ignored. Don't pad.

**Say what the passage is before what it foreshadows.** The Passover is first the night Israel left Egypt, then the lamb Paul and John see in Christ. Give the first, then the second, and take the second from scripture and the Church's sources (“Types of Christ,” below).

## The tabernacle, told twice

Chapters 25–31 are the Lord's instructions; 35–40 tell of the making in nearly the same words, in a different order (the building first, then the furniture, the garments last), with chapters 32–34 between. **Proposed:**

- **25–31 carry the explanations.** One note for each furnishing or garment a reader would ask about: what it was (plainly, with the Bible Dictionary's “Tabernacle” where a measurement or arrangement helps), what was done at it, and what scripture and the Church's sources say it signified. No note on a board, a socket, a tache or a loop unless the King James word stops the reader.
- **35–40 are short chapters about what is new.** The Sabbath set before the work (35:2), the willing offering and the wise-hearted women (35:21–29), more than enough (36:5–7), the looking glasses of the women at the door (38:8), the sum of the metals (38:21–31), “as the LORD commanded Moses” repeated through 39 and 40, Moses blessing the work (39:43), the first day of the first month (40:2, 17), the cloud and the glory (40:34–38). For everything else `plain` says briefly what was made and the `thread` says where the instruction is, with a `[[reference]]`. Why the account is told twice, and why the calf stands between the telling, is said once, in chapter 35's `thread`.
- **What the tabernacle means, from sources.** Scripture reads it: Hebrews 8–10 (the earthly sanctuary a “shadow of heavenly things”; the veil; the high priest entering once a year), `[[John 1:14]]`, `[[Rev. 21:3]]`. The Church's sources call it “a portable temple” (Bible Dictionary) and say that “each space represented an increasing proximity to God's holy presence” (*Scripture Helps*). Studies read its three spaces and its furniture as a way back into God's presence (Belnap and the *Old Testament Minute*, Latter-day Saint; Morales, in *Interpreter*, is by his own biography a Presbyterian elder: call his “one study,” not a Latter-day Saint one). Say what the text and Hebrews say plainly; give a scholar's reading of a furnishing as a reading.
- **The modern temple.** *Scripture Helps* says “Like modern temples, the tabernacle was the house of the Lord,” and of the priests' clothing, “Like clothing associated with temple ordinances in our day, the clothing worn by Israelite priests was sacred and deeply symbolic.” A chapter may say that much, citing it. **No note draws a line of its own from a furnishing, a garment or a rite to the endowment or to the garment worn today**, and none describes modern temple ordinances beyond what the Church's own published pages say.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, what an article argues, which later scripture takes up which verse) is direction about where to look, some of it from memory. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **`when`.** The book dates itself from the departure: the first month and its fourteenth day (12:2, 6), “the fifteenth day of the second month” (16:1), “the third month” (19:1), “the first month in the second year, on the first day of the month” (40:17). Use those. Before chapter 12, say where the chapter falls in the story (“During the oppression, before Moses's birth”; “Moses's eightieth year, by 7:7”). Give no B.C. year in `when`; the year is treated once, at 1:11.
- **Who wrote Exodus.** Said once, in chapter 1's `setting`, in two sentences: latter-day revelation names Moses (`[[Moses 1:40–41]]`; `[[1 Ne. 5:11]]`), and the Bible Dictionary (“Pentateuch”) adds that “scribes and copyists have left their traces.” Genesis 1 on the site has the fuller statement; link, and no later chapter argues authorship. Where an article read for a chapter says a passage “demonstrates inconsistencies that suggest multiple texts,” that is reported only if it answers a reader's question (a story that seems told twice), as that author's view.
- **Names of God.** Copy any phrase with LORD, Lord or GOD from `data/kjv/exodus.json`: the capitals were checked verse by verse. The identification of the LORD with Jesus Christ is made once (table above).
- **Moses 1 belongs between the bush and the return to Egypt.** `[[Moses 1:17]]` looks back on the bush and `[[Moses 1:25–26]]` forward to the sea and the deliverance. Chapter 3 or 4 says so once, where it answers a reader's question (what Moses knew when he went back); it is not retold. Moses 1 is on the site: link.
- **The Joseph Smith Translation** has appendix entries for six chapters (4:21 and 4:24–27; 18:1; 22:18; 32:14; 33:20, 23; 34:1–2, 14) and readings in the footnotes of eighteen chapters (the saved `fn<n>.txt` marks them). Use one where it answers the reader's question, which in Exodus is often: the hardening, the road to Egypt, 6:3, 32:14, 33:20, 34:1–2. A reading the footnote shows and nobody would ask about is not a note. For a reading in the appendix cite `jst-ex-<chapter>`; for one only in a footnote, `lds-scriptures` with a ledger row.
- **The New Testament reads Exodus constantly**: Stephen's speech (Acts 7), `[[1 Cor. 5:7]]` and `[[1 Cor. 10:1–4]]`, John 6, Romans 9, Hebrews 3 and 8–12, `[[2 Cor. 3:7–18]]`, `[[1 Pet. 2:9]]`. Where the New Testament says what a passage meant, the chapter says so as scripture's own reading.
- **The Book of Mormon reads it too.** Nephi argues from the sea, the manna and the rock (`[[1 Ne. 4:2]]`; `[[1 Ne. 17:23–42]]`); Abinadi recites the commandments (Mosiah 12–13); Limhi and Alma look back on the deliverance. A note may say that a Nephite prophet takes up the passage where that shows how it was read. **No comparison panel, and nothing is written for a Book of Mormon chapter** (`AUTHORING.md`). Studies that argue Lehi's journey is patterned on the Exodus are given as their authors' reading, in `explore` if at all.
- **Other books on the site.** Genesis ends where Exodus begins: link for Joseph, Goshen, the Hyksos, the covenant with Abraham (`[[Gen. 15:13–14]]` foretells the bondage and the going out “with great substance”), Melchizedek and the priesthood, circumcision (`[[Gen. 17:10]]`). Psalms 78, 95, 105, 106, 114 and 136 retell the Exodus; Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel 20 and Hosea look back on it. Link where a chapter meets one; don't re-explain what the other page explains.
- **Graphic or violent passages** (the death of the firstborn, the drowning, 32:27–28): say what happened plainly and briefly; describe rather than quote where it is graphic (`STANDARDS.md` §3).
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports an arc that joins chapters in a way a reader would not see chapter by chapter; the coordinator proposes about ten at most after the book is written (`STANDARDS.md` §8). No theme page is written until the author chooses it.

**Habits to avoid:** say a thing once in a chapter; don't re-explain what another chapter explains; a Witness of Christ note does not open by saying what the chapter lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer, since the site shows it alone as the note's card; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; “In plain words” adds nothing to the verse; don't join two verses yourself and call it the text's meaning; no personal application, which here means no “our own Egypt” or “our own wilderness.”

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that forty chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-11.

- **When, and which Pharaoh.** The text names none. KnoWhy #847: the 480 years of `[[1 Kgs. 6:1]]` would give 1446 B.C., but the number “is almost certainly supposed to be a symbolic number”; Merneptah's stela gives the latest possible date; the city Raamses (1:11) points to Ramesses II, who “may well have been the pharaoh of the Exodus,” “although it is difficult to be completely sure.” Muhlestein: “we cannot ascertain with any certainty which pharaoh interacted with Moses,” only that it “had at least happened by the reign of Merneptah.” The Bible Dictionary (“Pharaoh”) names Seti I and Ramses II for the oppression and says Merneptah as the Pharaoh of the Exodus “is generally believed” but doubted. **Proposed:** one note at 1:11 gives the evidence in that order (the number in Kings, the stela, the city) and the thirteenth century as the leading proposal, not a fact. Every other chapter says “Pharaoh.”
- **Did it happen as told?** Latter-day revelation treats the bondage, the plagues, the sea, the manna and Sinai as history (`[[1 Ne. 17:23–42]]`; `[[Hel. 8:11]]`; `[[Moses 1:25–26]]`; D&C 8:3; D&C 84). The Bible Dictionary (“Red Sea”): the parting “is confirmed in latter-day revelation.” No chapter argues the point or surveys doubt; Egyptian background (Muhlestein; KnoWhys #847, #848, #631; Gee's “Edfu and Exodus”) comes in where it helps a reader picture the event.
- **Six hundred thousand men (12:37).** The Student Manual has an enrichment section, “The Problem of Large Numbers in the Old Testament.” **Proposed:** one note at 12:37 says what the verse says, what the number implies (well over two million people), and how the manual treats the question, from that page; no chapter builds on a total.
- **The hardening of Pharaoh's heart.** *Scripture Helps*: “in the Joseph Smith Translation, each such passage is revised to say that Pharaoh hardened his own heart. These revisions emphasize Pharaoh's personal agency in resisting God's will.” The text itself also says Pharaoh hardened his own heart (8:15, 32; 9:34), and Paul reasons from 9:16 in `[[Rom. 9:17–18]]`. **Proposed:** the note at 4:21 gives the reader's difficulty, the JST's answer as the Church's reading, the verses where Exodus itself says Pharaoh did it, and whatever the Hebrew verbs add (a lead: there are three; say it only if a source read applies it). An outside explanation is not set beside the JST as an equal. `plain` in chapters 7–14 follows the King James wording of each verse and does not re-argue.
- **The night lodging (4:24–26).** *Scripture Helps*: the account “is difficult to understand because the text lacks many important details”; the JST says the Lord was angry “for failing to circumcise his son” and that Zipporah's act spared Moses. The JST (appendix, `jst-ex-4`) is the answer; say plainly that the Hebrew is obscure, and don't survey the theories.
- **Moses slays the Egyptian (2:11–15).** *Scripture Helps*: the word for “smiting” is the word for “slew” and “describes the violent action of soldiers,” so “Moses killed the Egyptian to save the life of the Hebrew” and “was not guilty of murder.” That is beside the verse already; a note goes further only with Acts 7:23–29 and a source read.
- **The plagues.** *Scripture Helps*: they “clearly demonstrated that Jehovah was more powerful than the gods of Egypt, including Pharaoh, who was seen by the Egyptians as a god.” Muhlestein doubts a plague-by-plague matching with single deities (many have tried it “with varying degrees of success”) and reads the whole as “a contest between Jehovah and Pharaoh,” the god-king who was Egypt's mediator; KnoWhy #631 is on why particular plagues were sent. **Proposed:** the contest with Pharaoh and with “all the gods of Egypt” (12:12) is the frame, from the text; a link between one plague and one god is given only from a source read, as its proposal. Natural explanations of the plagues are not surveyed; if one is mentioned it is in a clause, and the text's own point (timing, the exemption of Goshen, Moses's word) is the answer. On the magicians *Scripture Helps* quotes President Oaks; don't repeat it.
- **The death of the firstborn, Amalek, and the three thousand (12:29; 17:14–16; 32:27–28).** The Church's appendix on violence in the Old Testament (`scripture-helps-violence`, in `sources.yaml`) is the frame; read it before writing any of the three. *Scripture Helps* on Amalek: Deuteronomy “adds the important detail that the Amalekites attacked the rear of the camp, killing the most vulnerable.” Say what the text says, what its own reasons are (1:22 stands behind 12:29; 4:22–23 announces it), and what the Church's sources say. Don't soften it and don't defend it beyond them.
- **The Red Sea.** *Scripture Helps*: the Hebrew “can also be translated as ‘Reed Sea’ or ‘Sea of Reeds,’” so “some believe that the Israelites may have camped near some other body of water. However, other evidence suggests that the text is indeed referring to the Red Sea. Regardless … the scriptures suggest that the sea they crossed was a large body of water.” And: “The exact location where the Israelites camped after their departure from Egypt is unclear.” **Proposed:** one note at 13:18 on the name and the unknown route; chapter 14 is about the crossing, which latter-day revelation confirms, and about what Paul and the Doctrine and Covenants make of it (`[[1 Cor. 10:1–2]]`; `[[D&C 8:2–3]]`). Where Sinai is: “a traditional site” (*Scripture Helps*, caption); say the location is not known, once, at 19:1.
- **Sinai: what was offered and what was given.** The heart of the Latter-day Saint reading of the book. `[[D&C 84:19–27]]`: Moses “sought diligently to sanctify his people that they might behold the face of God; but they hardened their hearts and could not endure his presence,” so the Lord “took Moses out of their midst, and the Holy Priesthood also; and the lesser priesthood continued.” JST Ex. 34:1–2 (`jst-ex-34`): the second tables are “not … according to the first, for I will take away the priesthood out of their midst.” *Scripture Helps*: the people's drawing back at 20:18–21 and the calf together are why “the Lord withheld His higher law from them”; the first tables held “the Lord's instructions for the ordinances and covenants of the Melchizedek Priesthood.” Studies: Belnap and Skinner, “The Promise and the Provocation”; M. Catherine Thomas, “The Provocation in the Wilderness and the Rejection of Grace”; Brandt, “The Law of Moses and the Law of Christ”; `[[Jacob 1:7]]`, `[[Alma 12:36]]`, `[[Ps. 95:8]]` and Hebrews 3 on “the provocation.” The Bible Dictionary (“Law of Moses”) adds the balance: the law was “a good law,” with “many provisions … of high ethical and moral value,” and faith, repentance and baptism “were part of the law.” **Proposed:** as the table divides it (19:10; 20:19; 32:19; 34:1). The chapters of law (21–23) are therefore not presented as punishment: say what each law protects.
- **The Ten Commandments.** The Bible Dictionary (“Commandments, the Ten”) notes “a difference of opinion as to the way in which the commandments were divided into ten”; give the Church's numbering and say in a clause that others count differently. **“Thou shalt not kill”**: *Scripture Helps* says the word “is better understood as ‘murder.’” It is beside the verse; a note is written only if it goes further (the Hebrew verb and where else it is used, from `hebrew.mjs` and a lexicon page). Each commandment a reader would ask about gets a note that says what it forbade or required then; the *Ensign*'s 1994 series on the commandments and the conference talks are for a Latter-day Prophets note where an apostle's teaching answers the question. Abinadi's recital and `[[D&C 59:5–13]]` show how the Restoration restates them.
- **The laws a modern reader stops at (21–23).** Bowen's chapter (“The Three Law Codes of the Pentateuch”) is the Latter-day Saint study: the “Covenant Code” has been compared with Hammurabi's laws, though “direct textual dependency remains unclear at best,” and it “is framed as a direct revelation from God.” **Servants (21:2–11, 20–21, 26–27):** say what the law is (a term of six years, release, protections) and what kind of servitude it regulates, from a source read; the law regulates an institution and the chapter neither excuses nor applies it. **An eye for an eye (21:24):** what it limited, then `[[Matt. 5:38–39]]`, with Bowen on the Savior restoring “the true ethic underlying the Mosaic legislation.” **“Thou shalt not suffer a witch to live” (22:18):** the JST reads “Thou shalt not suffer a murderer to live” (`jst-ex-22`); say that, and nothing on later history. **Death penalties (21:12–17; 22:19–20; 31:14–15):** what the law says and what it marks as gravest; no survey. **The stranger, the widow, the fatherless, the poor (22:21–27; 23:9):** these are the chapter's center of gravity, and the reason given (“for ye were strangers”) is the thread.
- **The golden calf.** *Scripture Helps*: “Some scholars have suggested that the creation of the golden calf may have been a misguided attempt by the Israelites to remember Jehovah or draw upon His power”; Aaron “may have believed that the golden calf represented Jehovah.” Belnap and Skinner: “even if the golden calf was made to represent Yahweh,” it was an idol and broke the commandment just given. **Proposed:** the note on 32:4–5 gives that reading as a suggestion, from the article, with the feast “to the LORD” in the text; Aaron's part is told as the text tells it, without excusing or condemning beyond it. **“The LORD repented” (32:14):** the JST (`jst-ex-32`) answers it. **Moses's offer (32:32)** and his pleading are where the chapter points to Christ, from *Scripture Helps* and scripture (the Advocate); as a comparison, not an identification.
- **Seeing God.** 24:10–11 (“they saw the God of Israel”), 33:11 (“face to face”), 33:20 (“there shall no man see me, and live”), 33:23 (“my back parts”). *Scripture Helps*: the JST of 33:20 adds that “no sinful man hath at any time, neither shall there be any sinful man at any time, that shall see my face and live.” Also `[[Moses 1:2, 11]]`, `[[D&C 67:11]]`, `[[D&C 84:22]]`, `[[John 1:18]]` with its JST. One full note at 33:20; the theophany studies (Hardison; Muhlestein, “Darkness, Light, and the Lord”) for what the fire, cloud and darkness on the mountain meant.
- **Types of Christ.** Scripture makes these itself: the Passover lamb (`[[1 Cor. 5:7]]`; `[[John 1:29]]`; `[[John 19:36]]` with 12:46), the manna (`[[John 6:31–35, 48–51]]`), the rock (`[[1 Cor. 10:4]]`), the sea and the cloud as baptism (`[[1 Cor. 10:1–2]]`), the veil (`[[Heb. 10:19–20]]`), the high priest and the mercy seat (Hebrews 9), Moses as the prophet like unto whom one would be raised (`[[Deut. 18:15]]`; `[[Acts 3:22]]`; `[[3 Ne. 20:23]]`; `[[Moses 1:6]]`, “thou art in the similitude of mine Only Begotten”). *Scripture Helps* adds the tree at Marah, the blood sprinkled at 24:8, the firstborn, and Moses's intercession. **Proposed:** where scripture makes the type, say so without hedging; where only a Church manual makes it, give it as the manual's (“can remind us of”), once; where a scholar makes it, as that author's reading; and **no typology of the writer's own** (a color, a number, a metal, a board). A `christ` section for a chapter with nothing of this kind is short and rests on what the chapter says of the LORD.
- **Women in the narrative.** The midwives, Jochebed, Miriam, Pharaoh's daughter, Zipporah, the women who spun and gave their mirrors. Rosalynde Welch's reflection and Tilton's *Interpreter* article read chapters 1–2 as a deliverance carried by women; say what the text shows, and give a reading as its author's. Miriam “the prophetess” (15:20) is a real reader's question; answer it from a source read.

## Settled without the author

A reviewer does not ask the author whether to do what the standard already says (`AUTHORING.md`, “Decide, don't defer”; one or two questions a book is the measure). Each of these is decided, not asked:

- **No Church, BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* source answers a reader's real question, and an older commentary does:** give its answer as “one older reading,” within the limit of a third of the notes. If the question isn't one a reader would ask, there is no note.
- **Two passages share words and no source joins them:** a note may set them side by side as shared wording and say so; it draws nothing from the pairing. A pairing of ideas, not words, goes to `explore` or is cut.
- **The King James reads a verse one way and the Hebrew or a modern translation another:** the paraphrase, tagline and thread start from the King James; a well-supported clarification from the Hebrew or another translation may go into the paraphrase when it materially helps, and then a visible note explains the difference (`STANDARDS.md` §6); a disputed rendering is not presented as settled. Where the JST changes the verse, the note gives the JST as the Church's reading.
- **The Church's footnote points somewhere the note does not go:** leave it.
- **Two faithful sources read a verse differently:** give both, the Church's first, without choosing.
- **A teaching that is not a prophet's or apostle's** goes under another kind than Latter-day Prophets.
- **A source quoted inside a Church manual** whose own work can't be read is cited as the manual, for what the manual prints, as one view.
- **What the Restoration adds to a passage** may be said from scripture and the Church's reference works, as addition.

What does go to the author: a reading that touches doctrine where the Church's own sources pull two ways, or a faithful scholar's speculation the author may not want on the site.
