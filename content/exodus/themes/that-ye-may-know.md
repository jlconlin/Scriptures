Pharaoh’s first words about the God of Israel are a refusal to know Him: “Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice to let Israel go? I know not the LORD, neither will I let Israel go” (5:2). The rest of the book answers him, and it answers in his own verb. The Hebrew verb “know,” *yādaʿ*, occurs forty-five times in Exodus, and the sentence “that I am the LORD,” *kî ʾănî YHWH*, eleven times. Ten of the eleven follow a form of “know.”[@oshb-wlc] Five of the ten are said of Egypt or Pharaoh (7:5, 7:17, 8:22, 14:4, 14:18) and five of Israel (6:7, 10:2, 16:12, 29:46, 31:13).

The two sets are not the same sentence. When Pharaoh is told what he shall know, the added words tell where and how far: “in the midst of the earth” (8:22; and in the plague sayings, “none like me in all the earth,” 9:14, and “the earth is the LORD’s,” 9:29). Four of Israel’s five describe His relationship to Israel: “the LORD *your God*, which bringeth you out” (6:7), “the LORD *your God*” (16:12), “the LORD *their God*, that brought them forth … that I may dwell among them” (29:46), “the LORD that doth sanctify *you*” (31:13); the fifth, 10:2, adds nothing. Egypt is to learn that He is the LORD. Israel is to learn that He is theirs. Among them one outsider says, “Now I know” (18:11), and after all of them Moses asks for what no clause had promised: “shew me now thy way, that I may know thee” (33:13). Twice Moses is told, “I know thee by name” (33:12, 17).

The commentaries list the sentences together and gloss them; none of them follows the change in what is to be known from Egypt to Israel to Moses, and reading that change as the book’s own answer to 5:2 is mine. So is the last step, where I set Moses’s request beside the Savior’s prayer that His disciples “might know thee the only true God” (John 17:3). I say at each point what the sources carry and what I add.

## “I know not the LORD”

Pharaoh’s question is not idle. Ellicott takes it to mean “that Jehovah can have no authority over him, being not one of his gods,” and Keil grants it “a certain truth,” since God “had not yet made Himself known to him,” though it was “no justification.”[@ellicott-exodus][@keil-exodus] The Lord’s first reply is not a plague but a name. “I am the LORD: and I appeared unto Abraham, unto Isaac, and unto Jacob, by the name of God Almighty, but by my name JEHOVAH was I not known to them” (6:2–3). Read as saying that the fathers did not know the name, the verse contradicts Genesis; the Joseph Smith Translation makes it a question, “And was not my name known unto them?”, Scripture Helps notes the contradiction and the question, and the Student Manual answers it: “The answer is yes!”[@scripture-helps-ex-1][@ot-manual-ex-1-10] Ellicott reaches a similar place by translation, “was I not made manifest to them,” since the name “was ancient, and known to the patriarchs,”[@ellicott-exodus] and Keil says what the new knowing will be: the fathers knew Him as El Shaddai, “but now He was about to reveal Himself to Israel as Jehovah, as the absolute Being working with unbounded freedom in the performance of His promises.”[@keil-exodus]

That is the knowing promised in the first of the ten sentences, which is Israel’s, not Egypt’s: “I will take you to me for a people, and I will be to you a God: and ye shall know that I am the LORD your God, which bringeth you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians” (6:7). The Cambridge commentary glosses the verb “learn by what you witness and experience,” lists 7:5, 14:4, 14:18, 16:12 and 29:46 beside it, and adds that sentences of this type are “extremely common” in Ezekiel, “more than 50 times altogether.”[@cambridge-exodus] The Church’s footnote at “know” leads to 1 Nephi 17:13, where the Lord tells another family in another wilderness, “ye shall know that it is by me that ye are led,” and the footnote at “God” leads forward to 29:45–46, the dwelling.[@lds-scriptures] The book’s order, then, is not Egypt first and Israel after. Israel hears the promise before the plagues begin, and hears it again in the middle of them (10:2). What the plagues add is the other set.

## “The Egyptians shall know”

“And the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD, when I stretch forth mine hand upon Egypt, and bring out the children of Israel from among them” (7:5). Ellicott calls this “one of the main lessons intended to be taught by the whole series of miraculous events connected with the Exodus,” and Scripture Helps says the plagues “clearly demonstrated that Jehovah was more powerful than the gods of Egypt, including Pharaoh, who was seen by the Egyptians as a god.”[@ellicott-exodus][@scripture-helps-ex-7] Keil reads the sentences to Pharaoh as a graded course. In the first three plagues God showed him “that He, the God of Israel, was Jehovah (Exodus 7:17), i.e., that He ruled as Lord and King over the occurrences and powers of nature, which the Egyptians for the most part honoured as divine”; the fourth proved “that the God of Israel was Jehovah in the midst of the land (Exodus 8:22)”; from the last three “he had to learn … that there was no god in the whole earth like Jehovah.”[@keil-exodus] Each of Keil’s stages reaches farther than the last, and the farthest is not about Egypt at all: Pharaoh was spared “that my name may be declared throughout all the earth” (9:16), the verse Paul quotes in Romans 9:17.[@cambridge-exodus]

Egypt will know “when I stretch forth mine hand upon Egypt” (7:5); Israel will know Him as the one “which bringeth you out” (6:7). One act, read from two sides, is to teach two different things, and that pairing is my observation. Kerry Muhlestein says the same contest taught Israel too: “Jehovah would make it clear to the Israelites that he alone was divine; not Pharaoh, not the gods Pharaoh was one of or interacted with, but only Jehovah,” and he adds that a “fervent knowledge of the divine kingship of Jehovah, so forcefully demonstrated throughout the plagues narrative,” would make the covenant at Sinai “more meaningful and powerful.”[@muhlestein-pharaoh]

The narrative never says that Egypt came to know the LORD. Pharaoh never says “I know.” He says “Intreat the LORD” (8:8), “the LORD is righteous” (9:27), “I have sinned against the LORD your God” (10:16), “bless me also” (12:32), and his army says “the LORD fighteth for them” (14:25); none of these is the verb of 5:2. The one time the verb is used of him after 5:2, it is his servants asking, “knowest thou not yet that Egypt is destroyed?” (10:7). The last sentence said of Egypt is at the sea, “the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD, when I have gotten me honour upon Pharaoh” (14:18), and the chapter ends with Israel, not Egypt, and with a different verb: the people “believed the LORD, and his servant Moses” (14:31). Keil reads that verse as the fruit of the deliverance, “so wholesome a fear of the Lord, that they believed in Jehovah, and His servant Moses.”[@keil-exodus] After 14:18 no form of “know” in the book has Egypt or Pharaoh as its subject.[@oshb-wlc]

## “Now I know”

One person in Exodus says “Now I know,” and he is not an Israelite. Jethro, the priest of Midian, hears Moses tell “all that the LORD had done unto Pharaoh and to the Egyptians for Israel’s sake” (18:8), and says, “Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods” (18:11). He knows by report, not by plague, and his sentence is a comparison rather than “that I am the LORD.” Ellicott thinks he “adopted” the name from Moses and “expressed his conviction that Jehovah was exalted above all other gods”; Keil says that in him “the first-fruits of the heathen” who would seek the living God entered the camp, and that his words take in “all the plagues inflicted upon Egypt down to the destruction of Pharaoh and his army in the Red Sea.”[@ellicott-exodus][@keil-exodus] Scripture Central’s *Old Testament Minute* sets his words beside 6:7 and calls them “exactly the point that God made to the Israelites during the plagues.”[@ot-minute-ex-18]

## “That I may dwell among them”

The form “and they shall know,” *wĕyādĕʿû*, occurs four times in Exodus. Three are of Egypt (7:5, 14:4, 14:18). The fourth is 29:46.[@oshb-wlc] It comes at the end of the instructions for the tabernacle and the priests, and it is the only one of Israel’s five that gives a purpose past the knowing: “And they shall know that I am the LORD their God, that brought them forth out of the land of Egypt, that I may dwell among them: I am the LORD their God” (29:46). The Cambridge commentary says they will know “by the evidences of His presence in their midst,” and sends the reader to the manna, 16:6 and 16:12, where Israel was told it would know “at even” and “in the morning.”[@cambridge-exodus] Ellicott reads it as God’s “after care of them” proving Him “the same loving and all-powerful God whose help effected their deliverance from the bondage of Egypt.”[@ellicott-exodus] L. Michael Morales reads the tabernacle as the point the book is moving toward: “The building of the tabernacle, then, with the establishment of its cult, may be seen as a major goal of the exodus,” and he calls 25:8 and 29:45–46 “not merely incidental but programmatic.”[@morales-tabernacle]

None of the commentaries I read takes 29:46 as the goal of the plagues’ “that they may know,” and I do not either; the verse looks back to 6:7, as the footnotes in both directions show.[@lds-scriptures] What I notice is narrower. Egypt was to know the LORD by what He did to Egypt; Israel is to know the LORD their God by His living among them. The last of Israel’s five follows at once and names the means: the Sabbath, “that ye may know that I am the LORD that doth sanctify you” (31:13). Scripture Helps: the Sabbath was a “sign” of their covenant relationship, and “as they observed His Sabbath, the Lord promised to make His covenant people holy.”[@scripture-helps-ex-19] The Cambridge commentary notes that the Hebrew gives the verb no subject, “simply to know,” so that “all the world may recognize, by means of the sabbath, that it is Jehovah who ‘sanctifies’ Israel.”[@cambridge-exodus] What is added for Egypt says where He is Lord. What is added for Israel says what He does for Israel: brings out, feeds, dwells among, sanctifies.

## “That I may know thee”

Then the book turns to one man. After the golden calf, Moses pleads: “Thou hast said, I know thee by name, and thou hast also found grace in my sight. Now therefore, I pray thee, if I have found grace in thy sight, shew me now thy way, that I may know thee” (33:12–13). Three things are new here. The Lord’s knowing of Moses comes first; Keil notes that “though it has not been mentioned before, Jehovah had said to Moses, ‘I have known thee by name,’ – i.e., I have recognised thee as Mine, and chosen and called thee,” and the Cambridge commentary glosses the phrase “know thee individually, more intimately than the other Israelites.”[@keil-exodus][@cambridge-exodus] The verb changes direction: “shew me” is, the Cambridge commentary says, “lit. make me to know,” a form of the same verb that means causing someone to know.[@cambridge-exodus] And the object changes. Every earlier promise had been to know *that* something is true of the LORD. Moses asks to know *thee*.

The Cambridge commentary paraphrases the request as “understand what Thy nature and character is, and shape my petitions accordingly,” and for the “way” Moses asks to be shown it points to 34:6–7, where the Lord passes by and proclaims His name, “The LORD, The LORD God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth” (34:6).[@cambridge-exodus] Keil reads the request as Moses asking not to be left “in uncertainty as to Thine intentions concerning the people,” so “that my finding grace in Thine eyes may become a reality.”[@keil-exodus] The Lord’s “I will do this thing also that thou hast spoken” (33:17) is, on both commentators’ reading, the answer to Moses’s request in 33:15–16, that He go with Israel Himself, and the Lord closes it by repeating what Moses began with: “for thou hast found grace in my sight, and I know thee by name.”[@cambridge-exodus][@keil-exodus] What Moses asked at 33:13 is answered in the next chapter, in the name proclaimed, and that reading rests on the Cambridge note’s pointer and not on anything the verse itself says.

The Church’s footnote on “know” at 33:12 leads to John 10:14: “I am the good shepherd, and know my sheep, and am known of mine.”[@lds-scriptures] The LORD who said “I know thee by name” to Moses is, in the Church’s understanding, the premortal Jesus Christ; the Bible Dictionary says so under “Jehovah.”[@bd-jehovah] The footnote carries the knowing of Exodus 33 into the Gospel of John.

## “And this is life eternal”

In the prayer before Gethsemane the Savior says, “And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent” (John 17:3). No source I read sets that verse beside Exodus 33, and I set them together for one reason: the object. The Lord who answered Pharaoh with “they shall know that I am the LORD,” and Israel with “the LORD your God,” and Moses with “I know thee by name,” prays at the end of His mortal life that His own might “know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent.” The object Moses asked for is the object of the Savior’s definition of eternal life. Elder Robert D. Hales, speaking on that verse, testifies that “the way to know the truth about God is through the Holy Ghost.”[@hales-seeking-know] The New Testament Student Manual says that knowing God “means more than believing that He exists or having an intellectual understanding about Him; it means becoming acquainted with Him through personal experience and living His teachings,” and quotes Elder Bruce R. McConkie: “To know God is to think what he thinks, to feel what he feels, to have the power he possesses, to comprehend the truths he understands, and to do what he does.”[@nt-student-manual-27]

The book’s order holds for a disciple. The knowing that Exodus promises is learned, in the Cambridge commentary’s words, “by what you witness and experience,” and what Israel witnessed was what the Lord did for Israel: brought out, fed, dwelt among, sanctified.[@cambridge-exodus] The knowing Moses asked for came after the Lord had already known him by name. Elder C. Scott Grow, speaking of John 17:3, says, “God knows you and invites you to know Him”; that this is the order of 33:12–13 is my reading.[@grow-life-eternal-2017] Pharaoh asked “Who is the LORD?” and refused to know Him (5:2). Israel was told who He was to them, and learned it by being brought out and fed. Moses asked to know Him, and was told that he was already known. That is the order I read in Exodus, and the Savior’s prayer names its end.
