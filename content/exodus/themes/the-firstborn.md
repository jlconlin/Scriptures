The word “firstborn” stands twenty times in Exodus, in eleven verses, and most of the twenty are Egypt’s. Twelve name the firstborn the Lord smote at midnight, “from the firstborn of Pharaoh that sat on his throne unto the firstborn of the captive that was in the dungeon” (12:29). Five name Israel’s, and every one of the five is about the Lord claiming them or Israel buying them back (13:2, 13, 15; 22:29; 34:20). One names Reuben (6:14).[@oshb-wlc] The two that are left come before all the others, and the page turns on them: “Israel is my son, even my firstborn … if thou refuse to let him go, behold, I will slay thy son, even thy firstborn” (4:22–23).

The book, that is, calls Israel the Lord’s firstborn before any Egyptian firstborn is in danger, and the death in Egypt is announced as the answer to a refusal to release the Lord’s son. The morning after that death the Lord says of every firstborn in Israel, “it is mine” (13:2), and then tells Israel how to redeem them (13:13). Later the Levites stand in their place (Num. 3:12–13). And when Luke brings the infant Jesus to the temple, he quotes this law and no other: “Every male that openeth the womb shall be called holy to the Lord” (Luke 2:23).

Each of those steps is in the text or in the Church’s footnotes, and I say below where a commentator or a Church manual made the join before me. Reading them as one path is mine, and so is the last step. The child Luke sets under the words “it is mine” is the one Paul and the Doctrine and Covenants call the Firstborn, and President Howard W. Hunter says the Firstborn of God “had come to offer himself.”[@hunter-passover] The firstborn son in Exodus is redeemed; the firstling is offered. That in Jesus the two fates meet is my reading, not any source’s.

## “Israel is my son, even my firstborn”

Moses is still in Midian when the Lord gives him these words for Pharaoh (4:22–23). The Cambridge commentary notes that the title puts Israel, “treated as an individual,” in “the closest and dearest relation to Jehovah, as his ‘son’ (cf. Hosea 11:1), his ‘firstborn,’” and that the message is “formulated with special reference to the final and severest plague.”[@cambridge-exodus] Keil reads the sentence as a sentence of law: “if Pharaoh would not let Jehovah’s first-born son depart, he would pay the penalty in the life of his own first-born (cf. Exodus 12:29).”[@keil-exodus] The Church’s footnotes make the same join from both ends: the footnote on “son” at 4:23 leads to 11:5, where the plague is announced, and the footnote on “firstborn” at 11:5 leads back to 4:23.[@lds-scriptures] This is the reason the Lord gives Pharaoh for the tenth plague. The text does not give Pharaoh’s decree against the Hebrew boys in chapter 1 as the reason for the tenth plague, and neither do the footnotes or any commentary I read.

Keil adds that the firstborn “represented the whole race.”[@keil-exodus]

## “It is mine”

The night is told in three verses (12:12, 29–30), and what spared Israel is stated directly: “when I see the blood, I will pass over you” (12:13). The verb, *pāsaḥ*, is the root of “Passover,” and in Exodus it occurs only here, at 12:13, 23 and 27.[@bdb] Elder Gerrit W. Gong: “That passing by, or pass over, represents Jesus Christ ultimately overcoming death.”[@gong-hosanna-hallelujah]

Then, with Israel barely out of Egypt, the Lord speaks again, and the first thing He says is about the firstborn: “Sanctify unto me all the firstborn, whatsoever openeth the womb among the children of Israel, both of man and of beast: it is mine” (13:1–2). The Hebrew of the last clause is two words, *lî hûʾ*, “mine he.”[@oshb-wlc] Numbers states the ground: “all the firstborn are mine; for on the day that I smote all the firstborn in the land of Egypt I hallowed unto me all the firstborn in Israel” (Num. 3:13). Scripture Helps puts it the same way: “Having saved the firstborn of the faithful from death, the Lord claimed them as belonging to Him.”[@scripture-helps-ex-7] President John Taylor, as the Old Testament Student Manual quotes him, says that the Lord, “having saved the lives of all the first-born of Israel, made a claim upon them for their services in His cause.”[@ot-manual-ex-11-19]

Keil takes Numbers 3:13 as the ground too, but reads its order the other way from Scripture Helps and President Taylor. On his reading the Lord did not claim the firstborn because He had saved them; the sanctification “rested not upon the deliverance of the first-born sons from the stroke of the destroyer through the atoning blood of the paschal lamb, but upon the fact that God sanctified them for Himself at that time, and therefore delivered them,” and that rested in turn on His adopting Israel: “Jehovah sanctified the first-born of Israel to Himself by adopting Israel as His first-born son (Exodus 4:22).”[@keil-exodus] On either reading the Lord says “mine” before He says “redeem,” and the law that follows depends on that order. When the law is repeated among Israel’s covenant statutes (22:29), Keil calls the adoption of the firstborn “a perpetual guarantee to the whole nation of the right of covenant fellowship.”[@keil-exodus]

## “All the firstborn of my children I redeem”

The claim comes with a price. The firstborn is “all that openeth the matrix,” *peṭer reḥem*, a phrase Exodus uses only in these laws, at 13:2, 12, 15 and 34:19; the lexicon glosses *peṭer* as “that which separates, first opens.”[@bdb] For the firstling of a clean animal the law is sacrifice; for an ass, redemption with a lamb or death; for a son, redemption only: “all the firstborn of man among thy children shalt thou redeem” (13:13). The verb is *pādâ*, which the Cambridge commentary calls “the word used regularly of redeeming a person, or animal, from death or servitude,” and not *gāʾal*, the “redeem” of 6:6.[@cambridge-exodus] Keil says the sons too were consecrated “as a sacrifice,” but “not indeed in the manner of the heathen, by slaying and burning upon the altar, but by presenting them to the Lord as living sacrifices.”[@keil-exodus]

The father’s prescribed answer to his son gives the reason and the two fates in one sentence (13:14–15): “the LORD slew all the firstborn in the land of Egypt … therefore I sacrifice to the LORD all that openeth the matrix, being males; but all the firstborn of my children I redeem” (13:15). Every Israelite son who asked was to be told that the Lord slew the firstborn of Egypt, and that therefore he had been bought back.

Later a redemption price was fixed, and the Levites took the firstborn’s place. At Sinai the Lord took the Levites “instead of all the firstborn” (Num. 3:12), counted both, and had the 273 firstborn who exceeded the Levites redeemed at “five shekels apiece” (Num. 3:46–47). The Student Manual: the Levites acted “in the stead of the firstborn son,” and the excess “were redeemed by a five-shekel offering each.”[@ot-manual-numbers-1-12] The Bible Dictionary says the Levites “thus became God’s peculiar property, given to Him in place of the firstborn.”[@bd-levites] Scripture Helps adds what the firstborn had been for: “Originally, the firstborn male from each family was to serve in a priestly role in the tabernacle. Later, the tribe of Levi exclusively filled that role.”[@scripture-helps-ex-7] When the covenant is renewed on the second tablets the law returns almost word for word (34:19–20), and Keil files it there as “the consecration of the first-born connected with the Passover.”[@keil-exodus]

## “To present him to the Lord”

Luke quotes the law. “When the days of her purification according to the law of Moses were accomplished, they brought him to Jerusalem, to present him to the Lord; (As it is written in the law of the Lord, Every male that openeth the womb shall be called holy to the Lord;)” (Luke 2:22–23). The footnote on “womb” at Exodus 13:2 leads to Luke 2:23, and the footnote on Luke 2:23 leads back to Exodus 13:2 and 34:19.[@lds-scriptures] The Cambridge commentary on Luke names the law as Exodus 13:2, 22:29, 34:19 and Numbers 3:13, 18:15–16, with the Levites “sanctified to the Lord in lieu of the firstborn” and the five-shekel redemption “a rule afterwards extended to all the firstborn.”[@cambridge-luke] Ellicott calls the presentation “a witness of the idea of the priesthood of the firstborn—a survival of the idea in practice, even after the functions of that priesthood had been superseded by the priesthood of the sons of Aaron.”[@ellicott-luke] President Dallin H. Oaks, as the New Testament Student Manual quotes him: “In obedience to the law of Moses, Joseph and Mary brought the infant Jesus to the temple at Jerusalem after forty days, to present him to the Lord.”[@nt-student-manual-15]

Luke also leaves things out. He does not mention five shekels, and the Student Manual’s note on the passage names no redemption of the child; the offering it discusses, the pair of birds, is the mother’s under Leviticus 12.[@nt-student-manual-15] The Greek of 2:22 reads “their purification,” on which the Cambridge commentary observes, “Strictly speaking, the child was never purified, but only the mother.”[@cambridge-luke] The Bible Dictionary lists Luke 2:22–23 among the “special ordinances” provided for the firstborn’s redemption, and Andrew Skinner writes that Jesus was taken “to be presented to his Father and be redeemed or ransomed according to the Mosaic law”; Luke himself does not say so.[@bd-firstborn][@skinner-life-jesus] What Luke does say is that this child was set under the law of Exodus 13:2. And when he calls Him Mary’s “firstborn son” (2:7), the Cambridge commentary notes that the word does not imply other children, comparing Hebrews 1:6, “where first-born=only-begotten.”[@cambridge-luke]

## “The Firstborn of God”

The title the New Testament gives Him is the word Exodus has been using. He is “the firstborn of every creature” and “the firstborn from the dead; that in all things he might have the preeminence” (Col. 1:15, 18). The Cambridge commentary on Colossians reads the first as “priority of existence and supremacy of inherited right,” compares Psalm 89:27, where the Lord says of David, “I will make him my firstborn,” and reads the second as the same title carried into the resurrection: “He is not only the ‘First-fruits’ but the ‘First-Born’ in His resurrection.”[@cambridge-colossians] In the Doctrine and Covenants the Lord says it of Himself: “I was in the beginning with the Father, and am the Firstborn” (D&C 93:21). The Bible Dictionary gathers the senses: Jesus is “the firstborn of the spirit children of our Heavenly Father, the Only Begotten of the Father in the flesh, and the first to rise from the dead.”[@bd-firstborn] Scripture Helps, on Exodus 13:1–2, says the commandment “can remind us of Jesus Christ, who was the firstborn of all the spirit children of Heavenly Father,” and its endnote cites Colossians 1:14–18 and D&C 93:21.[@scripture-helps-ex-7]

The Guide to the Scriptures goes a step past a reminder: “The firstborn symbolized Jesus Christ and His earthly ministry, reminding the people that the great Messiah would come,” citing Moses 5:4–8, where Adam is told that the firstlings he offers are “a similitude of the sacrifice of the Only Begotten” (Moses 5:7).[@gs-firstborn] The Student Manual sets the plague beside the Atonement: “what finally released the children of Israel from the bondage of the pharaoh (the symbol of Satan) was the death of the firstborn of Egypt. In like manner the atoning sacrifice of the Firstborn Son of God freed the children of God from death, a bondage to Satan.”[@ot-manual-ex-11-19] And President Hunter, on how Jesus fulfilled the law of sacrifice: “No more would men be required to offer the firstborn lamb from their flock, because the Firstborn of God had come to offer himself as an ‘infinite and eternal sacrifice.’”[@hunter-passover]

In Exodus 13:15 the firstborn of beast is sacrificed and the firstborn of man is redeemed; the two fates stand in one verse. Luke brings a firstborn son to the temple under that law and records no price. President Hunter calls Him the Firstborn who “had come to offer himself,” without naming Luke 2; the Guide says the firstborn symbolized Him, without naming the two fates. Put together, the one presented as a son under that law went on to the fate the law assigned to the firstling, so that in Him the two fates meet. No source I read makes that join; it is this page’s reading and not a teaching of the Church.

## “The inheritance of the firstborn”

The claim at 13:2 did not end with the Levites. Hebrews calls the saints “the general assembly and church of the firstborn” (Heb. 12:23), and Ellicott reads the presentation in Luke 2 beside that verse, as “the expansion of the thought to the whole company of those who are the ‘firstborn.’”[@ellicott-luke] The revelation that calls Christ the Firstborn next describes those begotten through Him: “all those who are begotten through me are partakers of the glory of the same, and are the church of the Firstborn” (D&C 93:22). The Bible Dictionary: the faithful “receive through Jesus Christ the inheritance of the firstborn.”[@bd-firstborn]

The Student Manual carries the claim to everyone: Christ “came as the Redeemer, paying the price for all, and thus is justified in requesting that they serve Him,” and quotes Paul, “bought with a price” (1 Cor. 6:20).[@ot-manual-ex-11-19] President Taylor said the spared firstborn owed the Lord “their services in His cause.”[@ot-manual-ex-11-19] An Israelite son learned at his father’s knee that he lived because he had been bought back, and that he belonged to the One who bought him. The redeemed of the Firstborn are told the same thing, and are given His inheritance besides.
