The story of Sinai in Exodus is told in words of distance. Before the Lord comes down He says what He wants Israel to be: “ye shall be unto me a kingdom of priests, and an holy nation” (19:6), and He will come down “in the sight of all the people” (19:11). When He has come down, the people “stood afar off, and Moses drew near unto the thick darkness where God was” (20:21). When the covenant is made, seventy elders go up and “worship ye afar off,” and “Moses alone shall come near the LORD” (24:1–2). After the golden calf the Lord Himself withdraws: “I will not go up in the midst of thee” (33:3). And Joseph Smith’s revision of the account of the replacement tablets puts both losses in one sentence: “I will take away the priesthood out of their midst; therefore my holy order, and the ordinances thereof, shall not go before them; for my presence shall not go up in their midst” (JST Ex. 34:1).[@jst-ex-34]

The Hebrew keeps the measure. “Afar off,” *mērāḥōq*, stands four times in Exodus, and three of them are at Sinai: the people at 20:18 and 20:21, the seventy at 24:1. At Sinai, *nāgaš*, “draw near” to God, is said of the priests of 19:22, “which come near to the LORD,” and otherwise of Moses alone: at 20:21, and at 24:2, where the elders “shall not come nigh” and the people shall not go up at all.[@oshb-wlc] Ellicott and the Cambridge commentary read a priest as one “entitled to draw near” with “the right of access” to God, and 19:22 names the priests as those who come near.[@ellicott-exodus] So an offer that a whole people should be priests is an offer that a whole people should draw near, and the book records who did.

I read the repeated distance words as the book’s own measure of the offer and its loss; the commentaries discuss them verse by verse without tracing the pattern. The Church’s sources carry the loss itself, in Doctrine and Covenants 84 and the Joseph Smith Translation. The commentaries do not all agree that an offer was lost, and I say below where they differ. The close sets Hebrews’ “let us draw near” against “stood afar off.”

## “A kingdom of priests”

Ellicott reads the title as “kings and priests unto God … priests, as entitled to draw near to God in prayer without an intermediary,” and the Cambridge commentary as “a kingdom whose citizens are all priests, living wholly in God’s service, and ever enjoying the right of access to Him.”[@ellicott-exodus] Amy Blake Hardison: “God is inviting all of Israel to be priests, to have an intimate, personal relationship with him. This is unique in the ancient world.”[@hardison-theophany] Scripture Helps says the phrase “suggests a group of people who have received God’s priesthood authority and are responsible for administering the ordinances of the gospel,” and reads the offer through D&C 84: “the Lord had intended for the ordinances of the Melchizedek Priesthood to play an important role in the Israelites becoming a kingdom of priests and a holy nation that could ‘behold the face of God.’”[@scripture-helps-ex-19]

The offer comes with a date and a sight. “Be ready against the third day: for the third day the LORD will come down in the sight of all the people upon mount Sinai” (19:11). Bounds are set, crossing them carries death, and then: “when the trumpet soundeth long, they shall come up to the mount” (19:13). Who “they” are is disputed. Daniel Belnap and Andrew Skinner read the trumpet as marking “the time that the Israelites could now ascend the mountain,” and find the narrative that follows, where only Moses is called up, “a paradox”; the Old Testament Student Manual says the people “apparently … were not fully ready to come up ‘in the sight’ of the Lord.”[@belnap-skinner-sinai][@ot-manual-ex-11-19] Keil reads the verse the other way: the people might ascend “in the persons of their representatives,” and, “apart from the physical impossibility of 600,000 ascending the mountain, it never was intended that all the people should do so.”[@keil-exodus] The Cambridge commentary doubts the clause is in its original place at all.[@cambridge-exodus] The page does not settle this. What the text says without dispute is that the Lord came down in the sight of all the people (19:18, 20; 20:18), and what He said to all of them is in chapter 20.

## “And the people stood afar off”

“And all the people saw the thunderings, and the lightnings, and the noise of the trumpet, and the mountain smoking: and when the people saw it, they removed, and stood afar off. And they said unto Moses, Speak thou with us, and we will hear: but let not God speak with us, lest we die” (20:18–19). Moses answers, “Fear not: for God is come to prove you” (20:20), and the scene ends: “And the people stood afar off, and Moses drew near unto the thick darkness where God was” (20:21). The verb for “removed,” *nûaʿ*, means to sway or shake; the Cambridge commentary reads “the people were afraid, and trembled.”[@cambridge-exodus]

The commentaries stress the Lord’s approval of the request; the Latter-day Saint reading stresses what Israel lost. Ellicott: “God approved the people’s proposal, and directed that they should withdraw to their tents (Deuteronomy 5:28–30),” and he notes that “the same manifestation which repelled the people attracted Moses.”[@ellicott-exodus] Deuteronomy does record the approval: “They have well said all that they have spoken” (Deut. 5:28). The Church’s footnote at 20:19 leads to Deuteronomy 5:25 and 18:16, to Hebrews 12:19, and to D&C 84:21–26, where the request is read differently.[@lds-scriptures] D&C 84 says that Moses “sought diligently to sanctify his people that they might behold the face of God; But they hardened their hearts and could not endure his presence” (D&C 84:23–24). Scripture Helps grants that the Israelites heard the Ten Commandments, but “it appears that the Lord had intended for them to experience something more significant,” and it quotes Joseph Smith, “The Israelites prayed that God would speak to Moses and not to them; in consequence of which he cursed them with a carnal law.”[@scripture-helps-ex-19] Hardison keeps the request from being read as apostasy: it “does not … amount to a wholesale rejection of the covenant”; the people “place the burden and privilege of personally experiencing God on Moses. They prefer a less direct and less demanding experience.”[@hardison-theophany] Sharon Eubank puts the loss in a sentence: “They preferred to have Moses be their spokesman, but that isn’t a kingdom of priests.”[@eubank-kingdom-priests]

Both readings can be true of the same verse. The request was reasonable, and the Lord said so; it was also a smaller thing than He had offered, and the Doctrine and Covenants says that. This page follows the reading of a lost offer because the Doctrine and Covenants and the Joseph Smith Translation give it; Keil, who reads the bounds as the plan from the start, still grants that the people “gave up the right of speaking with God.”[@keil-exodus]

## “Moses alone shall come near”

“Come up unto the LORD, thou, and Aaron, Nadab, and Abihu, and seventy of the elders of Israel; and worship ye afar off. And Moses alone shall come near the LORD: but they shall not come nigh; neither shall the people go up with him” (24:1–2). The Cambridge commentary calls it a gradation: “the people generally are to remain at the foot of the mountain; Aaron, his two sons, and the elders come partly up the mountain; only Moses goes to the top.”[@cambridge-exodus] The same word that was said of the people, “afar off,” is now said of the elders. And yet the elders see: “they saw the God of Israel … and did eat and drink” (24:10–11). Keil reads this as what God had intended from the start: “What God really intended, came to pass. After the people had been received into fellowship with Jehovah through the atoning blood of the sacrifice, they were permitted to ascend the mountain in the persons of their representatives, and there to see God.”[@keil-exodus] Hardison, quoting Durham, calls it “a still more intimate contact with the Presence of Yahweh,” and the D&C Student Manual places the scene inside the story D&C 84 tells: the people covenanted (19:7–8), “some of them, including Aaron and two of his sons, ‘saw the God of Israel’ (Exodus 24:9–10),” and then “hardened their hearts and ‘corrupted themselves’ with idolatry.”[@hardison-theophany][@dc-student-manual-31] That is what the offer came to before the calf.

## “I will not go up in the midst of thee”

Moses comes down to the calf and “cast the tables out of his hands, and brake them beneath the mount” (32:19); Scripture Helps says the act “reflected Israel’s broken covenant.”[@scripture-helps-ex-19] Now the Lord withdraws. The Lord will send an angel, “for I will not go up in the midst of thee; for thou art a stiffnecked people: lest I consume thee in the way” (33:3), and again, “I will come up into the midst of thee in a moment, and consume thee” (33:5). Ellicott reads the withdrawal as mercy: “that they might not be consumed, as there was danger of their being unless they repented.”[@ellicott-exodus] Moses’s tent is pitched “without the camp, afar off from the camp” (33:7), and the chapter’s pleading is all for the presence: “If thy presence go not with me, carry us not up hence” (33:15); “let my Lord, I pray thee, go among us” (34:9).

The second tables come in that setting, and Joseph Smith’s revision says what did not come back with them. “It shall not be according to the first, for I will take away the priesthood out of their midst; therefore my holy order, and the ordinances thereof, shall not go before them; for my presence shall not go up in their midst, lest I destroy them” (JST Ex. 34:1), and the second verse has the oath of Psalm 95 in the Lord’s own mouth: “I have sworn in my wrath, that they shall not enter into my presence, into my rest, in the days of their pilgrimage” (JST Ex. 34:2).[@jst-ex-34] The revision of Deuteronomy names what the first tables had carried: the second have the words of the first “save the words of the everlasting covenant of the holy priesthood” (JST Deut. 10:2).[@jst-deut-10] D&C 84 tells the same story in order: Moses sought to sanctify the people “that they might behold the face of God; But they hardened their hearts … Therefore, he took Moses out of their midst, and the Holy Priesthood also; And the lesser priesthood continued” (D&C 84:23–26). The Bible Dictionary: “When the children of Israel failed to live up to the privileges and covenants of the Melchizedek Priesthood, the Lord took away the higher law and gave them a lesser priesthood and a lesser law.”[@bd-melchizedek-priesthood] The Guide to the Scriptures: “They had refused to be sanctified and receive the Melchizedek Priesthood and its ordinances.”[@gs-aaronic-priesthood] The Student Manual notes that the “it” of “it shall not be according to the first” refers to “the new order of things introduced because of the rebellion of Israel” rather than to the writing, since JST Deuteronomy 10:2 has the tables otherwise the same.[@ot-manual-ex-21-24]

In the Joseph Smith Translation the priesthood and the presence are withdrawn in one breath, and D&C 84:19–22 gives the reason: without the ordinances of the priesthood “no man can see the face of God, even the Father, and live.” Belnap and Skinner draw the consequence: “Instead of a holy nation, where all individuals could represent themselves directly to God, the priests began to act as intermediaries and represented the people before God.”[@belnap-skinner-sinai] Hardison says the people “are restored to a nation of priests, though not collectively,” the Levites bearing “a preparatory priesthood.”[@hardison-theophany] Kerry Muhlestein, writing for the *Ensign*, puts the people’s side plainly: “Though the Lord had more in store for them, the Israelites sent their leaders to Moses, telling him to converse with God alone.”[@muhlestein-exodus-deliverance]

## “Let us draw near”

Hebrews also sets Sinai against access to God. “Ye are not come unto the mount that might be touched, and that burned with fire … and the voice of words; which voice they that heard entreated that the word should not be spoken to them any more” (Heb. 12:18–19). The Church’s footnotes send the reader from “mount” to Exodus 19:12 and from “voice” to Exodus 20:19, so Hebrews recalls Israel’s request in Exodus 20:19.[@lds-scriptures] Then: “But ye are come unto mount Sion … and to Jesus the mediator of the new covenant” (Heb. 12:22, 24). Scripture Helps: the fire, tempest and trumpet are “an allusion to God’s presence on Mount Sinai … This awe-inspiring experience was preliminary. Believers are to look to another mountain, Mount Zion, for greater glory.”[@scripture-helps-nt-heb-7-13] Ellicott, quoting Delitzsch on these verses, says of Israel at Sinai what the Hebrew of Exodus had already said: “Their drawing nigh was at the same time a standing afar off; the mount of the revelation might not be approached by them.”[@ellicott-hebrews]

The oath of Psalm 95, “Unto whom I sware in my wrath that they should not enter into my rest” (Ps. 95:11), is quoted in Hebrews 3 and read there as the wilderness generation’s unbelief (Heb. 3:19). The psalm’s “provocation” is, in the Hebrew, the place name Meribah, as the Church’s footnote says, and the footnotes at Hebrews 3:8 lead to Numbers 14, not to Sinai.[@lds-scriptures] The Joseph Smith Translation and the Doctrine and Covenants put the oath at Sinai, in nearly the psalm’s words: the Lord “swore that they should not enter into his rest while in the wilderness, which rest is the fulness of his glory” (D&C 84:24), and Belnap and Skinner say that in D&C 84 “the event itself—the Israelites’ refusal to enter into God’s presence—is clearly the Exodus 19–20 narrative.”[@belnap-skinner-sinai] From the oath Hebrews draws its hope: “a promise being left us of entering into his rest” (Heb. 4:1), which Ellicott reads as a promise that “remains unexhausted, waiting for complete fulfilment.”[@ellicott-hebrews]

Peter writes to the Church in the words of 19:6, “a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people” (1 Pet. 2:9); the Church’s footnote on “holy” leads back to Exodus 19:6, and Ellicott calls the phrase “a direct quotation from Exodus 19:6.”[@lds-scriptures][@ellicott-1-peter] And Hebrews says to the Church what Exodus said of one man. “Let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith” (Heb. 10:22). In my reading, set beside “the people stood afar off, and Moses drew near,” the sentence is the offer of Sinai made again, to everyone; no source makes the pairing, and Delitzsch’s sentence above is the nearest.[@ellicott-hebrews]

## “The privilege of receiving the mysteries”

What was offered at Sinai is stated as a present offer in the Doctrine and Covenants. The greater priesthood “holdeth the key of the mysteries of the kingdom, even the key of the knowledge of God. Therefore, in the ordinances thereof, the power of godliness is manifest … For without this no man can see the face of God, even the Father, and live” (D&C 84:19–22). Its holders have “the privilege of receiving the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, to have the heavens opened unto them, to commune with the general assembly and church of the Firstborn, and to enjoy the communion and presence of God the Father, and Jesus the mediator of the new covenant” (D&C 107:19), a sentence whose footnote leads to Hebrews 12:22–24, the mount Sion passage.[@lds-scriptures] Joseph Smith drew this lesson from Sinai: “When God offers a blessing or knowledge to a man, and he refuses to receive it, he will be damned.”[@tpjs-2007-ch-22]

The offer, then, is the same one, and it is accepted the same way Israel was asked to accept it: by being sanctified for it (19:10–11; D&C 84:23). On this reading Israel feared the nearness and preferred a spokesman, and the Lord granted the smaller thing and withheld the greater. Scripture Helps says of the veil in Hebrews, “It is through Christ’s sacrifice that we are able to enter into God’s presence.”[@scripture-helps-nt-heb-7-13] Delitzsch’s sentence ends where this page does: under the new covenant the way to God is “opened through the Mediator of the New Covenant, accessible in virtue of His atoning blood.”[@ellicott-hebrews] His sacrifice opens the way across the distance Exodus measures, and “let us draw near” is Hebrews’ answer to “let not God speak with us.”
