When the Ten Commandments had been spoken, Israel asked for a man. “Speak thou with us, and we will hear: but let not God speak with us, lest we die” (Ex. 20:19). Deuteronomy gives the request in full, “Go thou near, and hear all that the LORD our God shall say: and speak thou unto us all that the LORD our God shall speak unto thee; and we will hear it, and do it,” and gives the Lord’s answer, which Exodus does not: “They have well said all that they have spoken” (Deut. 5:27–28). Moses describes what he became that day in one sentence: “I stood between the LORD and you” (Deut. 5:5).

The book of Exodus then shows what a man between does. When Israel makes the calf, Moses pleads for them (32:11–13), goes up a second time “peradventure I shall make an atonement for your sin” (32:30), and offers himself: “if thou wilt forgive their sin—; and if not, blot me, I pray thee, out of thy book which thou hast written” (32:32). The Lord refuses the offer and keeps the man: “Whosoever hath sinned against me, him will I blot out of my book” (32:33). Deuteronomy adds that at Horeb, with the same words He used to approve the request, the Lord promised a second man between, “a Prophet from among their brethren, like unto thee,” with His words in his mouth (Deut. 18:17–18), and the book closes with that promise open: “there arose not a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses” (Deut. 34:10). Peter, the risen Lord at Bountiful, and the Joseph Smith Translation of Galatians say who the second man is: Jesus Christ, “the mediator of the new covenant,” who in the Doctrine and Covenants pleads before the Father not His name but “the blood of thy Son which was shed” (D&C 45:4).

This page follows that one request through both books to its answer: Israel asked for a man between, was given one, and was promised the One. The commentaries and the Church’s footnotes carry nearly every step, and I say which. One reading is mine, named once below: that the refused offer of Exodus 32 and the Advocate’s plea of D&C 45 are the same scene with a different outcome.

## “I stood between the LORD and you”

Deuteronomy’s parenthesis is the book’s own description of a mediator. The Hebrew of 5:5 is *ʿōmēd bên YHWH ûbênêkem*, “standing between the LORD and between you,” and Keil reads it as “showing the mediatorial position which he occupied between the Lord and the people … which alone in fact rendered the conclusion of the covenant possible at all, on account of the alarm of the people.”[@keil-deuteronomy] The Cambridge commentary on the request that follows: “The people, fearing the fatal effect of hearing God’s voice directly, request Moses to act as mediator.”[@cambridge-deuteronomy] The Bible Dictionary gives the office its Old Testament shape: a priest was “a mediator between his people and God … By virtue of his office he was able to draw nigh to God, while they, because of their sins and infirmities, must needs stand afar off.”[@bd-priests] That is the picture of Exodus 20:21, “the people stood afar off, and Moses drew near.”

Two things are true of the request at once. The Lord approved it, “they have well said all that they have spoken” (Deut. 5:28), and Keil explains the approval: “God approved of what the people said, because it sprang from a consciousness of the unworthiness of any sinner to come into the presence of the holy God.”[@keil-deuteronomy] The Doctrine and Covenants, whose footnote at “hardened” in 84:24 leads to Exodus 20:19, reads the same drawing back as a hardening: the people “could not endure his presence” (D&C 84:24), and Scripture Helps says the Lord “had intended for them to experience something more significant.”[@scripture-helps-ex-19] The Lord’s next words at Horeb hold both: “O that there were such an heart in them, that they would fear me, and keep all my commandments always” (Deut. 5:29). The theme page [What Was Offered at Sinai](/exodus/themes/offered-at-sinai/) follows the loss, and this page follows the man who was granted.

The phrase “stood between” occurs in one other verse of the five books of Moses, of a priest at the moment of a plague: Aaron “stood between the dead and the living; and the plague was stayed” (Num. 16:48), the same two Hebrew words.[@oshb-wlc] The Numbers essay [Between the Dead and the Living](/numbers/themes/between-the-dead-and-the-living/) follows Aaron’s side of the office.

## “They have well said”

The Lord’s approval at Horeb is not the end of His sentence. Deuteronomy 18 gives the rest, and ties it to that day by name: “According to all that thou desiredst of the LORD thy God in Horeb in the day of the assembly … And the LORD said unto me, They have well spoken that which they have spoken. I will raise them up a Prophet from among their brethren, like unto thee, and will put my words in his mouth” (Deut. 18:16–18). “Well said” and “well spoken” are one Hebrew verb, *hêṭîbû*, in both chapters.[@oshb-wlc] Ellicott draws the conclusion: “It will appear by comparison of the two passages that the promise of the prophet like unto Moses was given at this very time.”[@ellicott-deuteronomy] The Church’s footnote at 18:16 “hear” leads back to Exodus 20:19 and Deuteronomy 5:25, and then to the Topical Guide heading “Hardheartedness,” so the edition carries both readings of the request into the promise at once.[@lds-scriptures]

The occasion explains what “like unto me” means. Keil: “at any time when the people stood in need of a mediator with God like Moses, God would invariably send a prophet,” and “the promised prophet, therefore, was to resemble Moses” in standing between.[@keil-deuteronomy] The Cambridge commentary: a prophet raised up “to be such a mediator of His word, as in Horeb they had prayed Moses to be,” adding that the phrase “does not imply equality in rank with Moses.”[@cambridge-deuteronomy] Matthew Bowen says the same from the Latter-day Saint side: the Israelites’ “rejection of the immediate presence of God and a request for human intermediaries served as the basis for the giving of this prophecy.”[@bowen-law-codes] The promise answers the request for a mediator.

The promise has been read of one prophet and of a succession of prophets. David Rolph Seely thinks it “more likely” that the singular has “a collective sense, referring to the future succession of prophets,” and concludes that for Latter-day Saints the prophecy “has been fulfilled in the past by Christ and others.”[@seely-prophet-like-moses] Keil agrees that it “treats of the sending of prophets generally” while insisting “that Christ, in whom the prophetic character culminated and was completed, is to be included.”[@keil-deuteronomy] The Church’s edition footnotes 18:15 to Acts 3:22, Acts 7:37, 1 Nephi 22:21, 3 Nephi 20:23 and Joseph Smith—History 1:40, and the Student Manual says that “in each instance these scriptures make it clear that the prophet like unto Moses was the Savior, Jesus Christ.”[@ot-manual-deuteronomy-17-33] Scripture Helps: “After His Resurrection, Jesus Christ declared that He was the fulfillment of Moses’s prophecy,” quoting 3 Nephi 20:23, “Behold, I am he of whom Moses spake.”[@scripture-helps-deut]

## “Blot me, I pray thee, out of thy book”

Between the request and the promise’s fulfilment, Exodus shows the first man between at work. The Lord says, “let me alone, that my wrath may wax hot against them … and I will make of thee a great nation” (32:10), and Moses does not let Him alone: “Moses besought the LORD his God” (32:11), pleading the Exodus and the oath to Abraham, Isaac and Israel (32:12–13), “And the LORD repented of the evil” (32:14). The Church’s chapter heading calls this “Moses serves as a mediator between God and rebellious Israel,” and Scripture Central’s *Old Testament Minute*: “Moses became the mediator between God and the people.”[@lds-scriptures][@ot-minute-ex-24]

The next day he goes further. “Peradventure I shall make an atonement for your sin” (32:30): the verb is *kāpar*, which Exodus otherwise uses only of the altar and the tabernacle’s rites (29:33–37; 30:10–16), and here of what a man might do for a people.[@oshb-wlc] Then the prayer: “Yet now, if thou wilt forgive their sin—; and if not, blot me, I pray thee, out of thy book which thou hast written” (32:32). “Forgive” is *nāśāʾ*, the ordinary verb for lifting and carrying, which stands thirty-three times in Exodus and in five verses has sin or iniquity as its direct object; one of the five is the verse about the high priest’s plate, “that Aaron may bear the iniquity of the holy things” (28:38), where the Church’s footnote reads “OR atone for.”[@oshb-wlc] Keil on that verse: “to bear iniquity (sin) and take it away; in other words, to exterminate it by taking it upon one’s self. The high priest was exalted into an atoning mediator of the whole nation.”[@keil-exodus] In 32:32 Moses asks the Lord to bear the people’s sin, with the verb the law used of the priest at Aaron’s plate, and asks to be blotted out if the Lord will not.

The Lord refuses. “Whosoever hath sinned against me, him will I blot out of my book” (32:33). The Cambridge commentary: “Jehovah replies that He will blot out of His book not the righteous, but those only who have sinned against Him.”[@cambridge-exodus] Ellicott: “God refuses the offer … As his sacrifice could not have redeemed them (Psalm 49:7), God did not accept it in the literal sense; but the offer may have availed much towards the pardon of the people.”[@ellicott-exodus] Keil adds what the intercession did and did not win: Moses “averted from the nation the abrogation of the covenant; but the covenant relation which had existed before was not restored in its integrity.”[@keil-exodus] The Church’s footnote at “blot” leads to Romans 9:3, where Paul “could wish that myself were accursed from Christ for my brethren,” and Ellicott makes the same comparison.[@lds-scriptures] What the Church’s sources say is that the offer shows what Moses was. The Student Manual: “Moses, like Christ whom he emulated, loved his people in spite of their hardheartedness and wickedness. He interceded in their behalf and saved them, but only on the condition of their repentance.”[@ot-manual-ex-21-24] Scripture Helps: his pleading “can remind us of Jesus Christ, who is our advocate with the Father.”[@scripture-helps-ex-19] The *Old Testament Minute*: “Moses’s ‘atoning’ for his people is a symbol of Christ’s own Atonement for all.”[@ot-minute-ex-24]

Deuteronomy remembers these forty days as prayer: “I fell down before the LORD … because of all your sins” (Deut. 9:18); “I prayed for Aaron also the same time” (9:20); “the LORD hearkened unto me at that time also, and the LORD would not destroy thee” (10:10). At Kadesh the same man prays the same prayer and hears, “I have pardoned according to thy word” (Num. 14:20). The man Israel asked for did the thing they asked, again and again, and at the one point where he offered more than pleading, the Lord said no.

## “There arose not a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses”

Deuteronomy ends by measuring every prophet against him: “there arose not a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face” (34:10), and the Church’s footnote on “prophet” sends the reader back to 18:15.[@lds-scriptures] Numbers had already set him apart: “My servant Moses is not so, who is faithful in all mine house. With him will I speak mouth to mouth” (Num. 12:7–8). Scripture Central’s *Old Testament Minute*, quoting Richard Elliott Friedman, says 34:10 and 18:15 “need not be a contradiction,” since Moses “became the prophetic archetype.”[@ot-minute-deut-rappleye] Hebrews takes Numbers 12:7 and turns it: “Moses verily was faithful in all his house, as a servant … But Christ as a son over his own house” (Heb. 3:5–6), which the Cambridge commentary paraphrases, “as a Son over the house is above a servant.”[@cambridge-hebrews] The book that promised a prophet like Moses closes by saying none has come, and the New Testament answers that the One who came is more than like him.

## “One mediator”

The New Testament gives Him the name of the office Israel asked for. “There is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus” (1 Tim. 2:5). Hebrews calls Him “the mediator of a better covenant” (8:6), “the mediator of the new testament” (9:15), “Jesus the mediator of the new covenant” (12:24), and the New Testament Student Manual explains the Greek term as “a third party who stands between two others to resolve their differences and to bring them together.”[@nt-manual-hebrews-7-13] Joseph Smith’s revision of Galatians names both men: Moses “was ordained by the hand of angels to be a mediator of this first covenant,” but “there is one mediator of the new covenant, who is Christ … Now Christ is the mediator of life” (JST Gal. 3:19–20), a passage the New Testament Student Manual quotes for the same point.[@jst-gal-3][@nt-student-manual-43] Robert Millet calls Moses’s prayer at Exodus 32:32 “a touching act of mediation.”[@millet-mediator-new-covenant]

The Church’s footnotes carry the two halves of the Horeb promise to Him. At Deuteronomy 18:18, “I will put my words in his mouth,” the footnote leads to John 12:49–50, where Jesus says, “whatsoever I speak therefore, even as the Father said unto me, so I speak”; a man of their brethren with God’s words in his mouth is how the Son describes His own speaking.[@lds-scriptures] At Deuteronomy 5:5, “ye were afraid,” the footnote leads to Matthew 17:6, where on another mountain a voice from a cloud says, “This is my beloved Son … hear ye him. And when the disciples heard it, they fell on their face, and were sore afraid” (Matt. 17:5–6).[@lds-scriptures] The edition sets the fear at Horeb beside the fear on the mount, and President Russell M. Nelson, without citing Deuteronomy, draws the lesson from the mount: “Our Father knows that when we are surrounded by uncertainty and fear, what will help us the very most is to hear His Son.”[@nelson-hear-him] Ellicott, on Deuteronomy 5:28: “The Angel of the covenant and the Prophet like unto Moses are one. He who gave the Law on Sinai died under it on Calvary.”[@ellicott-deuteronomy]

Here is the reading that is mine. In Exodus 32 the man between pleads the covenant, offers his own name against the people’s sin, and is told that each sinner will answer for himself. In the Doctrine and Covenants the second man between pleads too, and the Lord records the plea: “Listen to him who is the advocate with the Father, who is pleading your cause before him—Saying: Father, behold the sufferings and death of him who did no sin, in whom thou wast well pleased; behold the blood of thy Son which was shed … Wherefore, Father, spare these my brethren that believe on my name” (D&C 45:3–5). The scene is the same, a mediator before God asking that a sinful people be spared, and the difference is what each has to offer. Moses offered his name and was refused, because, as Ellicott says, his sacrifice “could not have redeemed them.” The Son offers His blood, already shed, for “these my brethren that believe on my name,” and “he ever liveth to make intercession for them” (Heb. 7:25). No source sets the two passages side by side; the Student Manual’s “like Christ whom he emulated” and the *Old Testament Minute*’s “symbol” are the nearest, and they do not say where the likeness ends.

## “We will hear it, and do it”

The request that made the office was also a promise: “speak thou unto us … and we will hear it, and do it” (Deut. 5:27). The Church’s footnote at that verse is Doctrine and Covenants 1:38, “whether by mine own voice or by the voice of my servants, it is the same,” so the edition sets Israel’s promise beside the terms on which the Lord still speaks through men.[@lds-scriptures] The Prophet promised at Horeb asked the same hearing: “him shall ye hear in all things whatsoever he shall say unto you” (3 Ne. 20:23).

So the request Israel made in fear has been answered. There is a Man between, of their brethren, with the Father’s words in His mouth, who pleads for His people with His blood already shed, and the answer owed Him is the one Israel gave at Horeb before the calf: to hear it, and do it.
