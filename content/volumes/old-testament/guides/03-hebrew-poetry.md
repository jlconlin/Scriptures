Much of the Old Testament is poetry, and so are many of the prophets' messages.[@barney-ot-poetry] Hebrew poetry “uses patterns of repetition” rather than meter or rhyme,[@barney-ot-poetry] “a symmetry of meaning, not syllables.”[@sc-knowhy-831] Because such a pattern rests on meaning, it “survives translation into the prose of any language with remarkably little loss,”[@ot-manual-g] so a reader of the King James text can see most of it. This guide names the forms with examples: how a verse is built, the acrostic, the kinds of psalm, the forms the prophets speak in, and what to keep in mind reading the King James text.

<h2 id="parallelism">How a verse is built: parallelism</h2>

Parallelism is “the most basic form of poetry in the Old Testament.”[@parry-synonymous] The first line states an idea and the second “restates, reinforces, or reconfigures” it; “in a parallelism, both lines are equally important.”[@parry-synonymous] The kinds below are “aids to help us recognize” patterns of repetition, not rigid rules; a second line may only partly parallel the first, and a parallelism may run to three or four lines.[@barney-ot-poetry]

| Kind | What the second line does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Synonymous | Says the first again in other words.[@parry-synonymous] | “Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth” ([[Isa. 1:2]]) |
| Antithetic | Contrasts with the first.[@barney-ot-poetry] | “the way of the righteous” against “the way of the ungodly” ([[Ps. 1:6]]) |
| Synthetic | Builds up the thought, each line adding to the first.[@ot-manual-g] | “like a tree planted by the rivers of water” ([[Ps. 1:3]]) |
| Emblematic | Sets a literal statement beside a simile or metaphor.[@barney-ot-poetry] | “As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God” ([[Ps. 42:1]]) |
| Stairlike | Repeats a phrase to introduce new thoughts.[@barney-ot-poetry] | “Give unto the LORD, O ye mighty, give unto the LORD glory and strength” ([[Ps. 29:1]]) |

A climactic form repeats words from one phrase to the next and in some cases builds to a crowning point; [[Joel 1:3–4]] is an example.[@parry-climactic]

**Chiasmus** is inverted parallelism: a series of words or ideas, then a second presentation “in reverse order.” The Old Testament has hundreds of chiasms, and Isaiah alone more than a hundred.[@parry-chiasmus] [[Isa. 5:20]] has three: “Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil” runs evil, good, good, evil, and the same reversal follows with darkness and light, bitter and sweet.[@parry-chiasmus]

Missing the pattern can lead to misreading a verse. In [[Gen. 4:23]], “Adah and Zillah” are the “wives of Lamech”; Lamech did not kill two men, for “the ‘man’ and the ‘young man’ are the same.”[@barney-ot-poetry] The note at [[Ps. 1:1]] and the second key of [How to Read Isaiah](/isaiah/guides/how-to-read-isaiah/) say more.

<h2 id="acrostics">Acrostics</h2>

An acrostic is a device “by which the first letters of a series of lines form words or the alphabet.” It is invisible in translation.[@barney-ot-poetry] [[Psalm 119]] has 22 stanzas of eight verses, one for each Hebrew letter: verses 1–8 begin with aleph, verses 9–16 with beth, and so on.[@scripture-helps-ps-102] The Church's edition prints the letter over each stanza.[@barney-ot-poetry] Other acrostics: Psalms 9, 34 and 37, Lamentations 1–4 and Proverbs 31:10–31;[@barney-ot-poetry] Psalms 25, 111, 112 and 145 are also acrostic in the Hebrew.[@fun-hebrew-poetry] Lamentations 1, 2 and 4 have 22 verses, one for each letter; chapter 3 has 66, three to a letter; chapter 5 is not alphabetical.[@bd-lamentations]

The form “may have been a valuable aid for memorizing,”[@scripture-helps-ps-102] and going through the whole alphabet “suggests completeness.”[@fun-hebrew-poetry] On Psalms 9 and 10 sharing one alphabet, see the note at [[Ps. 9:1]].

<h2 id="psalms">The kinds of psalm, and the shape of a lament</h2>

The Hebrew name of the book is Tehillim, “Praises.”[@bd-psalms] “No book of the Old Testament is more Christian in its inner sense … than the Psalms”: 116 of the New Testament's 283 direct citations from the Old are from them.[@bd-psalms] A psalm is “an inspired poem or hymn.”[@gs-psalm] The Hebrews divided the 150 psalms into five books, each ending with a doxology (a formal declaration of God's power and glory).[@ot-manual-psalms] The psalms can be grouped in seven kinds:[@hopkin-psalms-sung]

| Kind | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Lament | Pleas in personal or national crisis | 22, 44, 74, 137 |
| Praise | Calls Israel to gather and praise the LORD | 8, 19, 29, 100 |
| Thanksgiving | Gratitude after deliverance | 18, 30, 34, 116 |
| Royal | Celebrates events in the lives of royalty | 2, 45, 72, 110 |
| Songs of Zion | Celebrate the temple's place on Mount Zion | 46, 48, 84, 122 |
| Liturgies | Call and response between worshippers and priests | 15, 24, 50 |
| Wisdom and Torah | Discuss religious truths and advice on living | 37, 49, 73, 127 |

Of the royal psalms, 2, 72 and 110 are “the most striking,” picturing an ideal king.[@ot-manual-psalms]

Laments are the largest group: about a third of the psalms are or contain laments, individual (“I/me”) or communal (“we/us”). They generally have five elements, though the order can be rearranged: invocation, complaint, confession of trust, petition and vow of praise. [[Psalm 22]] has them: invocation, “My God, my God”; complaint, “why hast thou forsaken me?”; confession of trust, “He trusted on the Lord that he would deliver him” ([[Ps. 22:8]]); petition, “be not thou far from me, O Lord”; vow of praise, “in the midst of the congregation will I praise thee.”[@sc-knowhy-438] Many psalms are “people speaking to God” in prayerlike language; [[Psalm 51]] is David's desire to repent.[@ot-institute-manual-33] See the notes at [[Ps. 22:1]] and [[Ps. 6:1]] (the penitential psalms), and the Psalms theme pages.

<h2 id="prophets">How the prophets speak</h2>

**“Thus saith the LORD.”** A Hebrew prophet's message “was usually prefaced with the words ‘Thus saith Jehovah’”; as a rule a prophet “was a forthteller rather than a foreteller.”[@bd-prophet] The phrase is the messenger formula; “the word of the LORD came unto me, saying” is the revelation formula, which marks the start of a revelation and shows its origin and authority.[@parry-symbolic-action] Compare “Thus saith the LORD” ([[Jer. 2:5]]) and “the word of the LORD came unto me, saying” ([[Jer. 1:4]]). Other forms include “Hearken to the word of the LORD” and “As the LORD liveth.”[@parry-prophetic-forms]

**The burden.** “The word burden can refer to a ‘prophetic oracle’ or ‘prophetic pronouncement.’” “The burden of Babylon” ([[Isa. 13:1]]) is the first of a series of judgments against nations;[@scripture-helps-isa-13-14] chapters 13–23 are such oracles.[@sc-knowhy-870] The Hebrew word is *massa*.[@oshb-wlc] See the note at [[Isa. 13:1]].

**The woe.** A woe is a prophecy or statement “of impending anguish, misery, and judgment,” characteristically with an accusation, an addressee, an intent and a promise of judgment.[@parry-prophetic-forms] “Woe unto them that join house to house” ([[Isa. 5:8]]) begins with the Hebrew *hoy*; the King James renders the same word “Ah” ([[Isa. 1:4]]), “O” ([[Isa. 10:5]]) and “Ho” ([[Isa. 55:1]]). “Woe is me” ([[Isa. 6:5]]) is another word, *oy*.[@oshb-wlc]

**The lawsuit.** “Controversy” in [[Hosea 4:1]] is *rib*, which “never describes an appeal or call to repentance, but always a hostile confrontation, an accusation,” with “more formal application to disputation in a court of law.” In the verse the Lord brings legal proceedings “for breach of covenant.”[@jackson-hosea-marriage] [[Isa. 1:2]] calls heaven and earth to witness, “the ancient Israelite practice” that Nephi follows in [[Hel. 8:24]]; such an indictment has been widely identified as a “prophet lawsuit.”[@sc-knowhy-179] In Micah, “the LORD hath a controversy with his people” (Mic. 6:2).[@lds-scriptures] [[Micah 6]] is described as an imagined conversation between the Lord and Judah.[@ot-institute-manual-47] See the note at [[Hosea 4:1]].

**The call.** Isaiah answers, “Here am I; send me” ([[Isa. 6:8]]). Isaiah 6, Lehi's call and Revelation 1–5 have been found to share nine elements of a call narrative, from a historical introduction to God's reassurance; not every account has every one.[@ricks-heavenly-visions] Ezekiel “saw a vision and beheld God's glory” and was called to “serve as a watchman for the house of Israel” (see [[Ezek. 1:1]] and [[Ezek. 3:17]]).[@scripture-helps-ezek] Jeremiah's call predated his birth ([[Jer. 1:5]]).[@scripture-helps-jer-1-3] The Church's edition cross-references the calls: Isa. 6:3 with Ezek. 1:28, Isa. 6:7 with Jer. 1:9, Ezek. 2:8 with Rev. 10:10, and Ezek. 1:1 with 1 Ne. 1:8.[@lds-scriptures] See the note at [[Isa. 6:1]].

**The sign act.** A prophet may act out a message rather than say it, introduced by the messenger or revelation formula.[@parry-symbolic-action] “Go and loose the sackcloth from off thy loins, and put off thy shoe from thy foot” ([[Isa. 20:2]]). Isaiah's children's names were signs ([[Isa. 8:18]]): Maher-shalal-hash-baz, “to speed to the spoil, he hasteneth the prey.”[@scripture-helps-isa-1-12] Others are Jeremiah's yoke and Ezekiel's eating a scroll;[@parry-symbolic-action] see also [How to Read Jeremiah](/jeremiah/guides/how-to-read-jeremiah/).

**The dirge.** Lamentations is a set of dirges “over the fall of Jerusalem and the nation”;[@bd-lamentations] its five poems are laments with no response from the Lord.[@scripture-helps-jer-31-33] Their rhythm has been named *qinah*, “lament,” and is “often used for taunts or laments (as in the book of Lamentations)”;[@ot-manual-g] the word, which Lamentations itself does not use,[@oshb-wlc] is the “lamentation” of [[Ezek. 19:1]].[@oshb-wlc] Jeremiah's own poetic laments include [[Jer. 20:7]].[@scripture-helps-jer-1-3]

<h2 id="kjv">For a reader of the King James text</h2>

Hebrew poetry’s alliteration, assonance and word play “do not normally survive translation,” but the King James translators “infused the English text with the spirit of the Hebrew poetry where they were able.”[@barney-ot-poetry] Words in italics were “necessary in English” but “not present in the Hebrew.”[@bd-italics] Isaiah also uses hyperbole, “deliberate exaggeration” for effect: “the mountains and the hills shall break forth before you into singing, and all the trees of the field shall clap their hands” ([[Isa. 55:12]]).[@parry-isaiah-devices] Isaiah's “extensive use of symbolism” and his “literary style” make him hard to understand;[@bd-isaiah] [How to Read Isaiah](/isaiah/guides/how-to-read-isaiah/) helps.
