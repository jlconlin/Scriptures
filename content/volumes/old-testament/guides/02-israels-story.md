The Old Testament “is a record of God's dealings with His covenant children from the Creation until about four hundred years before the birth of Jesus Christ.”[@scripture-helps-welcome] Its books “were not always placed in chronological order but were instead grouped according to their subject material,” and the prophets' books “are not in historical order.”[@scripture-helps-welcome] This guide sets most of the books on one timeline, then tells the story once, in order, from Abraham through the Persian period, and names the empires that shaped it.

**Books hard to date.** Job is given no period.[@bd-job-book] [Joel](/joel/) may be “as early as the reign of Joash, before 850 B.C., or even so late as after the Return.”[@bd-joel] Jonah the man lived under Jeroboam II, but the book “is due to some later writer.”[@bd-jonah] Obadiah is “possibly” 848–844, “more probably” 586.[@bd-obadiah] Habakkuk is uncertain.[@bd-habakkuk] Chronicles runs from the Creation to Cyrus's proclamation, but “the date of composition cannot be fixed with certainty.”[@bd-chronicles]

<h2 id="dates">How sure the dates are</h2>

For the earliest parts of Old Testament history “we rely entirely on the scripture itself,” and the Hebrew Bible, the Septuagint and the Samaritan Pentateuch differ; “From the time of David onwards we get much assistance from secular history.” The dates at the top of many printed Bibles are Archbishop Ussher's, and “Some of them have been shown to be incorrect.”[@bible-chron-intro] The Church's Bible Chronology table gives no B.C. date before Saul, calls the arrangement and dates of the judges “very uncertain,” and from Saul on offers “approximate dates.”[@bible-chron-ot]

The Church's own pages print different figures for the same event. Samaria falls in 721 in the table and 722 in its revised column;[@bible-chron-ot] Jerusalem falls in 587 in the table and 586 in the Captivities entry;[@bible-chron-ot][@bd-captivities] Cyrus's decree is 537 in the table, 536 in the Captivities entry, and “about a year” after 539 in *Scripture Helps*.[@bible-chron-ot][@bd-captivities][@scripture-helps-ezra] One set of dates for the kings is “adapted from” a published chronology, and “Other chronologies may vary slightly.”[@ot-manual-kings-a] For the main events this guide gives one date and puts the table's figure in parentheses where it differs.

<h2 id="patriarchs">The patriarchs, and Egypt</h2>

Abraham, son of Terah, was born in Ur of the Chaldees, migrated to Haran, and then went to Canaan.[@bd-abraham] The Lord promised, “I will make of thee a great nation” ([[Gen. 12:2]]), and told him that his seed would be “a stranger in a land that is not theirs” for “four hundred years” ([[Gen. 15:13]]). To Jacob: “fear not to go down into Egypt; for I will there make of thee a great nation” ([[Gen. 46:3]]). Then “there arose up a new king over Egypt, which knew not Joseph” ([[Ex. 1:8]]), and the sojourning in Egypt “was four hundred and thirty years” ([[Ex. 12:40]]). The Book of Exodus gives the nation's early history in three stages: enslaved, redeemed, set apart.[@bd-exodus]

The Bible Chronology table lists Abram through Joshua with no date and places the Exodus “when Moses was 80 years old.”[@bible-chron-ot] Pithom and Pa-Ramses were built under Ramses II, with Israelite slaves;[@bd-egypt] Moses' return “probably took place soon after the death of Ramses Ⅱ. In that case the Pharaoh of the Exodus was Mer-ne-ptah.”[@bd-egypt] Dating the patriarchs is “a difficult task”; one Latter-day Saint study, following an earlier one, places Abraham's birth “in about 1943 BC,” in Egypt's Middle Kingdom (the Twelfth Dynasty, about 1950–1750), and Joseph “when the Hyksos began to take control of all of Egypt, in about 1650 BC,” while saying that Joseph cannot be dated precisely.[@muhlestein-israel-egypt] The Exodus was once dated to the fifteenth century from the 480 years of 1 Kings 6:1, but new evidence “points to a later date: the thirteenth century, perhaps early in the reign of Ramses II (1290–1224).”[@ot-manual-ex-11-19] The conquest “should have happened before 1200 BC,” since Israel is attested in Canaan under Merneptah, and “a nice, round date for the Exodus would be 1250 BC,” though that date is “speculative.”[@muhlestein-israel-egypt]

Read it in [Genesis](/genesis/), [Exodus](/exodus/), [Abraham](/abraham/) and [Moses](/moses/).

For Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph, see [The Patriarchs](/old-testament/guides/the-patriarchs/); for Israel in Egypt, the Exodus and the wilderness, see [Egypt and the Wilderness](/old-testament/guides/egypt-and-the-wilderness/).

<h2 id="judges">The conquest and the judges</h2>

Joshua, Moses's successor and one of the twelve spies, led the conquest of Palestine and died at 110.[@bd-joshua] Judges and Ruth hold “all the Jewish history that has been preserved to us of the times between the death of Joshua and the birth of Samuel.”[@bd-judges] The conquest is dated “between 1250 and 1200 B.C.”[@ot-manual-judges-1-12]

“The Lord raised up judges, which delivered them” ([[Judg. 2:16]]).[@lds-scriptures] The book names twelve judges, among them Deborah, Gideon and Samson;[@scripture-helps-judges] they ruled “for over three hundred years.”[@gs-israel] In this period the Israelites “formed a confederation of tribes rather than” a nation; the book was compiled long after the events, and its chronology is difficult because its figures are round numbers.[@bd-judges] It closes: “In those days there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes” ([[Judg. 21:25]]).[@lds-scriptures] Ruth is set “in the days of the Judges,” and Ruth is David's ancestor.[@bd-ruth]

<h2 id="united-kingdom">One kingdom: Saul, David, Solomon</h2>

Samuel, born in answer to Hannah's prayers, became “the great prophet and judge of Israel.”[@bd-samuel] The elders asked him, “make us a king to judge us like all the nations” ([[1 Sam. 8:5]]).[@lds-scriptures] Saul, son of Kish, was the first king, and was “rejected from being king.”[@bd-saul] David's life falls into four periods: tending sheep, serving at court, living as a fugitive, and reigning as king, first at Hebron and then over all Israel.[@bd-david] The Lord promised him, “thy throne shall be established for ever” ([[2 Sam. 7:16]]).[@lds-scriptures] Solomon, son of David and Bathsheba, built and dedicated the temple, and had a “fondness for foreign wives and foreign gods.”[@bd-solomon] The Lord said, “I will surely rend the kingdom from thee” ([[1 Kgs. 11:11]]), yet “in thy days I will not do it for David thy father's sake” ([[1 Kgs. 11:12]]).[@lds-scriptures]

By the text's reckoning each king reigned forty years: Saul ([[Acts 13:21]]), David ([[2 Sam. 5:4]]) and Solomon ([[1 Kgs. 11:42]]).[@lds-scriptures] 1 Kings begins with David's death “around 968 BC”; the Church's table prints Saul 1095, David 1055 at Hebron and 1047 at Jerusalem, and Solomon 1015.[@scripture-helps-1-kings-24][@bible-chron-ot] Seventy-three psalms are ascribed to David.[@bd-psalms] Proverbs 25–29 are Solomon's proverbs that “the men of Hezekiah … copied out.”[@bd-proverbs] The author of Ecclesiastes describes himself as “son of David, king in Jerusalem.”[@bd-ecclesiastes] Whether Solomon wrote the Song of Solomon “is doubtful.”[@bd-song-of-solomon] Read the [Psalms](/psalms/).

<h2 id="two-kingdoms">Two kingdoms: Israel and Judah</h2>

Under Rehoboam the division came, “mainly the result of an old” rivalry between Ephraim and Judah.[@bd-rehoboam][@gs-judah] The people answered, “What portion have we in David? … to your tents, O Israel” ([[1 Kgs. 12:16]]).[@lds-scriptures] Ahijah had prophesied the division; the immediate cause was revolt against the taxes of Solomon and Rehoboam, “at approximately 925 B.C.”[@bd-israel-kingdom] It is also dated 931 (Jeroboam I 931–909; Rehoboam 931–913); the table prints 975.[@ot-manual-kings-a][@bible-chron-ot]

**The north.** Jeroboam, of Ephraim, was the first king; he set up calves at Dan and Bethel[@bd-jeroboam] and “introduced idolatry.”[@scripture-helps-1-kings] Samaria, on a hill, was the capital.[@gs-samaria] The kingdom adopted the worship of Baal “notwithstanding the ministries of such great prophets as Elijah and Amos,” and lasted “over 200 years and a series of 19 kings.”[@bd-israel-kingdom] It “was far wealthier and more influential than the Kingdom of Judah” until Assyria took it.[@crapo-lehi-joseph]

**The south.** The house of David held the throne “until the Exile,” and Judah outlasted Israel by 135 years.[@bd-judah-kingdom]

**The prophets.** Elijah was a prophet “during the reign of Ahab”;[@scripture-helps-1-kings] the table places Elisha under Jehoram.[@bible-chron-ot] Jonah lived under Jeroboam II, who died about 750;[@bd-jonah][@bd-amos] Amos and Hosea prophesied in his reign[@bd-jeroboam], Hosea being “the only prophet of the northern kingdom who has left written prophecies.”[@bd-hosea] Micah prophesied under Hezekiah[@bd-micah], and Isaiah in Jerusalem “740–701 B.C.”[@bd-isaiah] See [Hosea](/hosea/) and, for the kings and nations of this period, [Two Kingdoms and Assyria](/old-testament/guides/two-kingdoms-and-assyria/).

<h2 id="assyria">Assyria and the fall of Samaria</h2>

Assyria was a major power “from the mid-12th century B.C. until the close of the 7th century B.C.”;[@gs-assyria] the Neo-Assyrian Empire ran “from about 911 BC to the fall of Nineveh in 612 BC.”[@sc-knowhy-711] In 740 Tiglath-pileser III carried away the trans-Jordan tribes and Galilee.[@bd-captivities] In 738 he took tribute from Damascus and Samaria. In 734 they revolted; Damascus was destroyed, Gilead and Naphtali were depopulated, and Judah accepted Assyrian overlordship.[@bd-assyria] Shalmaneser V began the siege of Samaria and Sargon II (722–705) concluded it;[@bd-assyria] the siege lasted three years.[@gs-samaria] “The king of Assyria took Samaria, and carried Israel away into Assyria” ([[2 Kgs. 17:6]]).[@lds-scriptures] The date is “thought to be 721 B.C., but it may have been 722.”[@ot-manual-12]

The Assyrians “brought men from Babylon … and placed them in the cities of Samaria” ([[2 Kgs. 17:24]]).[@lds-scriptures] The later history of the captives “cannot be followed with certainty”;[@bd-captivities] “Since their captivity they have never yet returned,” but the prophecies speak of their return.[@bd-israel-kingdom]

Judah paid Assyria tribute from Ahaz's treaty through Hezekiah “until around 705 BC.”[@scripture-helps-2-kings] Sennacherib (705–681) came to Palestine in 701;[@bd-assyria] Hezekiah, a reformer, had Isaiah's help.[@bd-hezekiah] Sennacherib king of Assyria did “come up against all the fenced cities of Judah” ([[2 Kgs. 18:13]]).[@lds-scriptures] [Two Kingdoms and Assyria](/old-testament/guides/two-kingdoms-and-assyria/#rulers) tells the crisis in detail; see also [[Isa. 36–37]].

<h2 id="babylon">Josiah, Babylon, and the fall of Jerusalem</h2>

Josiah (640–609; the Bible Dictionary prints 641–610) made a thorough reformation and was killed by Necho at Megiddo.[@schade-kingdom-judah][@bd-josiah] “Hilkiah the high priest said … I have found the book of the law” ([[2 Kgs. 22:8]]).[@lds-scriptures] Jeremiah was called in Josiah's thirteenth year and prophesied 626–586; Zephaniah prophesied in Josiah's reign; Habakkuk's date is uncertain, “possibly in the reign of Josiah or of Jehoiakim.”[@bd-jeremiah][@bd-zephaniah][@bd-habakkuk] Nahum's prophecy is dated between the fall of Thebes (about 660) and Nineveh's (there dated 606).[@bd-nahum]

Nineveh fell in 612 (the table: 606; the Student Manual: 609) to the Medes and Babylonians.[@scripture-helps-geography][@bible-chron-ot][@ot-manual-kings-g][@bd-assyria] Judah was under Egypt from 609 to 605, until Nebuchadnezzar beat Necho at Carchemish.[@schade-kingdom-judah][@bd-assyria] Nebuchadnezzar II reigned 605–562 (the Bible Dictionary: 604–561).[@scripture-helps-2-kings][@bd-nebuchadnezzar] Daniel and his companions were taken to Babylon “around the beginning of the sixth century BC”; Daniel dates it “In the third year of the reign of Jehoiakim” ([[Dan. 1:1]]).[@scripture-helps-dan]

Jehoiachin reigned three months and was deposed in 597 (the table: 598); the Babylonian Chronicle dates Zedekiah's appointment to March 16, 597.[@schade-kingdom-judah][@bible-chron-ot][@sc-knowhy-860] “He carried away all Jerusalem … even ten thousand captives” ([[2 Kgs. 24:14]]).[@lds-scriptures] Ezekiel was among them.[@bd-ezekiel]

Lehi's ministry is dated “in the commencement of the first year of the reign of Zedekiah” ([[1 Ne. 1:4]]).[@lds-scriptures] The Introduction to the Book of Mormon says 600 B.C.;[@lds-scriptures] proposed dates for Lehi's departure range from 605 to 588, many of them 597–596[@sc-knowhy-475], and one is November 605.[@chadwick-lehi-departure]

Zedekiah reigned 597–586; the final siege began in 587.[@schade-kingdom-judah] “In the fifth month … came Nebuzar-adan … And he burnt the house of the Lord” ([[2 Kgs. 25:8–9]]).[@lds-scriptures] The table has 587 for the capture.[@bible-chron-ot] Jeremiah counts 4,600 deported, in 597, 587 and 582.[@draper-exile] Lamentations was written “some years after the fall of the city,” and Obadiah “more probably” after 586.[@bd-lamentations][@bd-obadiah] See [Babylon and the Exile](/old-testament/guides/babylon-and-the-exile/), [Jeremiah's World](/jeremiah/guides/jeremiahs-world/), [Lamentations](/lamentations/), [Ezekiel](/ezekiel/) and [Daniel](/daniel/).

<h2 id="exile">The exile</h2>

“By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept” ([[Ps. 137:1]]). Ezekiel, a priest, settled at Tel Abib on the Chebar and prophesied 592–570 (also dated about 593–573).[@bd-ezekiel][@sc-knowhy-719] Daniel “continued even unto the first year of king Cyrus” ([[Dan. 1:21]]). Jeremiah had said, “these nations shall serve the king of Babylon seventy years” ([[Jer. 25:11]]).

The captivity “put a stop to the old sin of idolatry; it was a time of great spiritual revival,” and some psalms were written then.[@bd-captivities] Jerusalem's destruction “marks a major break in the course of Jewish history but not its end.”[@draper-exile] In 561 Jehoiachin's captivity was relaxed under Evil-merodach ([[2 Kgs. 25:27]]).[@bible-chron-ot][@lds-scriptures]

<h2 id="return">Persia and the return</h2>

Persia took Babylon in 539 (the table and the Bible Dictionary: 538).[@scripture-helps-ezra][@bible-chron-ot][@bd-persia] Cyrus, first king of the Persians and Medes, captured it from Nabonidus and Belshazzar:[@bd-cyrus] “In that night was Belshazzar the king of the Chaldeans slain” ([[Dan. 5:30]]). Isaiah had named Cyrus “about 180 years before”: “That saith of Cyrus, He is my shepherd” ([[Isa. 44:28]]).[@gs-cyrus]

The decree came about a year later, 538 (the table: 537; Captivities: 536): “he hath charged me to build him an house at Jerusalem” ([[Ezra 1:2]]).[@scripture-helps-ezra][@matson-between-testaments][@bible-chron-ot][@bd-captivities][@lds-scriptures] Zerubbabel, Jehoiachin's grandson and governor under Cyrus, rebuilt the temple with Haggai and Zechariah despite Samaritan opposition. Work resumed in 520, and the temple was dedicated in 516 (also dated 515).[@bd-zerubbabel][@bd-ezra][@bd-temple-of-zerubbabel][@matson-between-testaments] Haggai spoke about 520; Zechariah 520–518.[@bd-haggai][@bd-zechariah] Most exiles stayed in Babylon; those who remained abroad are the Dispersion.[@ot-manual-kings-j][@bd-dispersion]

Esther's story belongs to the captivity, under Ahasuerus, “commonly identified as Xerxes I, who ruled Persia from 486 to 465 BC.”[@bd-esther-book][@scripture-helps-esther] Ezra obtained Artaxerxes's edict in 458; Nehemiah, the king's cupbearer, rebuilt the walls (the table: 444); Malachi spoke about 430.[@bd-ezra][@bd-nehemiah][@bible-chron-ot][@bd-malachi] Judea was a Persian province until Alexander defeated Persia in 334.[@bd-persia][@ot-manual-kings-k] Chronicles, which runs from the Creation to Cyrus's proclamation[@bd-chronicles], appears to address the question “Are we still God's people and does the covenant still stand?”[@sc-knowhy-861] See [Persia and the Return](/old-testament/guides/persia-and-the-return/), [Daniel](/daniel/) and [Malachi](/malachi/).
