The Old Testament tells of women of great faith and also shows the hardships women faced in the ancient world, “oppression, abuse, and other forms of mistreatment”; customs that seem unjust now “were shaped by the cultural norms of the time.”[@scripture-helps-women] Ancient Israel was mostly male dominated: women’s roles lay in the household, and they did not usually hold religious or political leadership, which is likely why men are named more often.[@scripture-helps-women] A woman’s standing was typically tied to being a wife and mother, so an unmarried or widowed woman often faced poverty.[@scripture-helps-women] This guide describes how a household and a marriage were made, what the law said, what the text says of the women it names, the passages that are hard to read, and what latter-day revelation teaches.

<h2 id="household">The household and how a marriage was made</h2>

In the First Temple period the home was the *bet ab*, the “house of the father”: parents and children, and often the husband’s brothers and their families, “his unmarried, divorced, or widowed sisters,” and servants. A new bride’s greatest challenge was to find her place in that hierarchy, and barrenness “was considered the greatest curse upon a woman”; ancient biblical laws “generally provided for the divorce or expulsion of a wife who bore no children.”[@bybee-womans-world]

Marriage was usually preceded by a formal betrothal, “absolutely binding” once entered. On the wedding day the bride was escorted to her husband’s home in a procession, and a marriage deed was signed.[@bd-marriage] The betrothal contract could name the *mohar*, the bride-price the groom paid the bride’s family; a dowry “returned to the bride in the case of divorcement”; and “whether the groom could take another wife.” Betrothal generally lasted a year or more.[@bybee-womans-world]

The text shows bride-gifts and service. Abraham’s servant gives Rebekah jewels and raiment, and “he gave also to her brother and to her mother precious things” ([[Gen. 24:53]]). Shechem offers “never so much dowry and gift” ([[Gen. 34:12]]). Jacob serves “seven years for Rachel” ([[Gen. 29:18]]). Saul tells David that “the king desireth not any dowry, but an hundred foreskins of the Philistines” ([[1 Sam. 18:25]]).[@lds-scriptures] The notes at [[Ex. 22:16]] and [[Gen. 34:9]] take up the King James word “dowry.”

A dowry compensated a daughter for leaving the family property, helped set up the new household, and was her security should she be widowed or divorced.[@olson-matriarchs] Rachel and Leah say their father “hath sold us, and hath quite devoured also our money” ([[Gen. 31:15]]). A man newly married was free at home for a year “and shall cheer up his wife” ([[Deut. 24:5]]).[@lds-scriptures] A father gave his daughter: Laban brings Leah to Jacob and then gives him Rachel ([[Gen. 29:23]], [[Gen. 29:28]]). But Rebekah’s family say, “We will call the damsel, and inquire at her mouth,” and she answers, “I will go” ([[Gen. 24:57]], [[Gen. 24:58]]).

<h2 id="plural-marriage">More than one wife</h2>

The peoples around Israel accepted a man’s having more than one wife. Kings took several for alliances; among others the common reason was a first wife who had not conceived, and slave women often became second wives. Second marriages were regulated by law, as first ones were.[@sears-shannon-polygamy] The households of Genesis fit this. Sarai says, “go in unto my maid; it may be that I may obtain children by her” ([[Gen. 16:2]]); Rachel says of Bilhah, “that I may also have children by her” ([[Gen. 30:3]]); Leah “took Zilpah her maid, and gave her Jacob to wife” ([[Gen. 30:9]]).

A concubine (Hebrew *pilegesh*) was a wife of lesser legal standing, whose children might have lesser standing. Abraham gives the sons of his concubines gifts, not an explicit part of the inheritance ([[Gen. 25:6]]), while Jacob’s twelve sons seem to inherit alike whoever their mother was. Genesis calls Hagar Abram’s “wife” ([[Gen. 16:3]]); Rachel, Leah, Bilhah and Zilpah are almost always called Jacob’s wives, and Bilhah is labeled a concubine at [[Gen. 35:22]].[@sears-shannon-polygamy]

Israel practiced plural marriage for largely the same reasons and in largely the same ways as its neighbors, and the law’s rules for it almost always followed common custom.[@sears-shannon-polygamy] By the monarchy, monogamy seems to have been “the most common practice outside the royal household,” and the betrothal contract could forbid a second wife.[@bybee-womans-world] See [What the Restoration teaches](#restoration).

<h2 id="levirate-marriage">Levirate marriage</h2>

Levirate marriage was “the custom of a widow marrying her deceased husband’s brother or sometimes a near heir”; the name is from the Latin *levir*, “husband’s brother.”[@bd-levirate-marriage] The law: “her husband’s brother shall go in unto her, and take her to him to wife” ([[Deut. 25:5]]).[@lds-scriptures] The first son counted as the dead man’s heir, so that his name “be not put out of Israel”;[@strathearn-ruth] the law secured both the widow and the continuation of the dead man’s line and property.[@baird-jacob-levirate] If the brother refused, the widow came before the elders, took off his sandal and spat in his face ([[Deut. 25:9]]),[@lds-scriptures] and was free to marry outside the family.[@strathearn-ruth]

Judah tells Onan, “Go in unto thy brother’s wife, and marry her, and raise up seed to thy brother” ([[Gen. 38:8]]), and later sends Tamar home: “Remain a widow at thy father’s house” ([[Gen. 38:11]]). Childless, she was bereft of husband and offspring, and her husband’s property reverted to Judah and his other sons.[@spackman-judah-tamar]

Boaz redeems Naomi’s land and marries Ruth in a levirate-type marriage, though technically neither was required of him by law.[@strathearn-ruth] The book of Ruth uniquely associates the kinsman redeemer with the levirate law.[@scripture-helps-ruth-samuel] A nearer kinsman had the first right and declined, and Ruth became Boaz’s wife.[@bd-ruth]

<h2 id="divorce">Divorce</h2>

A husband who found “some uncleanness” in his wife could write her “a bill of divorcement” ([[Deut. 24:1]]).[@lds-scriptures] The bill let a divorced woman remarry, and her first husband could not take her back after she had married another; that rule “would necessarily put a check upon frivolous divorces.”[@ot-manual-deuteronomy-17-33] Divorce was permitted “because of the hardness of the people’s hearts,” but as Jesus explained, “from the beginning it was not so.”[@bd-divorce] The scriptures counsel against divorce generally.[@gs-divorce]

The Lord asks Israel, “Where is the bill of your mother’s divorcement, whom I have put away?” ([[Isa. 50:1]]). See the notes there and at [[Jer. 3:8]].

<h2 id="the-law">What the law provided for women</h2>

The law of Moses included guidelines “to protect and support women, especially those who were most vulnerable,” among them inheritance for daughters and the care of widows.[@scripture-helps-women] The provisions that name women:

- **Parents.** “Honour thy father and thy mother” ([[Ex. 20:12]]); Leviticus names the mother first: “Ye shall fear every man his mother, and his father” ([[Lev. 19:3]]).[@lds-scriptures]
- **A daughter given in marriage as a maidservant** ([[Ex. 21:7–11]]). She “shall not go out as the menservants do.” If her master, who “hath betrothed her to himself,” is displeased, he may not sell her to a foreign nation. If he takes another wife, “her food, her raiment, and her duty of marriage, shall he not diminish,” and if he withholds these, “then shall she go out free without money.” For “duty of marriage” an alternative rendering is “marital rights.”[@lds-scriptures] One reading holds that this guaranteed her rights “in sharp contrast to the practice of most other people whose women were viewed as property”;[@ot-manual-ex-21-24] another notes the same protection in the laws of Lipit-Eshtar of Sumer and Hammurabi of Babylon.[@sears-shannon-polygamy]
- **A woman enticed.** If a man entices a maid who is not betrothed, “he shall surely endow her to be his wife”; if her father refuses, “he shall pay money according to the dowry of virgins” ([[Ex. 22:16–17]]).
- **Widows.** “Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child” ([[Ex. 22:22]]). See the note at [[Ex. 22:23]].
- **Wives and inheritance.** The law forbids marrying a woman and her daughter, or two sisters; tells a king not to take so many wives that it becomes excessive; and protects the inheritance of the children of a less-loved wife.[@sears-shannon-polygamy] The man “may not make the son of the beloved firstborn before the son of the hated, which is indeed the firstborn” ([[Deut. 21:16]]).[@lds-scriptures]
- **A captive woman** ([[Deut. 21:11–14]]). A man may see “a beautiful woman” among the captives and desire her “to thy wife” ([[Deut. 21:11]]); if he has no delight in her, he must let her go, and “thou shalt not make merchandise of her, because thou hast humbled her” ([[Deut. 21:14]]).[@lds-scriptures] A woman so taken could be married “but could not be treated as a slave or as a captive.”[@ot-manual-deuteronomy-17-33]
- **A woman violated** ([[Deut. 22:23–29]]). The law distinguishes cases. On one reading: a betrothed woman in the city who did not cry out is “regarded as consenting,” and both die; in the open field, where she cried and no one helped, it is “a forcible rape,” and the man alone dies; an unbetrothed virgin is married by the man, who pays her father fifty shekels and may never divorce her.[@ot-manual-deuteronomy-17-33][@lds-scriptures] A woman sexually assaulted “would have lived desolately, unless the situation could be socially validated in marriage.”[@goff-plagiary]
- **Zelophehad’s daughters** come forward ([[Num. 27:1]]), and the LORD says, “The daughters of Zelophehad speak right” and gives them “a possession of an inheritance among their father’s brethren” ([[Num. 27:7]]).[@lds-scriptures] They may marry whom they think best, within their father’s tribe ([[Num. 36:6]]), so that the land does not pass to another tribe.[@ot-manual-numbers-13-36]

<h2 id="the-women">The women, and what the text says of them</h2>

**Sarah.** Barren ([[Gen. 11:30]]). God tells Abraham, “in all that Sarah hath said unto thee, hearken unto her voice” ([[Gen. 21:12]]); this suggests the Lord was working through Sarah.[@olson-matriarchs] Latter-day revelation says she “administered unto Abraham” ([[D&C 132:65]]).[@lds-scriptures]

**Hagar.** An Egyptian handmaid of Sarah.[@bd-hagar] Abraham and Sarah never use her name or speak to her directly; only God and His messenger do.[@smith-hagar] She is the first woman in the Old Testament to hear a birth announced, and the only person in it to name a divine messenger or, perhaps, the LORD: “Thou God seest me” ([[Gen. 16:13]]).[@easton-recognizing] One reading finds that the narrator consistently undermines her;[@easton-recognizing] another reads the chapters as showing that the enslaved woman was not overlooked by God.[@sc-knowhy-839] See [The God Who Sees, Hears, and Remembers](/genesis/themes/god-who-sees/).

**Rebekah.** Troubled by the struggle in her womb, “she went to inquire of the LORD” and is answered ([[Gen. 25:22–23]]). She is “far from colorless and helpless” in a patriarchal culture, and acts to see the chosen son receive the birthright.[@olson-matriarchs]

**Leah and Rachel.** “The LORD saw that Leah was hated” ([[Gen. 29:31]]). The sisters answer Jacob together: “Is there yet any portion or inheritance for us in our father’s house?” ([[Gen. 31:14]]).

**The barren wives.** Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel and other women of the Old Testament were barren, and God’s promises and covenants were kept through divine and joyous means; Luke’s account of Christ’s birth begins with another barren couple.[@sc-knowhy-705]

**Tamar** ([[Gen. 38]]). Judah withholds his third son; Tamar secures an heir by a deception. Told she is with child, Judah orders her burned, then says, “She hath been more righteous than I” ([[Gen. 38:24–26]]). One reading allows that her motives may have been good while the deceptive means and the immorality that followed were wrong;[@scripture-helps-gen-37-41] another, that by the standards of her day she had justifiably made Judah do what he had shirked.[@spackman-judah-tamar]

**The midwives.** Shiphrah and Puah “feared God, and did not as the king of Egypt commanded them” ([[Ex. 1:17]]), and “he made them houses” ([[Ex. 1:21]]).[@scripture-helps-ex-1] The women of Exodus act under their own moral authority and move the story.[@tilton-moses-midwife]

**Miriam.** “Miriam the prophetess” leads the women with timbrels ([[Ex. 15:20]]); later she murmurs against Moses and is smitten with leprosy.[@bd-miriam]

**Deborah.** “A prophetess” who “judged Israel” ([[Judg. 4:4]]), the only female judge in the Old Testament; a prophetess is “a woman who has received a testimony of Jesus and enjoys the spirit of revelation.”[@scripture-helps-judges] She says the LORD will sell Sisera “into the hand of a woman” ([[Judg. 4:9]]), and in her song she arose “a mother in Israel” ([[Judg. 5:7]]).[@lds-scriptures]

**Ruth and Naomi.** Ruth tells Naomi, “thy people shall be my people” ([[Ruth 1:16]]). Boaz says the whole city knows she is “a virtuous woman” ([[Ruth 3:11]]); the women call her “better to thee than seven sons” ([[Ruth 4:15]]).[@lds-scriptures] A foreign widow, she became a mother in the royal line of David and Jesus Christ.[@scripture-helps-women]

**Hannah.** Her barrenness brought shame and ridicule, even in her own household.[@scripture-helps-women] She vows to give a son to the LORD ([[1 Sam. 1:11]]).[@lds-scriptures] Her song has the first reference to the title Messiah, “his Anointed,” and may be compared with Mary’s.[@bd-hannah]

**Abigail.** “A woman of good understanding,” married to a man who was “churlish and evil” ([[1 Sam. 25:3]]); David blesses her “advice” for keeping him from shedding blood ([[1 Sam. 25:33]]).[@lds-scriptures] She “can be seen as a powerful type or symbol of Jesus Christ.”[@scripture-helps-1-samuel-17]

**The wise women.** The wise woman of Tekoah, whom Joab sends for ([[2 Sam. 14:2]]), and the wise woman of Abel, who speaks with Joab and goes “unto all the people in her wisdom” ([[2 Sam. 20:22]]).[@lds-scriptures]

**Huldah.** The priest and the king’s officers go to “Huldah the prophetess,” who answers, “Thus saith the LORD God of Israel” ([[2 Kgs. 22:14–15]]).[@lds-scriptures] It is unknown why they went to her rather than other prophets, but their going shows her spiritual stature.[@scripture-helps-2-kings-16-25]

**Esther.** Chosen queen after Vashti is removed, she risked her life to go before the king unsummoned, and her intervention saved her people.[@scripture-helps-esther] “If I perish, I perish” ([[Esth. 4:16]]); she pleads again “with tears” ([[Esth. 8:3]]).[@lds-scriptures] Mordecai indicated that the Lord may have placed her as queen to be His instrument.[@scripture-helps-esther] The book of Esther never mentions God.[@sc-knowhy-863]

**The woman of Proverbs 31.** The book ends with an acrostic poem on a woman who symbolizes the outcome of living by its wisdom; “virtuous woman” ([[Prov. 31:10]]) is better rendered “capable woman” or “woman of strength.”[@scripture-helps-proverbs]

<h2 id="hard-passages">The hard passages</h2>

Some accounts “show the harsh and painful realities of living in a fallen world.”[@scripture-helps-women] Stories like these have traditionally been neglected; of three of them, one reading holds that a close study of the narrative makes it clear that God does not condone the actions they tell.[@easton-recognizing]

**Hagar sent back** ([[Gen. 16:9]]). The angel tells her, “Return to thy mistress, and submit thyself under her hands.” There is “no definitive or easy way” to understand it. One explanation is that it was part of God’s plan for Abraham’s descendants; another, that a pregnant woman could not otherwise survive in the wilderness.[@easton-recognizing]

**Lot and his daughters** ([[Gen. 19]]). Genesis has Lot offer his two daughters to the men of Sodom in place of his guests ([[Gen. 19:8]]). The Joseph Smith Translation has the mob demand “the men, and thy daughters also,” and Lot plead that they not be brought out.[@jst-gen-19][@scripture-helps-gen-18-23] Afterward the daughters make their father drink wine and conceive by him ([[Gen. 19:32]]). In the Joseph Smith Translation the daughters “dealt wickedly.”[@lds-scriptures] The text does not explain their motive. One suggestion is that they believed marriage and children were no longer possible, and that, though they may have recognized their actions as immoral, they thought their desperate situation justified them.[@benson-lot] The children are said to be the ancestors of the Moabites and the Ammonites; one important Moabite was Ruth.[@scripture-helps-gen-18-23]

**Dinah** ([[Gen. 34]]). Shechem “took her, and lay with her, and defiled her” ([[Gen. 34:2]]). Her brothers are grieved because he “had wrought folly in Israel in lying with Jacob’s daughter; which thing ought not to be done” ([[Gen. 34:7]]); they slay Hamor and Shechem ([[Gen. 34:26]]); and the chapter ends with their question, “Should he deal with our sister as with an harlot?” ([[Gen. 34:31]]). Their outrage was justified, but the deceit and slaughter were evil, and Jacob’s blessing ([[Gen. 49:5–7]]) shows that neither he nor the Lord condoned them.[@ot-manual-gen-24-36]

**Jephthah’s daughter** ([[Judg. 11]]). Jephthah makes a vow on condition that the LORD give him victory over Ammon ([[Judg. 11:30]]); at the end of two months he “did with her according to his vow which he had vowed: and she knew no man,” and the daughters of Israel lament her four days each year ([[Judg. 11:39–40]]).[@lds-scriptures] One reading is that he “offered his own daughter in sacrifice”;[@bd-jephthah] another, that the vow dedicated her to lifelong virginity, since the text has her bewail her virginity and says she “knew no man.”[@ot-manual-judges-1-12]

**The Levite’s concubine** ([[Judg. 19]]). A Levite’s concubine leaves him for her father’s house; the King James Version says she “played the whore against him” ([[Judg. 19:2]]),[@lds-scriptures] the Septuagint only that she “departed from him,”[@lxx-rahlfs] and another reading takes it as unfaithfulness.[@keil-judges] Later the Levite brings her out to the men of Gibeah, who abuse her all night ([[Judg. 19:25]]); he divides her body into twelve pieces and sends them through Israel, and all who see it say that no such deed had been done since Israel came out of Egypt ([[Judg. 19:29–30]]).[@lds-scriptures] The book’s refrain is that “there was no king in Israel” ([[Judg. 21:25]]).[@lds-scriptures] These chapters, with “the rape of the concubine at Gibeah,” are “samples of Israel’s worst days,” and nothing in them shows Israel doing right.[@ot-manual-judges-13-21] Lot’s offer of his daughters at [[Gen. 19:8]] is cross-referenced to [[Judg. 19:24]].[@lds-scriptures] See also [[Hosea 9:9]].

**Bathsheba** ([[2 Sam. 11–12]]). David sends messengers and takes her ([[2 Sam. 11:4]]); “the thing that David had done displeased the LORD” ([[2 Sam. 11:27]]). Nathan says, “Thou art the man” ([[2 Sam. 12:7]]), and charges that David killed Uriah the Hittite “and hast taken his wife to be thy wife” ([[2 Sam. 12:9]]).[@lds-scriptures] “As a consequence of this evil deed, David lost his spiritual inheritance,” President Faust taught;[@scripture-helps-2-samuel-11] and the revelation says he sinned “in the case of Uriah and his wife” ([[D&C 132:39]]).[@lds-scriptures]

**Tamar, David’s daughter** ([[2 Sam. 13]]). She says, “do not force me; for no such thing ought to be done in Israel” ([[2 Sam. 13:12]]); Amnon, “being stronger than she, forced her” ([[2 Sam. 13:14]]). The event is called by its name, rape.[@easton-recognizing] Her brother Absalom tells her to “hold now thy peace”; she “remained desolate” in his house ([[2 Sam. 13:20]]), and David “was very wroth” ([[2 Sam. 13:21]]).[@lds-scriptures] David is found mourning Amnon, not Tamar.[@easton-recognizing] One reading finds that the narrator sides with Tamar as a woman of wisdom.[@easton-recognizing] Amnon used and then despised her, and David’s own sin left him without a basis to correct Amnon.[@ot-manual-2-samuel-13]

**The unfaithful wife of the prophets** ([[Hosea 1–3]]; [[Jer. 3]]; [[Ezek. 16]]; [[Ezek. 23]]). Jeremiah 3 and Ezekiel 23 picture Israel and Judah as the LORD’s two wives, unfaithful by worshipping other gods; hearers who knew plural households would have recognized the scandal of both wives betraying Him.[@sears-shannon-polygamy] The imagery of Ezekiel 16:30–34 is “some of the most scathing in all the scriptures,” and all the sexual references in Ezekiel 23 are metaphorical.[@ot-manual-26] The husband’s treatment of the wife in Hosea 2 “is distressing,” but the threats are to be read beside the grief in the same speech.[@hord-not-fragile] The images of violence against women in [[Hosea 13:16]] (and 9:10–17; 10:12–15) personify cities and “are not justifications of violence against women.”[@hord-not-fragile] To the charge that Ezekiel 16 and 23 are misogynist, one answer is: “They are violent and offensive texts, but that violence is not directed against women,” and they “give no comfort to contemporary abuse of women.”[@sloane-ezekiel] Whether Hosea’s marriage was literal or not, the message is fidelity to the Lord.[@schade-hosea-family] In Hosea the LORD promises a betrothal “in righteousness, and in judgment, and in lovingkindness, and in mercies” ([[Hosea 2:19]]), and in Ezekiel 16 He says, “I will remember my covenant with thee in the days of thy youth” ([[Ezek. 16:60]]). See [“I Will Betroth Thee unto Me”](/hosea/themes/betroth-thee/) and [Zion and Her Husband](/isaiah/themes/zion-and-her-husband/).

<h2 id="restoration">What the Restoration teaches</h2>

**Eve.** Her name means “the mother of all living” ([[Moses 4:26]]); she and Adam “will share eternal glory for their role in making possible the eternal progress of all mankind,”[@gs-eve] and latter-day revelation “gives us an awareness of her nobility.”[@bd-eve] Without the transgression, she says, “we never should have had seed” and never “the joy of our redemption” ([[Moses 5:11]]). Nephi: “Adam fell that men might be” ([[2 Ne. 2:25]]). Joseph F. Smith saw “our glorious Mother Eve, with many of her faithful daughters” ([[D&C 138:39]]).[@lds-scriptures] “Help meet” ([[Gen. 2:18]]) translates a Hebrew phrase whose key word, *ezer*, is never used of menial help but of help out of a desperate situation, often help from God; Moses 5 shows Eve working, praying and teaching beside Adam.[@sc-knowhy-833]

**Partnership.** “He shall rule over thee” ([[Gen. 3:16]]) describes the most common arrangement of Israelite marriage, and the wife’s dependence may be read beside the husband’s leaving his family to cleave to her, “a recognition that the wife was much more than property but, in fact, kin.”[@belnap-beginning] The verse has sometimes been misunderstood to permit a domineering husband, which is false.[@scripture-helps-gen-3-4] President Kimball: “I have a question about the word rule. It gives the wrong impression. I would prefer to use the word preside.”[@kimball-womanhood-1976] Elder Soares: “They walk side by side, as equals.”[@soares-partnership-2022] The rib “signifies neither dominion nor subservience, but a lateral relationship as partners.”[@nelson-eve-1987] Fathers and mothers “are obligated to help one another as equal partners.”[@family-proclamation] Over the years, “Females in many societies became subservient to males rather than side-by-side partners.”[@bingham-united-2020]

**Plural marriage.** Monogamy is God’s standard unless He declares otherwise;[@topics-plural-marriage] “in biblical times, the Lord commanded some to practice plural marriage.”[@gte-plural-marriage] Jacob calls the many wives and concubines of David and Solomon “abominable” ([[Jacob 2:24]]) and commands “one wife; and concubines he shall have none” ([[Jacob 2:27]]). The Lord, he says, does “delight in the chastity of women” ([[Jacob 2:28]]), and the exception is allowed only if the Lord commands it, to “raise up seed” ([[Jacob 2:30]]).[@sc-knowhy-64] The Lamanites kept that command ([[Jacob 3:5]]).[@lds-scriptures] Latter-day Saints “do not understand all of God’s purposes” in it.[@gte-plural-marriage]

D&C 132 names Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, David and Solomon as justified in “their having many wives and concubines” ([[D&C 132:1]]); says “God commanded Abraham, and Sarah gave Hagar to Abraham to wife” ([[D&C 132:34]]); and says of David, “in none of these things did he sin against me save in the case of Uriah and his wife” ([[D&C 132:39]]).[@lds-scriptures]

The revelation required that a wife give her consent before her husband could enter plural marriage.[@gte-plural-marriage-kirtland] Of a wife taught the law, verse 64 says, “then shall she believe and administer unto him, or she shall be destroyed” ([[D&C 132:64]]). Verse 65 says that if a wife “receive not this law,” she “becomes the transgressor,” and her husband “is exempt from the law of Sarah, who administered unto Abraham according to the law when I commanded Abraham to take Hagar to wife” ([[D&C 132:65]]).[@lds-scriptures] The law of Sarah is “presumably the requirement that the husband gain the consent of the first wife.”[@gte-plural-marriage-kirtland] The revelation names Sarah as one who “administered unto Abraham according to the law,” where Genesis tells Sarah’s giving of Hagar as the household’s expedient; Sarah’s giving is thus “transformed from a matter of expediency to an act of obedience to divine command.”[@sears-shannon-polygamy]

In the Old Testament itself there are “very few hints” of what the revelation declares, and almost everything known of Israelite plural marriage suggests a cultural practice that followed traditional procedures; the revelation “changes and expands” the Bible’s account.[@sears-shannon-polygamy] One suggestion is that a few held a higher understanding while most of Israel followed its neighbors, and that some doctrines were “kept hid” until this dispensation.[@sears-shannon-polygamy] Plural marriage is not required for exaltation, and God will not force anyone to enter or remain in a marriage he or she does not want.[@topics-plural-marriage]
