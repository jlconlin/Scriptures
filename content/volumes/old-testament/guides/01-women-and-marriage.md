God created man and woman in His image ([[Gen. 1:27]]) and “commanded Adam and Eve to have children and to be equal partners as husband and wife.”[@scripture-helps-gen-1-2] Over the years that understanding “largely disappeared. Females in many societies became subservient to males rather than side-by-side partners.”[@bingham-united-2020] The words “help meet” ([[Gen. 2:18]]) come from two Hebrew words “that mean helper, ally, or rescuer in an equal relation or role”: a help meet “was a companion who would walk shoulder to shoulder with Adam in full partnership.”[@scripture-helps-gen-1-2] The Old Testament shows both: women who “often suffered under the inequity of their time,” and a God who “still saw them, spoke to them, and worked through them in powerful ways.”[@scripture-helps-women] This guide covers the women's standing, the customs of marriage, what the law provided, the women the text names, the hard passages, and what the Restoration teaches.

<h2 id="valued">Valued, and more than property</h2>

**Wife and property.** The bride-price (*mohar*) is “bridewealth,” found “in many societies throughout the world” and “not considered a sale by people in those cultures”; “Israelite wives were not thought of as slaves in biblical texts.”[@bibleodyssey-marriage] A man's power over his wife and children “arguably constituted a type of limited ownership,” though an Israelite male “cannot be said to have ‘owned’ his wife in the way that he owned an article of clothing, a beast of burden.”[@kawashima-could-a-woman-say-no] A man leaves his father and mother and cleaves to his wife ([[Gen. 2:24]]), “a recognition that the wife was much more than property but, in fact, kin and to be treated as such.”[@belnap-beginning] Malachi calls her “the wife of thy covenant” ([[Mal. 2:14]]), and Old Testament marriage has been argued to be “a divinely protected covenant between husband and wife.”[@reynolds-hugenberger-review] The harshness of some ancient laws “extended to men as well as to women,” and to emphasize the provisions unfair to women “without explaining the legal system as a whole presents a distorted view.”[@byu-studies-baker-review]

**Mother beside father.** “Honour thy father and thy mother” ([[Ex. 20:12]]); Leviticus names the mother first: “Ye shall fear every man his mother, and his father” ([[Lev. 19:3]]). A son is told to forsake not “the law of thy mother” ([[Prov. 1:8]]); a rebellious son is brought to the elders by “his father and his mother” ([[Deut. 21:19]]). The word “mother or one of its derivatives occurs 232 times in the Old Testament—more than all of the other standard works combined.” Though one might think women “lesser than or subservient to men, their roles were equally important.”[@hilton-motherhood-ot] The Old Testament, “in spite of its largely male-focused culture,” depicts mothering “as an active process performed by agents.”[@shannon-models-motherhood]

**Valued by God, not by birthright or childbearing.** “The value God places on an individual is not attached to birthrights”: Isaac's greater responsibility did not make Sarah “superior in God's eyes to Hagar,” who “is worthy enough to receive divine revelation herself.” Rebekah spoke to God during her pregnancy, “an indication that her individual worth to God was not dependent on giving birth.”[@olson-matriarchs] Elkanah asks barren Hannah, “am not I better to thee than ten sons?” ([[1 Sam. 1:8]]). The angel “appeared unto the woman,” Manoah's wife ([[Judg. 13:3]]), and she answers her husband, “If the LORD were pleased to kill us, he would not have received a burnt offering and a meat offering at our hands” ([[Judg. 13:23]]). The Shunammite woman says to her husband, “I perceive that this is an holy man of God,” and proposes, “Let us make a little chamber, I pray thee, on the wall” ([[2 Kgs. 4:9–10]]).

**What a “virtuous woman” is.** The Hebrew behind “virtuous woman” ([[Ruth 3:11]]; [[Prov. 31:10]]; [[Prov. 12:4]]) is *'eshet chayil*. In “mighty men of valour” ([[Josh. 1:14]]; [[Judg. 6:12]]) *chayil* is strength; of a woman it is “ability, efficiency, often involving moral worth.”[@bdb] The woman of Proverbs 31 “symbolizes the outcome of living according to the wisdom found throughout Proverbs,” and “a more accurate rendering” of [[Prov. 31:10]] “would be ‘capable woman’ or ‘woman of strength.’”[@scripture-helps-proverbs] A lengthy part of the poem describes her economic enterprises ([[Prov. 31:13–22]]);[@sc-knowhy-639] she “openeth her mouth with wisdom” ([[Prov. 31:26]]), and her children “call her blessed; her husband also” ([[Prov. 31:28]]).

**What the women did.** The Old Testament preserves prophets and laws more than “the daily functioning of families and households, where women's roles were most significant,” so women's work is mentioned less prominently; women “simply wielded their influence in a more intimate, less visible sphere.” Mothers have been argued to have taken the primary role in educating children, and women to have been “active participants in the religious rites” of Israel, with a household role that “gave women considerable informal power and at least some legal authority.”[@sc-knowhy-639] Five women are called prophetesses: Miriam, Deborah, Huldah, Isaiah's wife and Noadiah. **Deborah** is, of all the judges, “the only one to be called a prophet, and the only one described as performing a judicial function”; Barak says, “if thou wilt not go with me, then I will not go” ([[Judg. 4:8]]). The wise woman of Abel speaks for her city ([[2 Sam. 20:16]]).[@jwa-deborah] **Rahab** says the LORD “is God in heaven above, and in earth beneath” ([[Josh. 2:11]]); “by faith the harlot Rahab perished not” ([[Heb. 11:31]]). **Jael** is “blessed above women” ([[Judg. 5:24]]). **Achsah** asks her father, “give me also springs of water. And he gave her the upper springs, and the nether springs” ([[Josh. 15:19]]). **Jehosheba** hid Joash from Athaliah ([[2 Kgs. 11:2]]). Job gave his daughters “inheritance among their brethren” ([[Job 42:15]]). The daughters of Zelophehad “were able to significantly shape the law itself,” and Esther's “political actions were instrumental in saving the lives of her people.”[@sc-knowhy-639] “Damsels playing with timbrels” went in the procession to the sanctuary ([[Ps. 68:25]]). Rizpah “suffered neither the birds of the air to rest on them by day, nor the beasts of the field by night” ([[2 Sam. 21:10]]).

**The law's care for the vulnerable.** “Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child” ([[Ex. 22:22]]); if they cry, “I will surely hear their cry” ([[Ex. 22:23]]). The gleanings are left “for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow” ([[Deut. 24:19]]). “Thou shalt not pervert the judgment of the stranger, nor of the fatherless; nor take a widow's raiment to pledge” ([[Deut. 24:17]]).

<h2 id="customs">The customs</h2>

<h3 id="household">The household and how a marriage was made</h3>

In the First Temple period the home was the *bet ab*, the “house of the father”: parents and children, often the husband's brothers and their families, and servants. A new bride's greatest challenge was to find her place in that hierarchy, and barrenness “was considered the greatest curse upon a woman.”[@bybee-womans-world] Marriage was usually preceded by a formal betrothal, “absolutely binding” once entered; on the wedding day the bride was escorted to her husband's home in a procession.[@bd-marriage] The betrothal contract could specify the *mohar*, the bride-price the groom paid, and “whether the groom could take another wife,” and could name a dowry to be “returned to the bride in the case of divorcement”;[@bybee-womans-world] the dowry was her security should she be widowed or divorced.[@olson-matriarchs] Jacob serves “seven years for Rachel” ([[Gen. 29:18]]), and Rachel and Leah say their father “hath sold us, and hath quite devoured also our money” ([[Gen. 31:15]]). Rebekah's family ask that she stay “a few days, at the least ten” ([[Gen. 24:55]]); they say, “We will call the damsel, and inquire at her mouth” ([[Gen. 24:57]]), and she answers, “I will go” ([[Gen. 24:58]]). “In their response, her family honored her opinion and wishes.”[@olson-matriarchs]

<h3 id="plural-marriage">More than one wife</h3>

The societies around Israel were “very accepting of polygamy.” Among those who were not royalty, a common reason for a second wife was that the first wife had not conceived. Slave women often became second wives, and second marriages were regulated by law, as first ones were.[@sears-shannon-polygamy] Sarai says, “go in unto my maid; it may be that I may obtain children by her” ([[Gen. 16:2]]); Rachel says of Bilhah, “that I may also have children by her” ([[Gen. 30:3]]). A concubine was a wife of lesser legal standing, whose children might have lesser standing.[@sears-shannon-polygamy] Israel practiced plural marriage for largely the same reasons and in largely the same ways as its neighbors.[@sears-shannon-polygamy] See [What the Restoration teaches](#restoration).

<h3 id="levirate-marriage">Levirate marriage</h3>

Levirate marriage was “the custom of a widow marrying her deceased husband's brother or sometimes a near heir.”[@bd-levirate-marriage] The law: “her husband's brother shall go in unto her, and take her to him to wife” ([[Deut. 25:5]]).[@lds-scriptures] The first son counted as the dead man's heir, so that his name “be not put out of Israel”;[@strathearn-ruth] the law secured both the widow and the continuation of the dead man's line and property.[@baird-jacob-levirate] If the brother refused, the widow took off his sandal and spat in his face ([[Deut. 25:9]]).[@lds-scriptures] Judah tells Onan, “Go in unto thy brother's wife” ([[Gen. 38:8]]); Boaz redeems Naomi's land and marries Ruth in a levirate-type marriage, though technically neither was required of him by law.[@strathearn-ruth]

<h3 id="divorce">Divorce</h3>

A husband who found “some uncleanness” in his wife could write her “a bill of divorcement” ([[Deut. 24:1]]).[@lds-scriptures] The bill let a divorced woman remarry, and her first husband could not take her back; that rule “would necessarily put a check upon frivolous divorces.”[@ot-manual-deuteronomy-17-33] Divorce was permitted “because of the hardness of the people's hearts,” but as Jesus explained, “from the beginning it was not so.”[@bd-divorce]

<h2 id="the-law">What the law provided for women</h2>

The law of Moses included guidelines “to protect and support women, especially those who were most vulnerable.”[@scripture-helps-women] “If a man sell his daughter to be a maidservant” ([[Ex. 21:7]]) and she becomes a wife, her “food, her raiment, and her duty of marriage” (or marital rights) could not be diminished, or “then shall she go out free without money” ([[Ex. 21:10–11]]).[@lds-scriptures] One reading takes the sale to mean a father arranging his daughter's marriage, and holds that this guaranteed her rights “in sharp contrast to the practice of most other people whose women were viewed as property”;[@ot-manual-ex-21-24] another calls her “a slave girl who marries her male owner” and notes the same protection for an existing wife in the laws of Lipit-Eshtar of Sumer and Hammurabi of Babylon.[@sears-shannon-polygamy] The law protects the inheritance of the children of a less-loved wife.[@sears-shannon-polygamy] A captive woman must be let go if the man has no delight in her: “thou shalt not make merchandise of her, because thou hast humbled her” ([[Deut. 21:14]]).[@lds-scriptures] Zelophehad's daughters come forward, and the LORD says, “The daughters of Zelophehad speak right” and gives them “a possession of an inheritance among their father's brethren” ([[Num. 27:7]]).[@lds-scriptures] They are to marry within their father's tribe, so that land does not pass to another tribe ([[Num. 36:6]]).[@ot-manual-numbers-13-36]

<h2 id="the-women">The women, and what the text says of them</h2>

**Sarah.** God tells Abraham, “in all that Sarah hath said unto thee, hearken unto her voice” ([[Gen. 21:12]]); this suggests the Lord was working through Sarah.[@olson-matriarchs]

**Hagar.** An Egyptian handmaid of Sarah.[@bd-hagar] She is the first woman in the Old Testament to hear a birth announced, and the only person in it to name a divine messenger or, perhaps, the LORD: “Thou God seest me” ([[Gen. 16:13]]).[@easton-recognizing] See [The God Who Sees, Hears, and Remembers](/genesis/themes/god-who-sees/).

**Rebekah.** Troubled by the struggle in her womb, “she went to inquire of the LORD” and is answered ([[Gen. 25:22–23]]).

**Tamar** ([[Gen. 38]]). Told she is with child, Judah orders her burned, then says, “She hath been more righteous than I” ([[Gen. 38:24–26]]). One reading allows that her motives may have been good while the deceptive means were wrong;[@scripture-helps-gen-37-41] another, that by the standards of her day she had justifiably made Judah do what he had shirked.[@spackman-judah-tamar]

**The midwives.** Shiphrah and Puah “feared God, and did not as the king of Egypt commanded them” ([[Ex. 1:17]]), and “he made them houses” ([[Ex. 1:21]]).[@scripture-helps-ex-1] The women of Exodus “are active participants in the salvation of Israel.”[@tilton-moses-midwife]

**Miriam.** “Miriam the prophetess” leads the women with timbrels ([[Ex. 15:20]]).[@bd-miriam]

**Deborah.** “A prophetess” who “judged Israel” ([[Judg. 4:4]]), the only female judge in the Old Testament;[@scripture-helps-judges] in her song she arose “a mother in Israel” ([[Judg. 5:7]]).[@lds-scriptures]

**Ruth.** Boaz says the whole city knows she is “a virtuous woman” ([[Ruth 3:11]]), with the same word as “mighty men of valour” (see [Valued, and more than property](#valued)); the women call her “better to thee than seven sons” ([[Ruth 4:15]]).[@lds-scriptures] A foreign widow, she became a mother in the royal line of David and Jesus Christ.[@scripture-helps-women]

**Hannah.** Her song has the first reference to the title Messiah, “his Anointed.”[@bd-hannah]

**Abigail.** “A woman of good understanding” ([[1 Sam. 25:3]]);[@lds-scriptures] she “can be seen as a powerful type or symbol of Jesus Christ.”[@scripture-helps-1-samuel-17]

**Huldah.** The priest and the king's officers go to “Huldah the prophetess,” who answers, “Thus saith the LORD God of Israel” ([[2 Kgs. 22:14–15]]).[@lds-scriptures] Their going shows her spiritual stature.[@scripture-helps-2-kings-16-25]

**Esther.** She risked her life to go before the king unsummoned.[@scripture-helps-esther] “If I perish, I perish” ([[Esth. 4:16]]).[@lds-scriptures]

<h2 id="hard-passages">The hard passages</h2>

Some accounts “show the harsh and painful realities of living in a fallen world.”[@scripture-helps-women] These stories have traditionally been neglected. One reading of the stories of Hagar and of David's daughter Tamar holds that close study makes it clear that God does not condone the actions described.[@easton-recognizing]

**Hagar sent back** ([[Gen. 16:9]]). The angel tells her, “Return to thy mistress, and submit thyself under her hands.” There is “no definitive or easy way” to understand it. One explanation is that it was part of God's plan for Abraham's descendants; another, that a pregnant woman could not otherwise survive in the wilderness.[@easton-recognizing]

**Lot and his daughters** ([[Gen. 19]]). Genesis has Lot offer his two daughters to the men of Sodom in place of his guests ([[Gen. 19:8]]). The Joseph Smith Translation has the mob demand “the men, and thy daughters also,” and Lot plead that they not be brought out.[@jst-gen-19][@scripture-helps-gen-18-23] Afterward the daughters make their father drink wine and conceive by him ([[Gen. 19:32]]). The Joseph Smith Translation of Genesis 19 “clearly describes the actions of Lot's daughters as wicked”; they “seemed to understand that they were doing something wrong.”[@scripture-helps-gen-18-23] Genesis does not explain their motive; one suggestion is that they believed marriage and children were no longer possible.[@benson-lot]

**Dinah** ([[Gen. 34]]). Shechem “took her, and lay with her, and defiled her” ([[Gen. 34:2]]). Her brothers' outrage was justified, but the deceit and slaughter were evil, and Jacob's blessing ([[Gen. 49:5–7]]) shows that neither he nor the Lord condoned them.[@ot-manual-gen-24-36]

**Jephthah's daughter** ([[Judg. 11]]). Jephthah makes a vow on condition that the LORD give him victory over Ammon ([[Judg. 11:30]]). One reading is that he “offered his own daughter in sacrifice”;[@bd-jephthah] another, that the vow dedicated her to lifelong virginity, since the text has her bewail her virginity and says she “knew no man.”[@ot-manual-judges-1-12]

**The Levite's concubine** ([[Judg. 19]]). The King James Version says she “played the whore against him” ([[Judg. 19:2]])[@lds-scriptures], the Septuagint only that she “departed from him.”[@lxx-rahlfs] “The man took his concubine, and brought her forth unto them; and they knew her, and abused her all the night until the morning” ([[Judg. 19:25]]).[@lds-scriptures] These chapters are “samples of Israel's worst days,” and nothing in them shows Israel doing right.[@ot-manual-judges-13-21]

**The unbetrothed virgin.** “Then the man that lay with her shall give unto the damsel's father fifty shekels of silver, and she shall be his wife; because he hath humbled her, he may not put her away all his days” ([[Deut. 22:29]]).[@lds-scriptures]

**Bathsheba** ([[2 Sam. 11–12]]). David sends messengers and takes her ([[2 Sam. 11:4]]); “the thing that David had done displeased the LORD” ([[2 Sam. 11:27]]).[@lds-scriptures] “As a consequence of this evil deed, David lost his spiritual inheritance.”[@scripture-helps-2-samuel-11]

**Tamar, David's daughter** ([[2 Sam. 13]]). She says, “do not force me; for no such thing ought to be done in Israel” ([[2 Sam. 13:12]]); Amnon, “being stronger than she, forced her” ([[2 Sam. 13:14]]). The event is called by its name, rape.[@easton-recognizing] She “remained desolate” in her brother's house ([[2 Sam. 13:20]]).[@lds-scriptures] One reading finds that the narrator sides with Tamar as a woman of wisdom.[@easton-recognizing]

**The unfaithful wife of the prophets** ([[Hosea 1–3]]; [[Ezek. 16]]; [[Ezek. 23]]). The imagery of Ezekiel 16:30–34 is “some of the most scathing in all the scriptures,” and all the sexual references in Ezekiel 23 are metaphorical.[@ot-manual-26] Of Ezekiel 16 and 23: “They are violent and offensive texts, but that violence is not directed against women.”[@sloane-ezekiel] The images of violence against women in [[Hosea 13:16]] personify cities and “are not justifications of violence against women.”[@hord-not-fragile] Whether Hosea's marriage was literal or not, the message is fidelity to the Lord.[@schade-hosea-family] See [“I Will Betroth Thee unto Me”](/hosea/themes/betroth-thee/).

<h2 id="restoration">What the Restoration teaches</h2>

**Eve.** She and Adam “will share eternal glory for their role in making possible the eternal progress of all mankind,”[@gs-eve] and latter-day revelation “gives us an awareness of her nobility.”[@bd-eve] Without the transgression, she says, “we never should have had seed” and never “the joy of our redemption” ([[Moses 5:11]]). Joseph F. Smith saw “our glorious Mother Eve, with many of her faithful daughters” ([[D&C 138:39]]).[@lds-scriptures]

**Genesis 3:16.** “He shall rule over thee” has sometimes been misunderstood to permit a domineering husband, which is false.[@scripture-helps-gen-3-4] Of the word “rule,” “I would prefer to use the word preside,”[@kimball-womanhood-1976] and husband and wife “walk side by side, as equals.”[@soares-partnership-2022]

**Plural marriage.** Monogamy is God's standard unless He declares otherwise;[@topics-plural-marriage] “in biblical times, the Lord commanded some to practice plural marriage.”[@gte-plural-marriage] Jacob calls the many wives and concubines of David and Solomon “abominable” ([[Jacob 2:24]]) and commands “one wife; and concubines he shall have none” ([[Jacob 2:27]]); the exception is allowed only if the Lord commands it, to “raise up seed” ([[Jacob 2:30]]).[@sc-knowhy-64] D&C 132 says “God commanded Abraham, and Sarah gave Hagar to Abraham to wife” ([[D&C 132:34]]).[@lds-scriptures]

The revelation required that a wife give her consent before her husband could enter plural marriage; the law of Sarah is “presumably the requirement that the husband gain the consent of the first wife.”[@gte-plural-marriage-kirtland] Of a wife taught the law, verse 64 says, “then shall she believe and administer unto him, or she shall be destroyed” ([[D&C 132:64]]). Verse 65 makes the exception: if a wife “receive not this law,” she “becomes the transgressor,” and her husband “is exempt from the law of Sarah, who administered unto Abraham according to the law when I commanded Abraham to take Hagar to wife” ([[D&C 132:65]]).[@lds-scriptures] Sarah's giving of Hagar is thus “transformed from a matter of expediency to an act of obedience to divine command.”[@sears-shannon-polygamy]

In the Old Testament itself there are “very few hints” of what the revelation declares.[@sears-shannon-polygamy] Plural marriage is not required for exaltation, and God will not force anyone to enter or remain in a marriage he or she does not want.[@topics-plural-marriage]

<h2 id="what-the-accounts-show">What the accounts show</h2>

Through Jesus Christ, “all injustices will one day be made right.”[@scripture-helps-women]
