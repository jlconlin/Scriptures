# New Testament guides: the plan

A project note (the build ignores it). It holds the author's decisions about guides for the New Testament as a volume. The standard for a guide is `PAGES.md` (“How a guide is written”); the Old Testament's decisions, which hold here unless this file says otherwise, are in `content/volumes/old-testament/GUIDES.md`. What the four Gospels' chapters share is in `GOSPELS.md` beside this file.

## State

Planned on 2026-11-20; nothing is written.

## What the author decided (2026-11-20)

- **No new category of page.** The New Testament's background (customs, the Jewish parties, Rome) goes into volume guides and chapter notes, the two kinds of page the site already has. If the list of guides grows long, the volume's index is grouped under headings; that is a change of layout, not a new kind of page.
- **Few guides, each with sections a note can link to, not a page for each group or custom.** A page each for the Pharisees, the Sadducees and the scribes would be the glossary the author set aside for the Old Testament (2026-10-23) and would retell the Bible Dictionary; the groups are also explained by their differences from each other, which separate pages lose.
- **The question is answered in the note; the guide is for going further.** A reader's question arises at a verse (“why is this question a trap?”) and its answer is in that verse's note, in a sentence or two. The guide holds the general description once, and the note links to its section.
- **A group's motives belong in the notes, not in a guide.** A guide makes no connections of its own (`PAGES.md`), and the Gospels give motives scene by scene (envy, the fear of Rome in [[John 11:48]]).
- **Guides are added as the chapters show the need** (author: “We can add them as we feel we need”). The five below are where to start. A guide is split, or one added, when a section has outgrown its guide or the notes keep explaining something no guide holds, not on a forecast.
- **A harmony is one of the volume's guides.** It is built from the table of parallels the writers work from (`GOSPELS.md`). The precise order of events is not the point (author: “Frankly, the precise chronology doesn't matter that much”); the author has a chronology of their own, mostly the Church's. A doctrinal harmony (what the four teach on a subject) is a different guide and is not planned.

## The guides

**To start with, for the Gospels:**

1. **Between the Testaments.** From Persia to Rome: Alexander and the Greek kingdoms, the Maccabees, Rome's arrival, the Herods and the governors down to Pilate, with the volume's timeline in the style of the Old Testament's. It picks up where the Old Testament's *Persia and the Return* stops. What was first listed as a separate guide on Rome and the Herods is merged into it: that guide's history is the end of this one's.
2. **The parties and leaders.** Pharisees, Sadducees, scribes, priests, the Sanhedrin, Samaritans, publicans. One guide, because each is clearest beside the others.
3. **Daily life.** The household, betrothal, meals, burial, money, farming and fishing: what the parables and the stories assume.
4. **Temple and Synagogue in Jesus's Day** (a working title). Only what the Old Testament's *The Law, the Covenant and the Temple* does not have, and linking back to it for what a priest, a sacrifice or a feast is: the synagogue; Herod's temple as it stood (the courts, the money changers, Solomon's porch, the veil); the feasts as they were kept then (the pilgrimage, the Passover meal, the water and light of Tabernacles behind John 7–8); the Feast of Dedication ([[John 10:22]]); Sabbath traditions, unless those sit better in guide 2 under the Pharisees and the oral law, which is decided when the two are outlined.
5. **The land.** Galilee, Samaria, Judea and the country beyond, with a map.

Then **the harmony**, whose table is built before the chapters and whose prose is written after them, when there are notes to link to.

Guides 2, 3 and 4 are worth writing before the chapters, so that the chapters link to them instead of each explaining the same thing.

**Where the rest of the first Rome guide went:** crucifixion and the trial before Pilate are answered in the notes of those chapters; taxes and publicans are in guide 2; a soldier or a centurion is a sentence in the note where he appears. Rome earns a guide of its own with Acts and Paul's letters (provinces, citizenship, the appeal to Caesar, travel), not with the Gospels.
