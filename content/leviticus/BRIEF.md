# Leviticus brief

Book-level decisions for Leviticus. The site-wide standard is in `STANDARDS.md`; this file records what is particular to Leviticus. Writers, checkers, and reviewers read both before starting a chapter. Jeremiah's brief (`content/jeremiah/BRIEF.md`) is the model, and its section “What a chapter is for” holds here. **Exodus's brief (`content/exodus/BRIEF.md`) is the nearest neighbor**: Leviticus opens where Exodus closes, at the tabernacle just set up, and the Exodus chapters already explain the furnishings, the priests' garments, their washing and anointing, and the word “atonement.” Read its table “Explained once: where” before writing.

## What the author has decided

Nothing for Leviticus in particular beyond choosing it and the theme page above. The standing rules are in `STANDARDS.md` §1 and `AUTHORING.md`. How three land on this book:

- **Newest trumps oldest.** *Scripture Helps: Old Testament* (2025) has no Leviticus chapter of its own. Its chapter “Exodus 35–40; Leviticus 1; 4; 16; 19” (`scripture-helps-ex-35`) gives Leviticus three sections: what the book is (paragraphs 46–47), the purpose of the sacrifices in 1–7 (48–52), and the Day of Atonement (53–55). It says nothing on chapters 8–15 or 17–27. The Student Manual (three chapters, 1980) is older; where they differ, follow *Scripture Helps*.
- **Don't replicate the Gospel Library.** *Scripture Helps*' three sections are beside the verse. A note on 1:3, 1:4 or 16:21 does not repeat them; it goes where they stop (usually to the study an endnote cites, or to the Hebrew), or it is not written.
- **Quote the Church's modern material sparingly.** Two passages every Church source prints: President Ballard's “the sacrifice changed from the offering to the offerer” and Elder Maxwell's “the animal in us.” Each is quoted at most once on the site, at the place named under “Explained once.” The volume guide *The Law and the Covenant* already quotes Ballard: read it and agree.

## What a chapter is for here

Leviticus is 859 verses in 27 chapters with two short stories in it (10:1–7; 24:10–23). It is the book readers stop at. The reader's questions are almost never “what does this require” and almost always *why*: *Why kill an animal to be forgiven? Why does it matter whether the animal is a bull or a pigeon, burned whole or eaten? What did Nadab and Abihu do that was so wrong? Why is a woman unclean after childbirth, and twice as long for a daughter? What is “leprosy” in a garment or a wall? Who or what is the scapegoat for? Why are pork and shellfish forbidden, and is that still binding? Why is “love thy neighbour as thyself” in the middle of laws about mixed seed and haircuts? Was the jubilee ever kept? What does any of this have to do with Christ, or with me?* (The last is answered by pointing to Christ, never by application: `STANDARDS.md` §1.)

**Say what the rite was before what it means.** A reader who can't picture the burnt offering can't see what it pointed to. A note first says plainly what was done (who brought what, where it was killed, what happened to the blood, the fat and the meat, who ate what), then why, from the text's own reasons (“to make atonement,” “a sweet savour,” “be holy, for I am holy”), then what scripture and the Church's sources say it signified. Keep the three apart.

**Law chapters get a thread, not a note per statute.** As in Exodus 21–23: a `thread` that says how the chapter's rules hang together and what they protect, notes on the rules a reader would stop at, and nothing on a rule that reads plainly.

**Short chapters are right for some of this.** Chapters 6–7 and 22 repeat instructions given earlier from the priest's side; chapters 13–15 are long and technical. Three or four notes and a clear `thread` are a full chapter. `setting` may fall under the checker's word count; ignore the warning. Don't pad.

**Graphic and bodily passages** (the blood rites; 15's discharges; 18 and 20's forbidden unions; 20:2–5's child sacrifice; 24's stoning): say what the text says plainly and briefly; describe rather than quote (`STANDARDS.md` §3).

## Divisions

The Bible Dictionary (“Leviticus”) gives the outline, and `book.yaml` follows it: “(1) The sacrificial ordinances (Lev. 1–7) … (2) The ritual observed in the consecration of priests, together with an account of the deaths of Nadab and Abihu … (Lev. 8–10). (3) Laws relating to ceremonial uncleanness (Lev. 13–15). (4) The ritual of the Day of Atonement (Lev. 16). (5) The law of holiness (Lev. 17–26) … Lev. 27 is supplementary.” The site puts 11–12 with 13–15 and 27 with 17–26.

Welch's *Old Testament Minute* reads a larger shape, after Mary Douglas: the book follows the high priest's path through the tabernacle, from the court of the altar (1–17) through the entrance (18 and 20 as its two pillars) and the holy place (21–24) to the Holy of Holies, where the Lord's voice is heard (26). That is his and Douglas's reading, and it may frame chapter 1's or chapter 17's `setting` in a sentence, as theirs; no chapter is built on it.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, which later scripture takes up which line) is the coordinator's direction of where to look, some of it from memory. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **`when`.** Leviticus is given at Sinai, from the tent just raised: Exodus 40:17 sets up the tabernacle on “the first month in the second year,” and Numbers 1:1 opens on “the first day of the second month, in the second year.” The book itself says only “in mount Sinai” (7:38; 25:1; 26:46; 27:34). **Proposed:** every chapter's `when` is “At Sinai, in the second year after the Exodus” or the like, from the text; no B.C. year (Exodus 1:11 on the site treats the date of the Exodus: point there if a reader needs it).
- **Who wrote Leviticus.** Said once, in chapter 1's `setting`, in two sentences: the book gives its words as the Lord's to Moses (1:1; 27:34), and latter-day revelation names Moses as the writer of the five books (`[[1 Ne. 5:11]]`); Genesis 1 and Exodus 1 on the site have the fuller statement: link. Welch: the material “developed from the theophany at Sinai and should be closely associated with Moses,” though “its present form may not come directly or completely from his personal handwriting.” Scholars assign 1–16 to a priestly source and 17–26 to a related “Holiness” source and date them later (Shannon; Pierce, footnote 6); that is said once, as scholars' view, in chapter 17's `setting` where the name “Holiness Code” is explained, and no chapter argues it.
- **Names of God.** Copy any phrase with LORD, Lord or GOD from `data/kjv/leviticus.json`; 24:11 and 24:16 were corrected to “LORD.” The footnote at 19:14 reads “HEB Jehovah.” The LORD of the Old Testament is the premortal Christ: said where it answers a question, in a clause, pointing to `[[Ex. 3:14]]`; not re-argued.
- **The Hebrew numbering differs in chapters 5–6 only.** Hebrew 5:20–26 is King James 6:1–7, and Hebrew 6:1–23 is King James 6:8–30; `hebrew.mjs` takes the King James number and prints both. A commentary may follow the Hebrew; cite the King James verse.
- **Hebrew only where it answers a reader's question** (`STANDARDS.md` §3). Leviticus has many words a reader stops at: “meat offering,” “scapegoat,” “leprosy,” “devils” (17:7), “jubile,” “afflict your souls.” Those pay. The names of the offerings (*ʿolah*, *minḥah*, *shelamim*, *ḥaṭṭaʾt*, *ʾasham*) are given once each, at the chapter that owns the offering, and only if the Hebrew answers a question the English raises (the burnt offering is the one that “goes up”; the sin offering's name is disputed: below). No more than about a quarter of a chapter's notes are Language notes.
- **The Joseph Smith Translation.** No Leviticus chapter has a JST footnote. Horton counts six small changes in the manuscript (12:3–5; 21:1; 21:11; 22:6, “wot” to “know”), and he says of 12:3–5 that “there is probably no objective way of knowing whether these changes were the Prophet's idea.” Give one only where it answers a reader's question, citing Horton for it, as a reading in the manuscript and not in the Church's printed footnotes.
- **The New Testament reads Leviticus.** Hebrews 7–10 (the high priest, the veil, the blood, “once”); `[[Matt. 22:39]]`, `[[Rom. 13:9]]`, `[[Gal. 5:14]]`, `[[James 2:8]]` on 19:18; `[[1 Pet. 1:16]]` on “be holy”; `[[Luke 2:22–24]]` (Mary's offering, 12:8); `[[Mark 1:44]]` (the cleansed leper sent to the priest, 14); `[[Luke 4:18–19]]` with Isaiah 61 and the jubilee (a reading: below); `[[Acts 10:9–15]]` and `[[Mark 7:19]]` on the food laws; `[[Gal. 3:24]]`. Where the New Testament says what a passage meant, the chapter says so as scripture's own reading.
- **The Book of Mormon reads it too.** Amulek on the “great and last sacrifice” (`[[Alma 34:10–14]]`); Abinadi on the law (`[[Mosiah 13:27–35]]`); `[[2 Ne. 25:24–25]]`; `[[Jacob 4:5]]`; `[[3 Ne. 9:19–20]]`; `[[3 Ne. 15:4–8]]`. Welch lists many more “verbal connections—some stronger than others” in his *Old Testament Minute*: one may be given where it shows how a Nephite prophet read the passage, as Welch's identification. **No comparison panel, and nothing is written for a Book of Mormon chapter** (`AUTHORING.md`).
- **Other books on the site.** Exodus for everything in the table above. Genesis for Adam's and Noah's offerings (`[[Gen. 4:4]]`, `[[Gen. 8:20–21]]`), Melchizedek's tithe. Amos 4:4–5 and 5:21–25, Hosea 6:6, Isaiah 1:11–17, Jeremiah 7:21–23, Psalms 40, 50 and 51 on sacrifice without righteousness: point from the chapter that owns the offering in a clause, without retelling; those notes already link here. Ezekiel 44–46 (the priests and offerings of the restored temple) and Malachi 1 (blemished offerings) and 3:3 (the sons of Levi): link where a chapter meets one. The volume guides *The Law and the Covenant* (`/old-testament/guides/law-and-covenant/`, sections on sacrifice, the feasts and clean and unclean) and *The Temple and Worship* carry the overviews: point to them, don't retell them.
- **The law fulfilled.** The Church's reading: the law of Moses was “a preparatory gospel to bring its adherents to Christ” (Bible Dictionary, “Law of Moses”), fulfilled in Him (`[[3 Ne. 15:4–5]]`), and “the law of Moses is not the same thing as the law of sacrifice,” whose principles continue (Ballard). Say it where a chapter's `christ` section needs it, briefly, and point to chapter 1's. The Bible Dictionary's own phrases “adapted to a lower spiritual capacity” and “carnal commandments” are quoted only with its balancing sentence, that the law had “many provisions … of high ethical and moral value that were equal to the divine laws of any dispensation.” **No chapter calls the law primitive, and none says Christians outgrew it**: it was given by Jehovah and fulfilled by Him.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports an arc that joins chapters in a way a reader would not see chapter by chapter; the coordinator collects them, and `propose-themes` and Codex propose the list at the end of the book. No theme page is written until the author chooses it.

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter; don't re-explain what another chapter explains, point with a `[[reference]]`; a Witness of Christ does not open by saying what the chapter lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer, since the site shows it alone as the note's card; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; “In plain words” adds nothing to the verse; Hebrew only where it changes what the reader understands; don't join two verses yourself and call it the text's meaning; no personal application.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that 27 chapters say the same thing. Quotations were copied from the pages named on 2026-10-14 and 15; re-read the page before using one.

- **Types of Christ.** The question behind the whole book. Scripture makes the types itself: the law points to Christ (`[[Jacob 4:5]]`; `[[Alma 34:14]]`, “every whit pointing to that great and last sacrifice”); Adam's offering is “a similitude of the sacrifice of the Only Begotten” (`[[Moses 5:7]]`); Hebrews 9–10 sets the high priest on the Day of Atonement beside Christ (the Bible Dictionary, “Fasts”: “a contrast is drawn”); `[[Heb. 13:11–12]]` reads the sin offering burned “without the camp” of Christ suffering “without the gate.” The Church's manuals add more and say “can”: *Scripture Helps* says the blood on the mercy seat “can also represent the blood of Jesus Christ” and the scapegoat “can symbolize Jesus Christ”; *Come, Follow Me* (“Thoughts to Keep in Mind”): “the goats symbolized Jesus Christ, taking the place of the sinful people.” **Proposed:** where scripture makes the type, say so without hedging; where only a manual makes it, give it as the manual's, once; where a scholar makes it, as that scholar's reading; and **no typology of the writer's own** (a color, a number, a part of the animal, a kind of wood). The `christ` section of a chapter with nothing of this kind is short and rests on what the chapter says of the Lord.
- **What did sacrifice do? (1:4; 4; 17:11).** The Bible Dictionary (“Sacrifices”): “The fundamental idea of the sin and trespass offerings was atonement, expiation”; offerings “must be the offerer's own property, properly acquired (Lev. 1:3)”; and “Sins committed with a high hand … did not admit of expiation under the Mosaic law (Num. 15:30–31).” *Scripture Helps*: sacrifice was “also intended to help them become more holy.” Whether the animal stood in the offerer's place is debated. Belnap: “Some have suggested that the placing of the hand indicated that a substitution was to take place, the animal now representing the offerer; others believe that it simply indicated ownership.” Lane reads substitution and says in a footnote that the passage behind it “is heavily debated.” **Proposed:** the note at 1:4 gives both readings, Belnap's and Lane's, and says the text says the offering is “accepted for him to make atonement for him,” no more. Christ's death in our place is taught by scripture in its own words (`[[Isa. 53:5–6]]`; `[[Mosiah 14]]`; `[[Alma 34:10–12]]`) and is said from there, not read into the hand.
- **The sin offering's name (chapter 4).** The King James “sin offering” is *ḥaṭṭaʾt*. Milgrom (a Jewish scholar, in an essay the Religious Studies Center published): it “must be rendered as ‘purification (or purgation) offering,’” and its blood cleanses the sanctuary, not the offerer, since “the impure person needs purification and the sinner needs forgiveness.” Gee assigns the sin offering to inadvertent sins and the trespass offering to “intentional sins”; Lane's notes show the trespass offering's scope is debated. **Proposed:** chapter 4 gives the King James name, says the Hebrew word can name both the sin and its cleansing (the lexicon page), gives Milgrom's reading as one scholar's, and does not settle it. The Church's reading (“atonement, expiation”) is given first.
- **Nadab and Abihu (10:1–3).** The Bible Dictionary (“Nadab”): “struck dead for offering ‘strange’ fire (fire not taken from the altar).” The text says only that the fire was one the Lord “commanded them not.” Welch reads it as noncompliance in the sacred service, with the story serving “as springboards for further instructions”; the commandment against wine at 10:9 follows, and some read a link. **No Latter-day Saint study read explains what the fire was.** **Proposed:** the note says what the text says, the Bible Dictionary's gloss, what the Lord's word in 10:3 means (“I will be sanctified in them that come nigh me”), and why holiness made the error mortal, from a source read; a theory of what they did (drunkenness, the wrong incense, the wrong time) only as one reader's, and only if one source read makes it. Aaron's silence (10:3) and Moses's anger and Aaron's answer (10:16–20) are worth a note each.
- **Clean and unclean (11–15).** The Bible Dictionary (“Clean and unclean”): uncleanness was “ceremonially or ritually unclean and should not be taken to mean that the touching of a dead body or the bearing of children was morally evil.” The Student Manual: unclean “did not suggest something disgusting or filthy.” The text's reason is holiness (11:44–45; 20:24–26, where the clean and unclean animals mark Israel off from the nations). **The health explanation of the food laws is not given as the reason**: no Church source says it, Lund's 2007 article (“swine … are not prohibited under the current health code”) states it in passing, and Milgrom disputes the hygiene rationale. A note may say that some have proposed it, as a proposal. That the food laws ended: `[[Acts 10:9–15]]`; `[[Mark 7:19]]`; the Bible Dictionary: “being fulfilled by the Atonement of Jesus Christ, they are no longer required of the believers.” The Word of Wisdom is not the same law and is not compared with it.
- **Childbirth (chapter 12).** A woman is unclean seven days for a son and fourteen for a daughter, and brings a burnt offering and a sin offering. *Why the difference* is not known: the Student Manual says the question “seems to have no key at present.” **Proposed:** say what the text requires, that uncleanness is not sin (the Bible Dictionary), that the sin offering here is the purification offering (chapter 4's note: point), and that the reason for the doubled time is not given in the text or known; give a proposed reason only from a source read, as its author's. **Don't use the Student Manual's argument from women's “status.”** Mary's offering of “a pair of turtledoves” at the Savior's birth (`[[Luke 2:22–24]]`, the poor woman's offering of 12:8) is the chapter's Witness of Christ, from the text. The women guide (`/old-testament/guides/women-and-marriage/`) may be linked.
- **“Leprosy” (13–14).** Scripture Central's “Leprosy”: biblical leprosy “refers to a variety of skin ailments, most of which are not to be associated with Hansen's disease”; it is “those skin diseases that rendered an individual ritually unclean under the law of Moses.” The Bible Dictionary: cloth and walls “were said to be leprous when they had patches of mildew or some fungous growth.” **Don't say the disease was a punishment or a symbol of sin as the text's meaning**: the Student Manual says leprosy “became a type or a symbol of sin” and glosses the word as “smitten of God” from a word study; that is its reading, given once if at all, as such. Identifying a modern disease is given only as a guess, as Scripture Central gives it. The priest diagnoses and readmits; he does not heal. The Savior heals and then sends the man to the priest, “for a testimony unto them” (`[[Mark 1:40–44]]`): chapter 14's Witness of Christ, from the text.
- **Discharges (15).** Briefly, plainly, without euphemism or detail. What the chapter teaches is that ordinary bodily life touches the holy, and that it is set right by washing and time. Welch's summary is enough.
- **The Day of Atonement (16).** *Scripture Helps* is beside the verse (the high priest in white linen, once a year; the goat for the Lord; the scapegoat; the high priest foreshadowing Christ). The note goes further: the order of the day (for himself, then for the sanctuary, then for the people), the incense cloud over the mercy seat (16:13), what “cleanse” means at 16:30, “afflict your souls” (16:29; fasting: the Bible Dictionary, “Fasts”: “appears to be the only fast ordered by the law”), and Hebrews 9. **Azazel (16:8, 10, 26).** The King James “scapegoat” is *la-ʿazazel*, “for Azazel.” The Bible Dictionary (“Fasts”) glosses Azazel as “the completely separate one, the evil spirit regarded as dwelling in the desert”; its entry “Scapegoat” says only “the goat upon whose head the sins of the people were laid.” Milgrom, as Welch quotes him: “the most plausible explanation is that Azazel is the name of a demon … who has been eviscerated of his erstwhile demonic powers.” Keil (in the Student Manual) makes Azazel Satan. **Proposed:** give the Hebrew, the King James's “scapegoat” (Tyndale's coinage, if a source read says so), and the readings (a name of a place, of a demon, or “the goat that departs”) with their sources; the goat carries the sins away, and the text does not offer it to Azazel. **The goat is not made Satan's, and it is not made Christ's beyond the Church's own “can symbolize.”** The rabbinic report that the goat was pushed from a cliff (KnoWhy #675; Milgrom: it “may not reflect biblical praxis”) is later tradition: say so if it is mentioned. KnoWhy #849 on why the day falls in autumn: its sabbatical year beginning on the Day of Atonement is from the Mishnah, not Leviticus (25:9 puts only the jubilee trumpet there).
- **Blood (17:10–14).** The Bible Dictionary (“Blood”): “The atoning power of a sacrifice was in the blood because it was regarded as containing the life of the animal and because the sacrifice was a type of the great sacrifice who is Jesus Christ (Lev. 17:11; Heb. 9:22).” One full note at 17:11. “Devils” (17:7) is *seʿirim*, “goats” (the lexicon page); say so, briefly.
- **Forbidden unions (18 and 20).** Say what each law forbids, by the relationships named, briefly; the chapter's frame is the text's own, “after the doings of the land of Egypt … and … of the land of Canaan, … shall ye not do” (18:3). **18:22 and 20:13** are given as what the text prohibits, in its own words, with no modern application and no argument; if a reader would ask how the Church teaches the law of chastity today, a sentence from the Church's own published page, cited, is enough. **Clarke's and the manual's picture of Canaanite religion** (“public prostitution formed a grand part of the worship”) is not used: it is nineteenth-century and no recent source read supports it. Burnside (BYU Studies, 2020) on how chapter 20 answers chapter 18 is the study for the pair: his structure is his argument. **Molech (18:21; 20:2–5):** the Bible Dictionary: “a fire god, worshipped by passing children through or burning them in fire.” Say what the text forbids, and what is not known, briefly.
- **Holiness (19:2).** *Come, Follow Me*: “simply being in holy places doesn't make us holy. Leviticus 19 describes laws and commandments the Lord gave to help the Israelites increase in holiness.” Bowen: holiness is “extrinsic rather than intrinsic because it has its ultimate source in Yahweh”; the Holiness Code is “the ‘instruction’ (tôrâ) … to teach the Israelites how to become holy like Yahweh himself.” The Guide (“Holy”): “The opposite of holy is common or profane.” Chapter 19's thread is that ritual and moral commands stand side by side under one refrain, “I am the LORD”: the text's own, and the reader's question (why haircuts beside love of neighbor). **19:18** is the full note on the second great commandment: the verse in its context (“thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge”), the Savior's use (`[[Matt. 22:39]]`), and who “thy neighbour” was in the law and in Luke 10, from sources read. Elder Poelman (October 1984) reads the verse directly and may be quoted once. **19:28** (cuttings and marks) is said briefly from the text and a lexicon page; Clarke's “Hindoos” and the mark of the beast are not used.
- **Priests with a blemish (21:16–23).** The man may eat the holy bread but not approach the altar. Describe it plainly, say what the text says (“that he profane not my sanctuaries”), and give the reason only from a source read. The Religious Studies Center's “Disability and Social Justice in Ancient Israelite Culture” (*Covenant, Compassion*) bears on it: read it before use. No modern application, and nothing that suggests a person's worth.
- **The feasts (23).** The volume guide has the table: point to it, and give in chapter 23 what is the text's own (the holy convocations, the sheaf, the seven weeks, the booths and why, 23:43). New Testament links are scripture's: Christ the “firstfruits” (`[[1 Cor. 15:20]]`) beside the sheaf of 23:10–11; Pentecost (`[[Acts 2:1]]`); the Passover lamb (`[[1 Cor. 5:7]]`; chapter 12 of Exodus owns it). Say the link where scripture makes it, and no calendar typology of the writer's own.
- **The blasphemer (24:10–23).** The book's second story. Say what happened and that Moses asked the Lord (24:12), and why the case set the law for the stranger as for the native (24:16, 22). Stoning is described, not dwelt on. “Eye for eye” points to Exodus 21:24.
- **The jubilee (25).** The land is the Lord's: “the land is mine; for ye are strangers and sojourners with me” (25:23), the chapter's center. The Bible Dictionary (“Jubilee, Year of”) says there is no Old Testament record of a jubilee being kept: the volume guide says so; point. Bowen ties the jubilee to Isaiah 61:1–2 and `[[Luke 4:18–19]]`, and reads the kinsman-redeemer as a christological type: his readings, given as his. “Proclaim liberty” (25:10) and the Liberty Bell is reception history: not used. **Bondservants (25:39–46):** an Israelite may not be held as a slave and goes free at the jubilee; foreign bondservants may be held for life. Say both plainly, from the text and a source read; the law regulates an institution, and the chapter neither excuses nor applies it.
- **Blessings and cursings (26).** Muhlestein (*Interpreter* 32, 2019): Leviticus 26 is where “the Abrahamic Covenant is most fully outlined for Israel,” and 1 Nephi 2 echoes its promises; give the comparison as his. The curses are given briefly and in their order (five rounds, “seven times more”); 26:40–45 (the Lord remembers the covenant when they confess) is the chapter's end and its point: the curses are meant to bring Israel back. Amos 4:6–11 on the site uses the same pattern; link. 26:34–35 (the land enjoys its sabbaths) is read by `[[2 Chr. 36:21]]`: scripture's own reading.
- **Vows and their values (27).** The chapter sets what a person vowed to the Lord is redeemed for in silver, by age and sex. **Say that these are values for redeeming a vow, and say what they are, plainly; don't argue from them about a person's worth** in either direction unless a source read does, and then as its reading. “Whatsoever passeth under the rod” (27:32) is the counting of the flock for the tithe. The book's last verse (27:34) closes the Sinai law.

## Settled without the author

Kinds of decision a reviewer makes by the standard, not a question for the author (Psalms' brief has the rule):

- **No Church, BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* source answers a reader's real question, and an older commentary does:** give its answer as “one older reading,” within the limit of a third of the notes. If the question isn't one a reader would ask, there is no note. Leviticus will need this more than most books: the Latter-day Saint studies cluster on 1–7, 16, 19 and 26.
- **A Jewish or Christian scholar's reading** that a Religious Studies Center, BYU or *Interpreter* publication prints (Milgrom; Morales; Burnside) is cited as that publication's author and given as one scholar's reading, beside the Church's, never above it.
- **Two passages share words and no source joins them:** a note may set them side by side as shared wording and say so; it draws nothing from the pairing.
- **The King James reads a verse one way and the Hebrew or a modern translation another:** the paraphrase and thread start from the King James; a clarification from the Hebrew may go into the paraphrase when it materially helps, and then a visible note explains it (`STANDARDS.md` §6).
- **Two faithful sources read a verse differently:** give both, the Church's first, without choosing.
- **A source quoted inside a Church manual** whose own work can't be read is cited as the manual, for what the manual prints, as one view.
- **The Church's footnote points somewhere the note does not go:** leave it.
