Leviticus is a book about coming near. Its ordinary word for bringing an offering, *qārab*, is the word for drawing near, and it stands a hundred and two times in the book.[@oshb-wlc] On the eighth day of the priests’ consecration “all the congregation drew near and stood before the LORD” (9:5), having “brought that which Moses commanded,” and Moses said, “This is the thing which the LORD commanded that ye should do: and the glory of the LORD shall appear unto you” (9:6). Fire “came … out from before the LORD, and consumed upon the altar the burnt offering” (9:24). In the next verses two of Aaron’s sons “offered strange fire before the LORD, which he commanded them not,” and “there went out fire from the LORD, and devoured them” (10:1–2). The two verses open with the same five Hebrew words, “and fire went out from before the LORD and consumed”; the King James Version renders the one verb “consumed” and “devoured.”[@oshb-wlc] Moses then says what the LORD had spoken: “I will be sanctified in them that come nigh me” (10:3), the “near ones,” the priests.[@oshb-wlc][@cambridge-leviticus]

The book’s answer to who may come near, and how, is given in that pair of verses and worked out afterward. Nearness is given on the LORD’s terms. Fire accepts what He commanded, and fire consumes the priests who offered what He “commanded not.” The rest of the book keeps the rule. The unclean are kept from the tabernacle “that they die not” (15:31). After the two deaths, Aaron is told not to come within the veil “at all times … that he die not” (16:2), and one entry a year, with blood, makes the people “clean from all your sins before the LORD” (16:30) without their entering at all. Hebrews reads the single yearly entry as the sign that “the way into the holiest of all was not yet made manifest” (Heb. 9:8), until Christ “by his own blood … entered in once” (Heb. 9:12). Keil says of the fire of chapter 9 that it consecrated the altar service “through which a way was to be opened for the people to His throne of grace,” and of chapter 16 that its yearly repetition “could not fail to awaken a longing for the perfect sacrifice of the eternal High Priest.”[@keil-leviticus] This page reads the book’s approaches as one rule, nearness given and never taken; the sources read each passage in its place, and the chapter notes already set the two fires side by side.

## “All the congregation drew near”

Chapter 9 shows the congregation drawing near. Moses tells the people what to bring, “and all the congregation drew near and stood before the LORD” (9:5). Twice the chapter says why they may: “This is the thing which the LORD commanded that ye should do” (9:6), and Aaron is to “offer the offering of the people, and make an atonement for them; as the LORD commanded” (9:7). Then “the glory of the LORD appeared unto all the people. And there came a fire out from before the LORD, and consumed upon the altar the burnt offering and the fat: which when all the people saw, they shouted, and fell on their faces” (9:23–24). The Cambridge commentary reads the glory and the fire as a further sign of God’s approval, and lists like “manifestations of approval” for Gideon, Elijah and David.[@cambridge-leviticus] The Student Manual calls this altar fire “kindled by direct action of Jehovah.”[@ot-manual-lev-1-10] Keil adds what the fire did for the people: it gave “a divine consecration to the altar, or sacrificial service of Aaron and his sons, through which a way was to be opened for the people to His throne of grace.”[@keil-leviticus] The note on [[Lev. 9:24]] discusses the fire.

## “Which he commanded them not”

The next two verses tell of the deaths of two priests. “Nadab and Abihu, the sons of Aaron, took either of them his censer, and put fire therein, and put incense thereon, and offered strange fire before the LORD, which he commanded them not. And there went out fire from the LORD, and devoured them, and they died before the LORD” (10:1–2). “Offered” is the book’s word for bringing near, in the form that means to cause to come near; the congregation “drew near” in 9:5 in the simple form of the same verb.[@oshb-wlc] What was wrong with the fire the text does not say beyond “which he commanded them not.” The Bible Dictionary glosses it “fire not taken from the altar”; the Student Manual says that whether the coals or the incense were at fault “is not clear from the account,” and that the point is “an unauthorized form of worship.”[@bd-nadab][@ot-manual-lev-1-10] Elder Bruce R. McConkie, as a Seminary manual prints him, described the strange fire as “ordinances of their own devising,” and wondered whether the fire that devoured them was not “a type and a shadow of the spiritual destruction awaiting all who pervert the right ways of the Lord with ordinances of their own.”[@ot-seminary-teacher-resource-lev-1-16]

The fire is described in the words of the day before. “And there came a fire out from before the LORD, and consumed” (9:24) and “And there went out fire from the LORD, and devoured” (10:2) are the same five words in Hebrew, *wattēṣēʾ ʾēš millipnê YHWH wattōʾkal*; only the object changes, “the burnt offering” in one and “them” in the other.[@oshb-wlc] The Church’s footnotes join the two verses, and the repeated verb ties the two scenes together: “The word consume, however, is used here to keep up the connection between this verse and chap 9:24.”[@lds-scriptures][@ellicott-leviticus] Keil reads the two fires as one: “The fire of the holy God … which had just sanctified the service of Aaron as well-pleasing to God, brought destruction upon his two eldest sons.”[@keil-leviticus] Whether the text means one fire or two it does not say; what it says, in the same words, is that the fire which met the commanded offering met the uncommanded one.

Moses’ word over the dead is the book’s rule for the near ones. “This is it that the LORD spake, saying, I will be sanctified in them that come nigh me, and before all the people I will be glorified. And Aaron held his peace” (10:3). The Hebrew behind “them that come nigh me” is an adjective from the verb for drawing near, the near ones, and the commentaries read it of the priests: “the priests are those who have the right to approach God, and He shews Himself holy in punishing those who do it improperly.”[@oshb-wlc][@cambridge-leviticus] The Pulpit commentary: “If they have greater privileges, they have greater perils.”[@pulpit-leviticus] The theme page [I Will Be Sanctified](/leviticus/themes/i-will-be-sanctified/) discusses the verb of that sentence; this page follows the nearness. The bodies are carried “from before the sanctuary out of the camp” (10:4), with the verb of drawing near used of the men who carry them.[@oshb-wlc]

## “That they die not”

The book then draws the line where the unclean stand. The leper “shall dwell alone; without the camp shall his habitation be” (13:46), and the laws of uncleanness close with their reason: “Thus shall ye separate the children of Israel from their uncleanness; that they die not in their uncleanness, when they defile my tabernacle that is among them” (15:31). “That they die not” is a formula; the Cambridge commentary lists it as the law’s phrase for breaches of the sanctuary rules by priests, Levites and people alike.[@cambridge-leviticus] The Bible Dictionary keeps the categories apart: uncleanness meant being “ceremonially or ritually unclean” and “should not be taken to mean that the touching of a dead body or the bearing of children was morally evil,” yet “So long as a person was unclean he was cut off from the congregation.”[@bd-clean-unclean] Chapter 16 describes the one day that answers this uncleanness, when the high priest makes atonement for the holy place “because of the uncleanness of the children of Israel” (16:16).

## “That he come not at all times”

The instructions for the Day of Atonement are introduced after the deaths. “And the LORD spake unto Moses after the death of the two sons of Aaron, when they offered before the LORD, and died; and the LORD said unto Moses, Speak unto Aaron thy brother, that he come not at all times into the holy place within the veil before the mercy seat, which is upon the ark; that he die not: for I will appear in the cloud upon the mercy seat” (16:1–2). “When they offered” is the verb of drawing near once more, “in their drawing near.”[@oshb-wlc] Keil reads the date as the point: the deaths “as a punishment for wilfully ‘drawing near before Jehovah,’ was to be a solemn warning to Aaron himself,” to come “only at the time to be appointed by Jehovah, and for the purposes instituted by Him.”[@keil-leviticus] The Pulpit commentary reads it the same way.[@pulpit-leviticus] The Cambridge commentary says only that the warning “that he die not” is the law’s general formula and does not itself point to the story.[@cambridge-leviticus] What joins the chapters is 16:1, which dates the law by the deaths and names what the sons did, “when they offered,” and the Church’s footnotes join 16:1 to 10:1.[@lds-scriptures] The note on [[Lev. 16:2]] discusses the veil.

What is given is one man, one day, one way. The high priest alone goes within the veil, “and there shall be no man in the tabernacle of the congregation when he goeth in” (16:17). He makes atonement for the holy place “because of the uncleanness of the children of Israel, and because of their transgressions in all their sins” (16:16). The people, who never enter, are told, “on that day shall the priest make an atonement for you, to cleanse you, that ye may be clean from all your sins before the LORD” (16:30), “once a year” (16:34). *Scripture Helps*: the day “was the only occasion when the high priest could enter the Holy of Holies.”[@scripture-helps-ex-35] *Come, Follow Me* says what the day did for the congregation outside: “This allowed God’s presence to remain among the people,” and of the goats, “Sin must not be allowed in God’s presence. But rather than destroy or drive out the sinners, God provided another way—a goat would be killed or driven out instead.”[@cfm-ot-2026-tabernacle] I read the manual’s “destroy” as the fire of 10:2, the answer the book has already shown to a sin committed in the LORD’s presence; the manual names no chapter. The day gives the people what the two priests could not take: they are made “clean from all your sins before the LORD” (16:30) without coming in, and God’s presence remains among them.

The near ones remain under the rule. “No man that hath a blemish of the seed of Aaron the priest shall come nigh to offer the offerings of the LORD made by fire,” nor may he “go in unto the veil, nor come nigh unto the altar … that he profane not my sanctuaries” (21:21, 23); the verb for coming near in these two verses is a second Hebrew verb, *nāgaš*.[@oshb-wlc] A priest who “goeth unto the holy things … having his uncleanness upon him, that soul shall be cut off from my presence” (22:3). Numbers states the limit for everyone else: “the stranger that cometh nigh shall be put to death” (Num. 18:7).[@lds-scriptures] Jennifer Lane reads the whole arrangement as a lesson in the holiness of the presence: the law set “strict boundaries about who could officiate and approach the presence of God,” and “the symbolism of that which is imperfect being separated from the presence of the Lord” was not its last word.[@lane-presence-panim]

## “The way into the holiest of all was not yet made manifest”

Hebrews reads Leviticus 16 as a sign. “Into the second went the high priest alone once every year, not without blood … The Holy Ghost this signifying, that the way into the holiest of all was not yet made manifest, while as the first tabernacle was yet standing” (Heb. 9:7–8). Then: “Neither by the blood of goats and calves, but by his own blood he entered in once into the holy place, having obtained eternal redemption for us” (Heb. 9:12). The Church’s footnotes run both ways between Leviticus 16:2 and Hebrews 9:12 and 10:19.[@lds-scriptures] The Cambridge commentary on Hebrews: “Entrance into the Holiest symbolised direct access to God, and the ‘way’ into it had not been made evident until He came.”[@cambridge-hebrews] The reading is Hebrews’s own, and the Church’s sources follow it. *Scripture Helps*: “the high priest who took the sacrificial blood through the veil foreshadowed Jesus Christ, the ‘great high priest’ who passed through the veil to make intercession for us through His Atonement,” and “Just as the veil of the temple gave the high priest access to the Holy of Holies, the Atonement of Jesus Christ gives us access to God’s presence.”[@scripture-helps-ex-35][@scripture-helps-nt-heb-7-13] *Come, Follow Me*: “God wants all of His children to enter into His presence … But our sins prevent us from obtaining that blessing, for ‘no unclean thing can dwell with God’ (1 Nephi 10:21). So God the Father sent Jesus Christ … He parts the veil for us.”[@cfm-ot-2026-tabernacle] The Student Manual prints Gerald Lund: “Through the shedding of his blood he became capable of entering the heavenly Holy of Holies where he offered his own blood as payment for the sins of those who would believe in him and obey his commandments.”[@ot-manual-lev-11-18]

Hebrews says the priesthood is changed and the law with it: “For the priesthood being changed, there is made of necessity a change also of the law” (Heb. 7:12). The principle this page has traced through Leviticus remains unchanged: nearness is still given, not taken. What changes is the high priest who opens the way, and who may come in by it. “Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh … Let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith” (Heb. 10:19–22). John Tanner: “In ancient Israel only the high priest could pass through the second veil into this inner sanctuary where stood the mercy seat. Christ’s atoning sacrifice has opened the sanctuary of God to all believers.”[@tanner-advocate] Elder Patrick Kearon, in the April 2024 general conference: “The veil of the temple was rent in twain when Jesus died upon the cross, symbolising that access back to the presence of the Father had been ripped wide open—to all who will turn to Him, trust Him, cast their burdens on Him, and take His yoke upon them in a covenant bond.”[@kearon-gods-intent] The way that Leviticus gave to one man is given, in these readings, to all who come by the one who consecrated it; on this page’s reading, Hebrews calls for the approach of 9:5, with what He commanded, rather than that of 10:1. This page sets Hebrews’s “let us draw near” against the two approaches of Leviticus 9 and 10. The page [Who May Enter?](/psalms/themes/who-may-enter/#forerunner) follows the forerunner through the Psalms, and [What Was Offered at Sinai](/exodus/themes/offered-at-sinai/) the nearness Israel was offered at the mountain.

For a disciple the book’s rule stands in two statements. Nearness to God is on His terms and by His way, and the strange fire, in Elder McConkie’s words, is “ordinances of their own devising.”[@ot-seminary-teacher-resource-lev-1-16] And the way He gave is wide: access to the Father, in Elder Kearon’s words, is “ripped wide open—to all who will turn to Him.”[@kearon-gods-intent]
