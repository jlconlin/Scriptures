Leviticus opens with an offering that “shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him” ([[Lev. 1:4]]) and closes its covenant with a scattered people who, far from any altar, “accept of the punishment of their iniquity” ([[Lev. 26:41]]). “Accepted” and “accept” are one Hebrew verb, *ratsah*.[@oshb-wlc] This page reads the book, from its first chapter to the end of its covenant in chapter 26, as pointing toward a change that came after Christ's sacrifice: the focus of sacrifice moved from the offering to the offerer.[@ballard-sacrifice] The reading does not say that the altar did not matter. At each step the sinner brings less and the confession stays: an animal; then what a poor man can carry, down to a handful of flour; then one goat for everyone, which the sinner does not bring himself, with the priest's confession spoken over it; then nothing but confession and a humbled heart.

What the book teaches, on this reading, is what the animal was for: when the altar is gone, the text shows what it had stood in for, a person confessing, humbled, and not cast away. The Book of Mormon names the offering. Christ “offereth himself a sacrifice for sin, to answer the ends of the law, unto all those who have a broken heart and a contrite spirit” ([[2 Ne. 2:7]]). Read so, “accepted for him” is a sentence about Christ, the offering accepted for us, and Leviticus 26:40–45 is not a loophole tacked onto the curses but the covenant's last word on how a sinner is accepted.

<h2 id="accepted">Accepted for him</h2>

The book's first law is the burnt offering:

> If his offering be a burnt sacrifice of the herd, let him offer a male without blemish: he shall offer it of his own voluntary will at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation before the LORD. And he shall put his hand upon the head of the burnt offering; and it shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him. ([[Lev. 1:3–4]])

The grammar of verse 4 sets the question the rest of the book answers. In the Hebrew the offering is the subject of “accepted,” and the man is the one it is accepted “for.”[@oshb-wlc] What is accepted is the animal; who is accepted is the man. Even “of his own voluntary will” in verse 3 is the verb's noun, *ratson*,[@oshb-wlc] which means “acceptance” as well as “will”[@bdb] (the note “Willing, and accepted” on [chapter 1](/leviticus/1/) gives both readings).

How an animal could be accepted for a man is what the hand on its head was taken to show. The laying on of hands “dedicated the animal to God and made it the sacrificer's representative and substitute.”[@bd-sacrifices] “The animal takes the place of the person in the sacrifice”; in the burnt offering “God accepts the animal as ransom for man.”[@lane-whole-meaning] “The whole offering rendered the offeror acceptable to God.”[@sc-knowhy-545] The hand has also been read as “indicating ownership,”[@gee-last-sacrifice] and the note on the hand at [[Lev. 1:4]] sets out the readings. Whatever the hand meant, the offerings as a whole “pointed Israel to Jesus Christ and His atoning sacrifice.”[@scripture-helps-ex-35]

The book's question is set: how does a man come to be accepted, and what stands in for him?

<h2 id="confess">Confess, then bring what he has</h2>

Chapter 5 adds a word that chapter 1 did not have:

> And it shall be, when he shall be guilty in one of these things, that he shall confess that he hath sinned in that thing: And he shall bring his trespass offering unto the LORD for his sin which he hath sinned, a female from the flock, a lamb or a kid of the goats, for a sin offering; and the priest shall make an atonement for him concerning his sin. ([[Lev. 5:5–6]])

“Confess” translates one form of the Hebrew verb *yadah*, and in Leviticus that form stands in three verses only: here, at 16:21, and at 26:40.[@oshb-wlc] Elsewhere the law joins the same confession to repayment: “they shall confess their sin which they have done: and he shall recompense his trespass” ([[Num. 5:7]]). Atonement could be made for “crimes that a man voluntarily confessed, and for which he made (if possible) compensation (Lev. 5:5).”[@bd-sacrifices] The hand of chapter 1 belongs to the same act: it “really pointed to the substitution of the sacrifice for the sacrificer. Hence it was always accompanied by confession of sin and prayer.”[@ot-manual-lev-1-10]

Then the law makes room for the poor. “And if he be not able to bring a lamb, then he shall bring for his trespass, which he hath committed, two turtledoves, or two young pigeons” ([[Lev. 5:7]]). “But if he be not able to bring two turtledoves, or two young pigeons, then he that sinned shall bring for his offering the tenth part of an ephah of fine flour for a sin offering; he shall put no oil upon it, neither shall he put any frankincense thereon: for it is a sin offering” ([[Lev. 5:11]]). Of the flour the verdict is the same as of the lamb: “the priest shall make an atonement for him as touching his sin that he hath sinned in one of these, and it shall be forgiven him” ([[Lev. 5:13]]). Chapter 1 had done the same for the burnt offering, letting Israelites “offer acceptable sacrifices in conditions of poverty.”[@ot-seminary-lev-57] (The note “The poor man's offering” on [chapter 5](/leviticus/5/) has the detail.)

The flour has no blood in it, and the text still says “make an atonement… and it shall be forgiven him.” Hebrews says that “almost all things are by the law purged with blood; and without shedding of blood is no remission” ([[Heb. 9:22]]). Leviticus does not say how the handful of flour fits that rule, and the two are left here as they stand.

So the offering may shrink to a handful of flour and the atonement stays whole. What never shrinks is the confession, which comes first, in verse 5, for the lamb, the birds and the flour alike. The confession does not replace the offering (verse 6 still says “he shall bring”), but the weight is already moving toward the sinner's own part.

<h2 id="one-goat">One goat for all</h2>

The second “confess” is on the Day of Atonement:[@oshb-wlc]

> And Aaron shall lay both his hands upon the head of the live goat, and confess over him all the iniquities of the children of Israel, and all their transgressions in all their sins, putting them upon the head of the goat, and shall send him away by the hand of a fit man into the wilderness: And the goat shall bear upon him all their iniquities unto a land not inhabited: and he shall let go the goat in the wilderness. ([[Lev. 16:21–22]])

The confession of verse 21 is tied to Hebrews' “remembrance again made of sins every year” ([[Heb. 10:3]]), and the goat that bears “all their iniquities” is listed among the figures that anticipate Jesus Christ.[@lds-scriptures] The scapegoat “can symbolize Jesus Christ, who has ‘borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows.’”[@scripture-helps-ex-35] “The scapegoat and the altar victim of ancient Israel, if offered with repentance and contrition, were accepted by the Lord in mitigation of the sins of the people.”[@ot-manual-lev-11-18] The people still had their own part: “ye shall afflict your souls” ([[Lev. 16:29]]). (The rest of the day is in the notes on [chapter 16](/leviticus/16/) and in [Them That Come Nigh Me](/leviticus/themes/them-that-come-nigh-me/#once-a-year).)

The page reads 16:21 against 5:5. At 5:5 the sinner confesses his own sin, “that he hath sinned in that thing,” and brings his own lamb, or birds, or flour. At 16:21 the one who confesses is Aaron, the sins are “all the iniquities of the children of Israel,” and there is one animal for all of them, with the confession spoken over its head. Read so, the book has already drawn, in the middle of the law, the shape it will give to Christ: one bearer for everyone, and the confession of everyone spoken over Him.

<h2 id="no-altar">No altar</h2>

Then Leviticus 26 takes the altar away. If Israel “shall despise my statutes, or if your soul abhor my judgments” ([[Lev. 26:15]]), the end of the curses is this: “And I will make your cities waste, and bring your sanctuaries unto desolation, and I will not smell the savour of your sweet odours” ([[Lev. 26:31]]); “I will scatter you among the heathen” ([[Lev. 26:33]]).

“Sweet odours” is *nichoach*, the word the book has used for the offerings' “sweet savour” in sixteen verses before this one, beginning with the burnt offering at 1:9.[@oshb-wlc] The LORD's refusal to smell is a refusal to accept: “I will not accept your burnt offerings.”[@lds-scriptures] Israel's part is named in two verbs, “despise” (*maʾas*) and “abhor” (*gaʿal*), at 26:15 and again at 26:43.[@oshb-wlc]

So the book takes away the means it began with. After 26:31 there is no sanctuary and no altar on which anything can be “accepted for him.” The question of chapter 1, how a man is accepted and what stands in for him, is now the exiles' question, and they have nothing to bring.

<h2 id="punishment">Accept of the punishment</h2>

The chapter's answer is the third “confess”:[@oshb-wlc]

> If they shall confess their iniquity, and the iniquity of their fathers, with their trespass which they trespassed against me, and that also they have walked contrary unto me; And that I also have walked contrary unto them, and have brought them into the land of their enemies; if then their uncircumcised hearts be humbled, and they then accept of the punishment of their iniquity: Then will I remember my covenant with Jacob, and also my covenant with Isaac, and also my covenant with Abraham will I remember; and I will remember the land. ([[Lev. 26:40–42]])

“Humbled” (*kanaʿ*) stands nowhere else in Leviticus,[@oshb-wlc] and the humbled heart is classed with the contrite heart.[@lds-scriptures] “Accept” is *ratsah*, the verb of 1:4,[@oshb-wlc] but here it has another sense: “make acceptable, satisfy (by paying off debt).” It is the sense of the land that “enjoys” its sabbaths at 26:34 and 43, “paying off its sabbaths,” and of the guilt in 26:41 and 43.[@bdb] The same sense is in Isaiah's word to exiled Jerusalem that “her iniquity is pardoned” ([[Isa. 40:2]]): “her punishment is accepted (as satisfactory).”[@bdb] Not every reader takes it so. Another reading takes these verses as “accept, be satisfied with [in payment]”;[@bdb] on one older reading the exiles “take pleasure” in what their sins have brought, since it has humbled them: “We have just what we deserve.”[@keil-leviticus] (The note “Accept of the punishment” on [chapter 26](/leviticus/26/) gives the verse alone.)

The setting is exile without a temple, and later scripture fills it in. Solomon prays for captives who “repent, and make supplication unto thee in the land of them that carried them captives” ([[1 Kgs. 8:47]]), a prayer cross-referenced at 26:44.[@lds-scriptures] Daniel in Babylon “made my confession” ([[Dan. 9:4]]), in the verb of 26:40,[@bdb] and says, “As it is written in the law of Moses, all this evil is come upon us” ([[Dan. 9:13]]), a line referred to Leviticus 26:14–46[@lds-scriptures] (see the notes on [Daniel 9](/daniel/9/)). The curses end, “conditioned upon repentance, for those who are suffering in sin and exile.”[@ot-minute-lev]

And the LORD's answer turns Israel's own verbs. “And yet for all that, when they be in the land of their enemies, I will not cast them away, neither will I abhor them, to destroy them utterly, and to break my covenant with them: for I am the LORD their God” ([[Lev. 26:44]]). The verbs are *maʾas* and *gaʿal*, the despising and abhorring of 26:15 and 43 (“their soul abhorred my statutes”), now with the LORD as subject and both negated.[@oshb-wlc] The chapter has used *gaʿal* of the LORD twice before: “my soul shall not abhor you” ([[Lev. 26:11]]) and “my soul shall abhor you” ([[Lev. 26:30]]).[@oshb-wlc] The promise, the threat, and then, in the land of their enemies, the promise again.

Read together, 1:4 and 26:41 use one verb in two senses, and the second sense is the point. At the altar a substitute was brought, the substitute was accepted, and the man was accepted for its sake. In exile there is nothing to bring, and the same verb is used of a people who, confessing and humbled, accept, or on the lexicon's reading pay off, the punishment of their iniquity. The LORD's answer is not to accept an offering, because there is none. It is to remember His covenant and to refuse to abhor the people who had abhorred His law. The page does not claim that Moses meant the two verses to answer each other.

One verse outside Leviticus shows where this goes. To scattered Israel, Ezekiel hears the LORD say, “I will accept you with your sweet savour, when I bring you out from the people, and gather you out of the countries wherein ye have been scattered” ([[Ezek. 20:41]]). The Hebrew has *ratsah*, the verb of 1:4, and *nichoach*, the “sweet savour” of 1:9 and the “sweet odours” refused at 26:31.[@oshb-wlc] Its object is “you,” the people themselves.[@bdb] The page takes this as the next step of the same movement: to the exiles, the person has become what is accepted, and the savour that was refused is promised again.

<h2 id="heart">The offering and the heart</h2>

If the person is what is accepted, Leviticus 26 still leaves a question open: who answers the punishment the exiles accepted? The law's offerings could not finally pay it. They were “the whole meaning of the law, every whit pointing to that great and last sacrifice; and that great and last sacrifice will be the Son of God” ([[Alma 34:14]]). “It is not possible that the blood of bulls and of goats should take away sins” ([[Heb. 10:4]]), but “we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” ([[Heb. 10:10]]). The substitute the animal pictured is Christ: “He stood in our place, gave His life to pay the penalty of sin.”[@cfm-ot-2026-tabernacle]

Lehi puts the two parts in one sentence:

> Behold, he offereth himself a sacrifice for sin, to answer the ends of the law, unto all those who have a broken heart and a contrite spirit; and unto none else can the ends of the law be answered. ([[2 Ne. 2:7]])

Three verses later Lehi joins “the ends of the law” to “the inflicting of the punishment which is affixed” ([[2 Ne. 2:10]]). The ends of the law are read as “punishment,” and Christ answered them because “He suffered our punishment for breaking the law”;[@bom-teacher-resource-2ne] the phrase “refers to the consequences of the Fall and to the ‘punishment that is affixed’ for disobedience.”[@bom-gospel-doctrine-lesson-6] After His sacrifice, the Lord's voice to the Nephites ended the altar and kept the heart: “your sacrifices and your burnt offerings shall be done away, for I will accept none of your sacrifices and your burnt offerings. And ye shall offer for a sacrifice unto me a broken heart and a contrite spirit” ([[3 Ne. 9:19–20]]).

The heart was asked for under the law as well. “Thou delightest not in burnt offering” ([[Ps. 51:16]]) is *ratsah* again,[@oshb-wlc] and the next verse names what God does accept: “The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart” ([[Ps. 51:17]]). David's words show “that burnt offerings alone were not enough”;[@porter-broken-heart] “after the internal humility had been demonstrated, then the outward symbol of blood sacrifice would be efficacious.”[@seely-sacrifice] (Psalms' [The Sacrifice God Desires](/psalms/themes/the-sacrifice-god-desires/#broken-heart) follows that psalm.) After Christ, “the Atonement of Christ is the offering, and we put up a broken heart and a contrite spirit.”[@gee-last-sacrifice] After the Savior's sacrifice, the sacrament took the place of sacrifice, and the focus of sacrifice moved from a person's animal to the person[@ballard-sacrifice] (in words quoted in the Witness of Christ on [chapter 1](/leviticus/1/)). What this page finds in Leviticus points toward that change.

The page reads Lehi beside Leviticus 26. With no altar, the exiles could confess and, humbled, accept the punishment of their iniquity. Lehi says Christ answers the punishment the law affixes, for those with a broken heart and a contrite spirit. Read together, the punishment the exiles accepted is what Lehi says Christ answers for the broken-hearted. The sinner's part is still what Leviticus 26 left it, confession and a humbled heart. The substitute's part, which the animal carried in chapter 1 and the exiles had no animal to carry, is Christ's. Read that way, “accepted for him” was always about Him: He is the offering accepted for us.

<h2 id="broken-heart">A broken heart and a contrite spirit</h2>

A disciple's offering is the one the exiles were left with. The command stands: “Thou shalt offer a sacrifice unto the Lord thy God in righteousness, even that of a broken heart and a contrite spirit” ([[D&C 59:8]]). “To have a broken heart is to be humble, contrite, repentant, and meek—that is, receptive to the will of God.”[@gs-broken-heart] The gift He accepts now “is the gift of yourself—what you are and what you are becoming.”[@christofferson-converted]

So a disciple brings to the sacrament table what Leviticus 26 left the exiles holding, a confession and a humbled heart; the offering accepted for him is Christ's.[@bd-sacrifices] Those with a broken heart “are willing to do anything and everything that God asks of them”; “in such a condition of submissiveness, the Atonement can take effect and true repentance can occur.”[@porter-broken-heart] And the law's provision for the poor (5:11) and its last word to the scattered (26:41, 44) agree on one thing: a sinner who has little or nothing to bring is not for that reason refused. “I will not cast them away, neither will I abhor them” ([[Lev. 26:44]]).
