Leviticus opens with an offering that “shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him” ([[Lev. 1:4]]). Twenty-six chapters later, with the sanctuary desolate and the people scattered, it ends its covenant with exiles who “accept of the punishment of their iniquity” ([[Lev. 26:41]]). “Accepted” and “accept” are one Hebrew verb, *ratsah*.[@oshb-wlc] I want to follow that verb from the first chapter to the last, because what happens between the two verses is, I think, the book's own teaching on how a sinner comes to be accepted, and it is not the teaching one expects from a manual of sacrifice. At each step the thing the sinner brings grows smaller and the confession stays. An animal; then what a poor man can carry, down to a handful of flour; then one goat for all Israel, with the priest's confession spoken over it; then nothing but a confession and a humbled heart. When the altar is finally gone, the text shows what the animal had been standing in for all along. Lehi names the offering that answers for such a person, and it is not an animal.

## What is accepted, and for whom

The book's first law is the burnt offering:

> If his offering be a burnt sacrifice of the herd, let him offer a male without blemish: he shall offer it of his own voluntary will at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation before the LORD. And he shall put his hand upon the head of the burnt offering; and it shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him. ([[Lev. 1:3–4]])

The grammar of verse 4 sets the question the rest of the book will answer. In the Hebrew the offering is the subject of “accepted” and the man is the one it is accepted “for.”[@oshb-wlc] What is accepted is the animal; who is accepted is the man. Even “of his own voluntary will” in verse 3 is the same verb's noun, *ratson*,[@oshb-wlc] which the lexicon glosses “acceptance” as readily as “will”[@bdb] (the note “Willing, and accepted” on [chapter 1](/leviticus/1/) gives both readings). From its third verse the book is talking about acceptance, and acceptance by way of something brought.

How an animal could be accepted for a man is what the hand on its head was taken to show. The laying on of hands “dedicated the animal to God and made it the sacrificer's representative and substitute.”[@bd-sacrifices] “The animal takes the place of the person in the sacrifice”; in the burnt offering “God accepts the animal as ransom for man.”[@lane-whole-meaning] “The whole offering rendered the offeror acceptable to God.”[@sc-knowhy-545] Gee reads the hand as also “indicating ownership,”[@gee-last-sacrifice] and the note on [[Lev. 1:4]] sets out the readings; but on any of them the offerings as a whole “pointed Israel to Jesus Christ and His atoning sacrifice.”[@scripture-helps-ex-35] So the question is set: how does a man come to be accepted, and what stands in for him?

## The poor man’s flour

Chapter 5 adds a word chapter 1 did not have:

> And it shall be, when he shall be guilty in one of these things, that he shall confess that he hath sinned in that thing: And he shall bring his trespass offering unto the LORD for his sin which he hath sinned, a female from the flock, a lamb or a kid of the goats, for a sin offering; and the priest shall make an atonement for him concerning his sin. ([[Lev. 5:5–6]])

“Confess” translates a form of the verb *yadah*, and in Leviticus that form occurs in exactly three verses: here, at 16:21, and at 26:40.[@oshb-wlc] Those three verses are the spine of this essay. Elsewhere the law ties the same confession to restitution, “they shall confess their sin which they have done: and he shall recompense his trespass” ([[Num. 5:7]]), and the Bible Dictionary notes that atonement could be made for “crimes that a man voluntarily confessed, and for which he made (if possible) compensation.”[@bd-sacrifices] The hand of chapter 1 belongs to the same act: it “really pointed to the substitution of the sacrifice for the sacrificer. Hence it was always accompanied by confession of sin and prayer.”[@ot-manual-lev-1-10]

Then the law does something a reader of chapter 1 would not predict. It makes room for the poor, and it keeps lowering the price. “And if he be not able to bring a lamb, then he shall bring for his trespass, which he hath committed, two turtledoves, or two young pigeons” ([[Lev. 5:7]]). “But if he be not able to bring two turtledoves, or two young pigeons, then he that sinned shall bring for his offering the tenth part of an ephah of fine flour for a sin offering; he shall put no oil upon it, neither shall he put any frankincense thereon: for it is a sin offering” ([[Lev. 5:11]]). And the verdict over the flour is the verdict over the lamb: “the priest shall make an atonement for him as touching his sin that he hath sinned in one of these, and it shall be forgiven him” ([[Lev. 5:13]]). Chapter 1 had already done this for the burnt offering, letting Israelites “offer acceptable sacrifices in conditions of poverty.”[@ot-seminary-lev-57] (The note “The poor man's offering” on [chapter 5](/leviticus/5/) has the detail.)

I should say plainly what the text does not resolve. The flour has no blood in it, and the law still says “make an atonement… and it shall be forgiven him,” while Hebrews says that “without shedding of blood is no remission” ([[Heb. 9:22]]). Leviticus does not say how the handful of flour fits that rule, and I leave the two as they stand.

What I do take from chapter 5 is this. The offering may shrink to a handful of flour and the atonement stays whole. What never shrinks is the confession, which comes first, in verse 5, for the lamb, the birds and the flour alike. Confession does not replace the offering; verse 6 still says “he shall bring.” But the weight has already begun to move toward the sinner's own part.

## One goat

The second “confess” is on the Day of Atonement:[@oshb-wlc]

> And Aaron shall lay both his hands upon the head of the live goat, and confess over him all the iniquities of the children of Israel, and all their transgressions in all their sins, putting them upon the head of the goat, and shall send him away by the hand of a fit man into the wilderness: And the goat shall bear upon him all their iniquities unto a land not inhabited: and he shall let go the goat in the wilderness. ([[Lev. 16:21–22]])

Compare this with chapter 5. There the sinner confesses his own sin, “that he hath sinned in that thing,” and brings his own lamb, or birds, or flour. Here the one confessing is Aaron, the sins are “all the iniquities of the children of Israel,” and there is one animal for all of them, with the confession of everyone spoken over its head. No individual brings this goat. The Church's edition ties the confession of verse 21 to Hebrews' “remembrance again made of sins every year” ([[Heb. 10:3]]) and lists the goat that bears “all their iniquities” among the figures that anticipate Jesus Christ;[@lds-scriptures] the scapegoat “can symbolize Jesus Christ, who has ‘borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows.’”[@scripture-helps-ex-35] Talmage adds the condition: “The scapegoat and the altar victim of ancient Israel, if offered with repentance and contrition, were accepted by the Lord in mitigation of the sins of the people.”[@ot-manual-lev-11-18] The people's own part on that day was not an animal at all: “ye shall afflict your souls” ([[Lev. 16:29]]). (The rest of the day is in the notes on [chapter 16](/leviticus/16/) and in [Them That Come Nigh Me](/leviticus/themes/them-that-come-nigh-me/#once-a-year).)

So by the middle of the law the book has already drawn the shape it will give to Christ: one bearer for everyone, and the confession of everyone spoken over him. No source I know compares 5:5 with 16:21, and the comparison is mine; but the three verses with “confess” in them are the text's, and this is the second.

## No altar

Then Leviticus 26 takes the altar away. If Israel “shall despise my statutes, or if your soul abhor my judgments” ([[Lev. 26:15]]), the curses run to this: “And I will make your cities waste, and bring your sanctuaries unto desolation, and I will not smell the savour of your sweet odours” ([[Lev. 26:31]]); “I will scatter you among the heathen” ([[Lev. 26:33]]).

“Sweet odours” is *nichoach*, the word the book has used for the offerings' “sweet savour” sixteen times before this verse, beginning with the burnt offering at 1:9.[@oshb-wlc] The refusal to smell is a refusal to accept, and the Church's edition glosses it exactly so: “I will not accept your burnt offerings.”[@lds-scriptures] Note also the two verbs the chapter gives Israel, “despise” (*maʾas*) and “abhor” (*gaʿal*), at 26:15 and again at 26:43;[@oshb-wlc] we will meet them once more.

The book has now removed the thing it began with. After 26:31 there is no sanctuary and no altar on which anything can be “accepted for him.” The question of chapter 1, how a man is accepted and what stands in for him, is now the exiles' question, and they have nothing to bring.

## Paying the debt

The chapter's answer is the third “confess”:[@oshb-wlc]

> If they shall confess their iniquity, and the iniquity of their fathers, with their trespass which they trespassed against me, and that also they have walked contrary unto me; And that I also have walked contrary unto them, and have brought them into the land of their enemies; if then their uncircumcised hearts be humbled, and they then accept of the punishment of their iniquity: Then will I remember my covenant with Jacob, and also my covenant with Isaac, and also my covenant with Abraham will I remember; and I will remember the land. ([[Lev. 26:40–42]])

Three things in this passage deserve a close look. “Humbled,” *kanaʿ*, occurs nowhere else in Leviticus,[@oshb-wlc] and the Church's footnote files the humbled heart under the contrite heart.[@lds-scriptures] “Accept” is *ratsah*, the verb of 1:4,[@oshb-wlc] but the lexicon gives it a different sense here: “make acceptable, satisfy (by paying off debt).” It is the sense in which the land “enjoys” its sabbaths at 26:34 and 43, paying off the sabbaths it was denied, and of the guilt in 26:41 and 43.[@bdb] Isaiah uses the same sense of exiled Jerusalem, “her iniquity is pardoned” ([[Isa. 40:2]]), which the lexicon renders “her punishment is accepted (as satisfactory).”[@bdb] Not every reader takes it quite so: the lexicon also allows “accept, be satisfied with [in payment],”[@bdb] and Keil has the exiles “take pleasure” in what their sins have brought, since it has humbled them, as if to say “We have just what we deserve.”[@keil-leviticus] (The note “Accept of the punishment” on [chapter 26](/leviticus/26/) treats the verse on its own.) The lexicon and Keil each treat 26:41 by itself; neither mentions 1:4. The join is mine.

The setting is exile without a temple, and later scripture fills in what that looked like. Solomon prays for captives who “repent, and make supplication unto thee in the land of them that carried them captives” ([[1 Kgs. 8:47]]), a prayer the Church's edition cross-references at 26:44.[@lds-scriptures] Daniel in Babylon “made my confession” ([[Dan. 9:4]]), in the verb of 26:40,[@bdb] and says, “As it is written in the law of Moses, all this evil is come upon us” ([[Dan. 9:13]]), a line the footnote sends to Leviticus 26:14–46[@lds-scriptures] (see the notes on [Daniel 9](/daniel/9/)). The curses, as Welch puts it, end “conditioned upon repentance, for those who are suffering in sin and exile.”[@ot-minute-lev]

And then the LORD answers in Israel's own verbs. “And yet for all that, when they be in the land of their enemies, I will not cast them away, neither will I abhor them, to destroy them utterly, and to break my covenant with them: for I am the LORD their God” ([[Lev. 26:44]]). “Cast away” and “abhor” are *maʾas* and *gaʿal*, the despising and abhorring of 26:15 and 43, now with the LORD as their subject and both of them negated.[@oshb-wlc] The chapter had used *gaʿal* of the LORD twice before, in the promise “my soul shall not abhor you” ([[Lev. 26:11]]) and the threat “my soul shall abhor you” ([[Lev. 26:30]]);[@oshb-wlc] now, in the land of their enemies, the promise returns.

Set 1:4 and 26:41 side by side, then, and read the verb in both senses. At the altar a substitute was brought, the substitute was accepted, and the man was accepted for its sake. In exile there is nothing to bring, and the same verb describes a people who, confessing and humbled, accept, or in the lexicon's phrase pay off, the punishment of their iniquity. The LORD's answer is not to accept an offering, because there is none. It is to remember His covenant and to refuse to abhor the people who had abhorred His law. Whether Moses meant the two verses to answer each other I cannot say. That the book's first word about acceptance and its last are the same word, with the offering present in one and absent in the other, is simply there in the text.

One verse outside Leviticus shows where this goes. To scattered Israel, Ezekiel hears the LORD say, “I will accept you with your sweet savour, when I bring you out from the people, and gather you out of the countries wherein ye have been scattered” ([[Ezek. 20:41]]). The verb is *ratsah*, the verb of 1:4; the noun is *nichoach*, the “sweet savour” of 1:9 and the “sweet odours” refused at 26:31;[@oshb-wlc] and the object is “you.”[@bdb] To the exiles, the person has become what is accepted, and the savour that was refused is promised again.

## Who pays

If the person is what is accepted, Leviticus 26 still leaves one question open: who answers the punishment the exiles accepted? The law's offerings could not finally do it. They were “the whole meaning of the law, every whit pointing to that great and last sacrifice; and that great and last sacrifice will be the Son of God” ([[Alma 34:14]]). “It is not possible that the blood of bulls and of goats should take away sins” ([[Heb. 10:4]]), but “we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” ([[Heb. 10:10]]). The substitute the animal pictured is Christ: “He stood in our place, gave His life to pay the penalty of sin.”[@cfm-ot-2026-tabernacle]

Lehi puts the two parts of the matter in one sentence:

> Behold, he offereth himself a sacrifice for sin, to answer the ends of the law, unto all those who have a broken heart and a contrite spirit; and unto none else can the ends of the law be answered. ([[2 Ne. 2:7]])

Three verses later he joins “the ends of the law” to “the inflicting of the punishment which is affixed” ([[2 Ne. 2:10]]), and the Church's manuals read the phrase that way: the ends of the law are “punishment,” and Christ answered them because “He suffered our punishment for breaking the law”;[@bom-teacher-resource-2ne] the phrase “refers to the consequences of the Fall and to the ‘punishment that is affixed’ for disobedience.”[@bom-gospel-doctrine-lesson-6] After His sacrifice the Lord's own voice to the Nephites ended the altar and kept the heart: “your sacrifices and your burnt offerings shall be done away, for I will accept none of your sacrifices and your burnt offerings. And ye shall offer for a sacrifice unto me a broken heart and a contrite spirit” ([[3 Ne. 9:19–20]]).

Now put Lehi beside Leviticus 26. With no altar, the exiles could confess and, humbled, accept the punishment of their iniquity. Lehi says Christ answers the punishment the law affixes, and does it for those with a broken heart and a contrite spirit. The punishment the exiles could only accept is the punishment Lehi says Christ answers. The sinner's part is exactly what Leviticus 26 left it, a confession and a humbled heart; the substitute's part, which the animal carried in chapter 1 and the exiles had no animal to carry, is Christ's. “Accepted for him,” read that way, was always about Him. He is the offering accepted for us. No source joins Leviticus 26:41 to 2 Nephi 2:7, and I do not claim Lehi had Leviticus in mind; I claim only that the two texts describe the same transaction from its two ends.

The heart was asked for under the law as well, and the same verb says so. “Thou delightest not in burnt offering” ([[Ps. 51:16]]) is *ratsah* once more,[@oshb-wlc] and the next verse names what God does accept: “The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart” ([[Ps. 51:17]]). David's words show “that burnt offerings alone were not enough”;[@porter-broken-heart] “after the internal humility had been demonstrated, then the outward symbol of blood sacrifice would be efficacious.”[@seely-sacrifice] (Psalms' [The Sacrifice God Desires](/psalms/themes/the-sacrifice-god-desires/#broken-heart) follows that psalm.) After Christ, as Gee puts it, “the Atonement of Christ is the offering, and we put up a broken heart and a contrite spirit.”[@gee-last-sacrifice] President Ballard dates the change to the Savior's sacrifice, after which the sacrament took the place of sacrifice and the focus moved from a person's animal to the person[@ballard-sacrifice] (the Witness of Christ on [chapter 1](/leviticus/1/) quotes him). What I have found in Leviticus is that the book was already pointing there.

## What we bring

The command stands: “Thou shalt offer a sacrifice unto the Lord thy God in righteousness, even that of a broken heart and a contrite spirit” ([[D&C 59:8]]). “To have a broken heart is to be humble, contrite, repentant, and meek—that is, receptive to the will of God.”[@gs-broken-heart] The gift He accepts now “is the gift of yourself—what you are and what you are becoming.”[@christofferson-converted]

So what a disciple brings to the sacrament table is what Leviticus 26 left the exiles holding, a confession and a humbled heart, and the offering accepted for him is Christ's.[@bd-sacrifices] Those with a broken heart “are willing to do anything and everything that God asks of them”; “in such a condition of submissiveness, the Atonement can take effect and true repentance can occur.”[@porter-broken-heart] And the law's provision for the poor and its last word to the scattered agree on one thing: a sinner who has little or nothing to bring is not for that reason refused. “I will not cast them away, neither will I abhor them” ([[Lev. 26:44]]).
