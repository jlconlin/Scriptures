Leviticus begins with an offering. A man brings an animal to the door of the tabernacle, lays his hand on its head, and the text says that “it shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him” ([[Lev. 1:4]]). Twenty-six chapters later the book ends its covenant in a very different scene. The sanctuary is in ruins, the people are scattered among their enemies, and there is no altar anywhere. What the text says of them is that they “accept of the punishment of their iniquity” ([[Lev. 26:41]]).

“Accepted” in the first verse and “accept” in the last are the same Hebrew verb.[@oshb-wlc] That is the thread this essay follows. I think the distance between those two verses is the book's own teaching on how a sinner comes to be accepted, and it is not the teaching one expects from a manual of sacrifice.

Here is the shape of it. At each step the thing the sinner brings grows smaller, and the confession stays. First an animal. Then whatever a poor man can afford, down to a handful of flour. Then a single goat for all Israel, with the priest's confession spoken over it. Then, in exile, nothing at all but a confession and a humbled heart. When the altar is finally gone, the text shows what the animal had been standing in for all along. And the Book of Mormon names the offering that answers for a person who has nothing to bring.

## What is accepted, and for whom

The book's first law is the burnt offering:

> If his offering be a burnt sacrifice of the herd, let him offer a male without blemish: he shall offer it of his own voluntary will at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation before the LORD. And he shall put his hand upon the head of the burnt offering; and it shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him. ([[Lev. 1:3–4]])

Look at the grammar of that last sentence. In the Hebrew, the offering is the subject of “accepted,” and the man is the one it is accepted “for.”[@oshb-wlc] What is accepted is the animal. Who is accepted is the man. Even the phrase “of his own voluntary will,” a verse earlier, is built on the same verb; its noun can mean “acceptance” as readily as “will.”[@bdb] From its third verse, then, Leviticus is a book about acceptance, and about acceptance by way of something brought. (The note “Willing, and accepted” on [chapter 1](/leviticus/1/) gives both readings of the phrase.)

How could an animal be accepted for a man? That is what the hand on its head was understood to show. The Bible Dictionary says the laying on of hands “dedicated the animal to God and made it the sacrificer's representative and substitute.”[@bd-sacrifices] A Latter-day Saint scholar puts it more simply: “The animal takes the place of the person in the sacrifice,” and in the burnt offering “God accepts the animal as ransom for man.”[@lane-whole-meaning] The result was that “the whole offering rendered the offeror acceptable to God.”[@sc-knowhy-545] Not every reader takes the hand the same way; it has also been read as a mark of ownership,[@gee-last-sacrifice] and the note on [[Lev. 1:4]] sets the readings out. But on any reading, the offerings “pointed Israel to Jesus Christ and His atoning sacrifice.”[@scripture-helps-ex-35]

So the question the book opens with is this. How does a man come to be accepted, and what stands in for him? Chapter 1 answers with an animal. The rest of the book keeps asking.

## The poor man’s flour

Chapter 5 adds a word that chapter 1 did not have:

> And it shall be, when he shall be guilty in one of these things, that he shall confess that he hath sinned in that thing: And he shall bring his trespass offering unto the LORD for his sin which he hath sinned, a female from the flock, a lamb or a kid of the goats, for a sin offering; and the priest shall make an atonement for him concerning his sin. ([[Lev. 5:5–6]])

The new word is “confess.” It translates a form of the Hebrew verb *yadah*, and in all of Leviticus that form occurs in exactly three verses: here, at 16:21, and at 26:40.[@oshb-wlc] Those three verses are the spine of this essay, and this is the first of them. Here the confession belongs to the sinner. He says what he did, and then he brings his animal. The Bible Dictionary notes that atonement could be made for “crimes that a man voluntarily confessed, and for which he made (if possible) compensation.”[@bd-sacrifices] The Student Manual adds that the hand on the animal's head, which we met in chapter 1, “was always accompanied by confession of sin and prayer.”[@ot-manual-lev-1-10] Confession and offering go together.

Then the law does something a reader of chapter 1 would not predict. It makes room for the poor, and it keeps lowering the price. If a man cannot bring a lamb, he may bring two turtledoves or two young pigeons ([[Lev. 5:7]]). If he cannot bring the birds, he may bring “the tenth part of an ephah of fine flour,” with no oil and no frankincense on it, “for it is a sin offering” ([[Lev. 5:11]]). And the verdict pronounced over the flour is the same as the verdict over the lamb: “the priest shall make an atonement for him as touching his sin that he hath sinned in one of these, and it shall be forgiven him” ([[Lev. 5:13]]). The Seminary manual observes that chapter 1 had already done this for the burnt offering, so that Israelites could “offer acceptable sacrifices in conditions of poverty.”[@ot-seminary-lev-57] (The note “The poor man's offering” on [chapter 5](/leviticus/5/) has the detail.)

I should say plainly what the text leaves unresolved. The flour has no blood in it, and Leviticus still says “make an atonement… and it shall be forgiven him,” while Hebrews says that “without shedding of blood is no remission” ([[Heb. 9:22]]). The law does not explain how a handful of flour fits that rule. I leave the two where they stand.

What chapter 5 shows, then, is that the offering may shrink a long way and the atonement stays whole. The one thing that never shrinks is the confession. It comes first, in verse 5, for the lamb and the birds and the flour alike. Confession has not replaced the offering; verse 6 still says “he shall bring.” But the weight has begun to shift toward the sinner's own part.

## One goat

The second “confess” comes on the Day of Atonement:[@oshb-wlc]

> And Aaron shall lay both his hands upon the head of the live goat, and confess over him all the iniquities of the children of Israel, and all their transgressions in all their sins, putting them upon the head of the goat, and shall send him away by the hand of a fit man into the wilderness: And the goat shall bear upon him all their iniquities unto a land not inhabited: and he shall let go the goat in the wilderness. ([[Lev. 16:21–22]])

Set this beside chapter 5 and everything has changed scale. There the sinner confessed his own sin, “that he hath sinned in that thing,” and brought his own lamb or birds or flour. Here the one confessing is Aaron. The sins are “all the iniquities of the children of Israel.” And there is one animal for all of them, with the confession of the whole nation spoken over its head. No individual Israelite brings this goat. His part that day is not an animal at all: “ye shall afflict your souls” ([[Lev. 16:29]]).

The Church's edition reads this scene toward Christ. Its footnote at “confess” points to Hebrews' “remembrance again made of sins every year” ([[Heb. 10:3]]), and its footnote at “iniquities” files the goat among the figures that anticipate Jesus Christ.[@lds-scriptures] *Scripture Helps* says the scapegoat “can symbolize Jesus Christ, who has ‘borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows.’”[@scripture-helps-ex-35] And Talmage, quoted in the Student Manual, adds the condition on the people's side: the goats “if offered with repentance and contrition, were accepted by the Lord in mitigation of the sins of the people.”[@ot-manual-lev-11-18] (The rest of the day is in the notes on [chapter 16](/leviticus/16/) and in the essay [Them That Come Nigh Me](/leviticus/themes/them-that-come-nigh-me/#once-a-year).)

Here is what I take from the second confession. By the middle of the law, the book has already drawn the shape it will later give to Christ: one bearer for everyone, with the confession of everyone spoken over him. I know of no commentary that compares 5:5 with 16:21, and the comparison is mine. But the three verses with “confess” in them are the text's own, and this is the second of them.

## No altar

Then Leviticus 26 takes the altar away.

The chapter sets out blessings for obedience and curses for rebellion, and the curses grow worse in stages. If Israel “shall despise my statutes, or if your soul abhor my judgments” ([[Lev. 26:15]]), the end of it will be this: “I will make your cities waste, and bring your sanctuaries unto desolation, and I will not smell the savour of your sweet odours” ([[Lev. 26:31]]), and then, “I will scatter you among the heathen” ([[Lev. 26:33]]).

“Sweet odours” is a word we have met before. It is *nichoach*, the “sweet savour” of the offerings, which the book has used sixteen times before this verse, beginning with the burnt offering of chapter 1.[@oshb-wlc] So the Lord's refusal to smell is a refusal to accept, and the Church's edition glosses the line in exactly those terms: “I will not accept your burnt offerings.”[@lds-scriptures] Keep in mind, too, the two verbs the chapter gives to Israel here, “despise” and “abhor,” at verse 15 and again at verse 43.[@oshb-wlc] We will meet them once more, with a different subject.

The book has now removed the thing it started with. After verse 31 there is no sanctuary and no altar on which anything can be “accepted for him.” The question of chapter 1, how a man is accepted and what stands in for him, has become the exiles' question, and the exiles have nothing to bring.

## Paying the debt

The chapter's answer is the third “confess”:[@oshb-wlc]

> If they shall confess their iniquity, and the iniquity of their fathers, with their trespass which they trespassed against me, and that also they have walked contrary unto me; And that I also have walked contrary unto them, and have brought them into the land of their enemies; if then their uncircumcised hearts be humbled, and they then accept of the punishment of their iniquity: Then will I remember my covenant with Jacob, and also my covenant with Isaac, and also my covenant with Abraham will I remember; and I will remember the land. ([[Lev. 26:40–42]])

Three words in this passage deserve a closer look.

The first is “humbled.” The Hebrew verb occurs nowhere else in Leviticus,[@oshb-wlc] and the Church's footnote files the humbled heart under “Contrite Heart.”[@lds-scriptures] Hold onto that; it will matter at the end.

The second is “accept.” This is *ratsah*, the verb of chapter 1,[@oshb-wlc] but the lexicon gives it a different sense here. Instead of “be accepted, of sacrifices,” the sense at 1:4, it offers “make acceptable, satisfy (by paying off debt).”[@bdb] The same sense appears twice more in the chapter, where the land “enjoys” its sabbaths while it lies empty, which is to say the land pays off the sabbaths it was denied ([[Lev. 26:34]]; [[Lev. 26:43]]). Isaiah uses the verb the same way of exiled Jerusalem, “her iniquity is pardoned” ([[Isa. 40:2]]), a line the lexicon renders “her punishment is accepted (as satisfactory).”[@bdb] Not every reader takes it quite so strongly. The lexicon also allows the milder “accept, be satisfied with [in payment],”[@bdb] and Keil has the exiles “take pleasure” in what their sins have brought, since it has humbled them, as if to say, “We have just what we deserve.”[@keil-leviticus] But on any of these readings, the exiles are no longer bringing something to be accepted. They are themselves accepting, or paying, or being satisfied with, the punishment. (The note “Accept of the punishment” on [chapter 26](/leviticus/26/) treats the verse on its own.)

The third thing to look at is the setting. These are people in exile, far from any temple, and later scripture shows what that was like. Solomon, dedicating the first temple, prayed for captives who would “repent, and make supplication unto thee in the land of them that carried them captives” ([[1 Kgs. 8:47]]), a prayer the Church's edition cross-references at this very passage.[@lds-scriptures] Daniel in Babylon “made my confession” ([[Dan. 9:4]]), using the verb of Leviticus 26:40,[@bdb] and said, “As it is written in the law of Moses, all this evil is come upon us” ([[Dan. 9:13]]), which the footnote sends back to Leviticus 26.[@lds-scriptures] One Latter-day Saint commentator sums up the end of the chapter as a promise “conditioned upon repentance, for those who are suffering in sin and exile.”[@ot-minute-lev] (The notes on [Daniel 9](/daniel/9/) follow his prayer.)

Now watch what the Lord says in reply. “And yet for all that, when they be in the land of their enemies, I will not cast them away, neither will I abhor them, to destroy them utterly, and to break my covenant with them: for I am the LORD their God” ([[Lev. 26:44]]). “Cast away” and “abhor” are the two verbs from verse 15, the despising and abhorring that Israel did to the Lord's statutes. Here the Lord is their subject, and both are negated.[@oshb-wlc] Israel abhorred His law. He will not abhor them.

Let me put the two ends of the book together. In chapter 1 a substitute was brought, the substitute was accepted, and the man was accepted for its sake. In chapter 26 there is nothing to bring, and the same verb describes a people who, confessing and humbled, accept the punishment of their iniquity. The Lord's answer is not to accept an offering, because there is none. It is to remember His covenant and to refuse to abhor the people who had abhorred His law. I cannot say whether Moses meant the two verses to answer each other, and the lexicon and the commentaries treat 26:41 without a glance at 1:4. The join is mine. But that the book's first word about acceptance and its last are the same word, with the offering present in one and absent in the other, is simply there in the text.

One verse outside Leviticus shows where this leads. To the scattered of Israel, Ezekiel hears the Lord say, “I will accept you with your sweet savour, when I bring you out from the people, and gather you out of the countries wherein ye have been scattered” ([[Ezek. 20:41]]). The verb is the verb of 1:4, and the “sweet savour” is the *nichoach* that was refused in 26:31.[@oshb-wlc] But the object of the verb is no longer an animal. It is “you.”[@bdb] To the exiles, the person has become what is accepted, and the savour once refused is promised again.

## Who pays

If the person is what is accepted, Leviticus 26 still leaves one question open. Who answers the punishment the exiles accepted?

It was never going to be the offerings. The Book of Mormon says that the law's sacrifices were “every whit pointing to that great and last sacrifice; and that great and last sacrifice will be the Son of God” ([[Alma 34:14]]), and Hebrews says why: “it is not possible that the blood of bulls and of goats should take away sins” ([[Heb. 10:4]]). What the blood of bulls and goats could not do, one offering did. “We are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” ([[Heb. 10:10]]). The substitute the animal pictured is Christ, who, in the words of this year's *Come, Follow Me*, “stood in our place, gave His life to pay the penalty of sin.”[@cfm-ot-2026-tabernacle]

Lehi puts the whole matter in a single sentence:

> Behold, he offereth himself a sacrifice for sin, to answer the ends of the law, unto all those who have a broken heart and a contrite spirit; and unto none else can the ends of the law be answered. ([[2 Ne. 2:7]])

Two phrases in that sentence need unpacking. “The ends of the law” sounds abstract, but three verses later Lehi ties it to “the inflicting of the punishment which is affixed” ([[2 Ne. 2:10]]), and the Church's manuals read it that way: the ends of the law are “punishment,” and Christ answered them because “He suffered our punishment for breaking the law.”[@bom-teacher-resource-2ne] The phrase “refers to the consequences of the Fall and to the ‘punishment that is affixed’ for disobedience.”[@bom-gospel-doctrine-lesson-6] The other phrase, “a broken heart and a contrite spirit,” is the condition on the sinner's side. And after the Savior's death the Lord said so in His own voice to the Nephites: “your sacrifices and your burnt offerings shall be done away, for I will accept none of your sacrifices and your burnt offerings. And ye shall offer for a sacrifice unto me a broken heart and a contrite spirit” ([[3 Ne. 9:19–20]]).

Now set Lehi beside Leviticus 26. With no altar, the exiles could confess and, with humbled hearts, accept the punishment of their iniquity. Lehi says that Christ answers the punishment the law affixes, and that He does it for those with a broken heart and a contrite spirit. The punishment the exiles could only accept is the punishment Lehi says Christ answers. On this reading the sinner's part is exactly what Leviticus 26 left it, a confession and a humbled heart. The substitute's part, the part the animal played in chapter 1 and no animal could play in chapter 26, is Christ's. “Accepted for him” turns out to have been about Him all along. He is the offering accepted for us. I should be clear that no source joins Leviticus 26:41 to 2 Nephi 2:7, and I do not claim Lehi had Leviticus in mind. I claim only that the two passages describe the same transaction from its two ends.

The heart was asked for under the law as well, and the same verb says so. When David wrote, “thou delightest not in burnt offering” ([[Ps. 51:16]]), the verb is *ratsah* once more,[@oshb-wlc] and the next verse names what God does accept: “The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart” ([[Ps. 51:17]]). Elder Porter reads the psalm as showing “that burnt offerings alone were not enough,”[@porter-broken-heart] and the Seelys add that “after the internal humility had been demonstrated, then the outward symbol of blood sacrifice would be efficacious.”[@seely-sacrifice] After Christ, as Gee puts it, “the Atonement of Christ is the offering, and we put up a broken heart and a contrite spirit.”[@gee-last-sacrifice] President Ballard dates the change to the Savior's sacrifice, when the sacrament took the place of sacrifice and the focus moved from a person's animal to the person.[@ballard-sacrifice] What I have found in Leviticus is that the book was already pointing there. (The Witness of Christ on [chapter 1](/leviticus/1/) quotes President Ballard in full, and Psalms' [The Sacrifice God Desires](/psalms/themes/the-sacrifice-god-desires/#broken-heart) follows David's psalm.)

## What we bring

The command has not changed. “Thou shalt offer a sacrifice unto the Lord thy God in righteousness, even that of a broken heart and a contrite spirit” ([[D&C 59:8]]). The Guide to the Scriptures says what that means: “To have a broken heart is to be humble, contrite, repentant, and meek—that is, receptive to the will of God.”[@gs-broken-heart] And Elder Christofferson says what is being offered: the gift He accepts now “is the gift of yourself—what you are and what you are becoming.”[@christofferson-converted]

So what a disciple brings to the sacrament table is what Leviticus 26 left the exiles holding, a confession and a humbled heart. The offering accepted on his behalf is Christ's.[@bd-sacrifices] Elder Porter describes the condition from the inside. Those with a broken heart “are willing to do anything and everything that God asks of them,” and “in such a condition of submissiveness, the Atonement can take effect and true repentance can occur.”[@porter-broken-heart]

There is one more thing Leviticus says to a disciple, and it says it twice. The law's provision for the poor man and its last word to the scattered agree on this: a sinner who has little or nothing to bring is not, for that reason, refused. The man with a handful of flour was forgiven. The exiles with nothing at all heard the Lord say, “I will not cast them away, neither will I abhor them” ([[Lev. 26:44]]).
