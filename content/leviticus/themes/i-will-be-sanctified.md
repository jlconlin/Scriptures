The verb behind “sanctify,” “hallow” and “consecrate” in Leviticus is one word, *qādaš*, and it stands thirty-one times in the book.[@oshb-wlc] Who does it changes. In chapter 8 Moses does it to Aaron. In chapters 11 and 20 Israel is told to do it to itself. Seven times, from chapter 20 on, the LORD says that He is the one who does it, “I am the LORD which sanctify you” (20:8). The rest are about holy things: what touches the offerings becomes holy (6:18, 27), a man sanctifies a house or a field to the LORD (ch. 27), Israel hallows the jubilee year (25:10), and on the Day of Atonement the high priest hallows the altar “from the uncleanness of the children of Israel” (16:19).[@oshb-wlc][@lds-scriptures] And twice the LORD says it of Himself, in a form the King James renders “I will be sanctified” (10:3) and “I will be hallowed” (22:32); the Church’s footnote at the second notes that the Hebrew is “sanctified.”[@lds-scriptures] Those two are the only places in Leviticus where the LORD is the subject of that form of the verb, and they stand far apart: the first over the bodies of Nadab and Abihu on the priesthood’s first day of service, the second at the close of the laws for the priests (chapters 21–22), in the same sentence as “I am the LORD which hallow you.”[@oshb-wlc]

This page follows the verb through its subjects. What it finds is that Leviticus never lets “sanctify” rest with one of them. Israel is told to sanctify itself in one verse and told in the next that the LORD sanctifies it (20:7–8); the people are told to sanctify the priest, and the reason given is that the LORD sanctifies (21:8); and at the end the LORD’s own hallowing and His hallowing of Israel are one sentence (22:32). The two places where He says “I will be sanctified” show the verb at two moments: when those near Him fail to sanctify Him, and after the book has said, again and again, that He is the giver of holiness. This page reads the two verses together, from the priesthood’s first day to the close of its law; the one source found that treats both reads each toward the hallowing of God’s name, not toward each other.[@bowen-thy-will]

|---|---|---|

| Israel, of itself | 11:44; 20:7 | “sanctify yourselves, and ye shall be holy” |

## “Anointed him, to sanctify him”

The verb’s first subject in Leviticus is Moses. He “poured of the anointing oil upon Aaron’s head, and anointed him, to sanctify him” (8:12), and at the end of the day, with oil and blood, he “sanctified Aaron, and his garments, and his sons” (8:30). Five times in the chapter the verb is Moses’s, in the stem that makes a thing holy.[@oshb-wlc] But the chapter begins and ends with the LORD’s command. It opens “the LORD spake unto Moses” and “This is the thing which the LORD commanded to be done” (8:1, 5), and it closes “So Aaron and his sons did all things which the LORD commanded by the hand of Moses” (8:36).[@lds-scriptures] The command stands in Exodus, where the LORD says what He Himself will do through it: “I will sanctify also both Aaron and his sons, to minister to me in the priest’s office,” and in the verse before, “the tabernacle shall be sanctified by my glory” (Ex. 29:43–44).[@lds-scriptures] One older reading takes the subject of that last clause to be Israel, “and it (Israel) shall be sanctified through My glory,” the LORD manifesting Himself to the whole nation at the altar it was allowed to approach.[@keil-exodus] Another has tent, altar and priests “alike … hallowed by the power of Jehovah’s sanctifying presence.”[@cambridge-exodus] On either, the oil in Moses’s hand is the LORD’s “I will sanctify” carried out. The Student Manual reads the two chapters as “the actual setting apart of Aaron and his sons and the sanctification of the tabernacle that were commanded in Exodus 28–29,”[@ot-manual-lev-1-10] and Keil reads chapters 8 to 10 as one movement: consecration, entrance on office, and then “the sanctification of their priesthood by the judgment of God.”[@keil-leviticus] See [Leviticus 8:12](/leviticus/8/).

## “I will be sanctified in them that come nigh me”

The judgment comes in chapter 10. Nadab and Abihu “offered strange fire before the LORD, which he commanded them not,” and “there went out fire from the LORD, and devoured them” (10:1–2). Then Moses says to his brother, “This is it that the LORD spake, saying, I will be sanctified in them that come nigh me, and before all the people I will be glorified. And Aaron held his peace” (10:3).[@lds-scriptures]

The words are Moses’s, and he gives them as the LORD’s. Where the LORD spoke them the commentators do not agree. The Pulpit commentary points to Exodus 19:22, 28:41 and 29:44 and Leviticus 8:33.[@pulpit-leviticus] Keil’s answer is that He “had not communicated to Moses by revelation the words” but “had made the fact known by the position assigned to Aaron and his sons”: He had brought them near and “sanctified them to Himself by anointing … that they might sanctify Him in their office and life. If they neglected this sanctification, He sanctified Himself in them by a penal judgment.”[@keil-leviticus] The Cambridge commentary hears a quotation in poetic parallelism, “In them that come nigh me I will shew myself holy, / And before all the people I will glorify myself,” and the sense that “He shews Himself holy in punishing those who do it improperly.”[@cambridge-leviticus] The Pulpit commentary keeps both doors open: “God will be sanctified either by the obedience or by the punishment of those that come nigh him, that is, his priests.”[@pulpit-leviticus]

The stem here is the one English renders passively, and Keil and Ellicott both read it reflexively, “I will sanctify Myself.”[@keil-leviticus][@ellicott-leviticus] The lexicon reads it otherwise. It files 10:3 and 22:32 together under “be honoured or treated as sacred,” the first in parallel with “be glorified,” the second as the opposite of profaning the name, and keeps the sense “shew oneself sacred” for Isaiah and Ezekiel.[@bdb] On the lexicon’s reading the verse says what the priests owed the LORD and did not give; on the commentators’, what the LORD did when it was not given. On either reading the near ones are the priests the LORD had sanctified in chapter 8. The verse does not settle which, and this page does not. Matthew Bowen translates the line “Through those who come unto me I will be sanctified / and before all the people I will be glorified,” and takes the parallel to show that “sanctify” and “glorify” are close in sense here, as in Ezekiel 28:22.[@bowen-thy-will]

The verb comes back to Aaron at the rock. The LORD tells him and Moses, “Because ye believed me not, to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel,” in the causative stem, and the account closes in 10:3’s own form, “he was sanctified in them” (Num. 20:12–13).[@lds-scriptures][@oshb-wlc] Keil: “God sanctified Himself on them, by the fact that, on the one hand, He put their unbelief to shame by the miraculous gift of water, and on the other hand punished Moses and Aaron.”[@keil-numbers] The Student Manual reads the verse as the LORD chiding the two “for their failure to sanctify Him in the eyes of the people.”[@ot-manual-numbers-13-36] This page sets the rock beside the strange fire. In both the LORD is sanctified when those He had set nearest fail to sanctify Him before the people, and in both the failure is in Aaron’s house, his sons in the one and Aaron himself in the other. At the rock the same sanctifying also gave the people water, and the lexicon does not file the two verses together: it reads Numbers 20:13 with Ezekiel’s “shew oneself sacred.”[@bdb]

The Church’s footnote at the verse is not on “sanctified” but on “held.” Aaron’s silence is cross-referenced to a modern revelation: “For all those who will not endure chastening, but deny me, cannot be sanctified” (D&C 101:5).[@lds-scriptures] This page reads the footnote’s connection this way: the one who holds his peace under the judgment is the one in whom the sanctifying can go on. Elder D. Todd Christofferson quotes the same revelation of the chastening that “can refine us and prepare us for greater spiritual privileges.”[@christofferson-chasten] Keil reads Moses’s words not as a reproof to Aaron but as “an admonition to all who heard it, not to Aaron only but to the whole nation, to sanctify God continually in the proper way.”[@keil-leviticus] For who “them that come nigh me” are, and how the book treats nearness after this day, see [Them That Come Nigh Me](/leviticus/themes/them-that-come-nigh-me/); this page follows the verb. See [Leviticus 10:3](/leviticus/10/).

## “Sanctify yourselves … I am the LORD which sanctify you”

When the verb next has Israel for its subject, the stem turns it back on the doer. “Ye shall therefore sanctify yourselves, and ye shall be holy; for I am holy” (11:44), and “Sanctify yourselves therefore, and be ye holy: for I am the LORD your God” (20:7), the second “almost verbally identical” with the first.[@lds-scriptures][@cambridge-leviticus] The lexicon’s gloss for this stem in these two verses is plain, “keep oneself apart from unclean things.”[@bdb] The Pulpit commentary says what that was for: “They were to sanctify themselves, that is, to avoid uncleanness, because God is holy, and they were God’s. They were thus taught that ceremonial cleanness of the body was a symbol of holiness of heart, and a means of attaining to the latter.”[@pulpit-leviticus] The Bible Dictionary cites 11:44 among the places where “holy” came “to refer to moral character.”[@bd-holiness]

Then the next verse changes subject without changing the sentence. “And ye shall keep my statutes, and do them: I am the LORD which sanctify you” (20:8).[@lds-scriptures] This is the first in Leviticus of seven participles, “the one who sanctifies,” and the lexicon groups all seven with Exodus 31:13 and Ezekiel 20:12 under one heading, “of God, keeping his people pure and sacred.”[@bdb] Keil puts the two verses into one sentence of his own: Israel was “to sanctify themselves, i.e., to keep themselves pure from all idolatrous abominations, to be holy because Jehovah was holy … and to keep the statutes of their God who sanctified them (Exodus 31:13).”[@keil-leviticus] Ellicott: “It is only by keeping the Divine ordinances that the Israelites will attain to that state of holiness … which will enable them to reflect the holiness of their Lord.”[@ellicott-leviticus]

The Church’s materials make the same move. The footnote on “Sanctify” at 20:7 goes to 21:8, to 22:32, and to a modern commandment that holds both agents in one verse: “sanctify yourselves; yea, purify your hearts, and cleanse your hands and your feet before me, that I may make you clean” (D&C 88:74).[@lds-scriptures] The Student Manual reads the chapter’s purpose as separating Israel “so that they could be sanctified and become holy unto God (see vv. 7–8, 24, 26).”[@ot-manual-lev-19-27] The Seminary student material draws from the same two verses the principle that “if we obey the Lord’s commandments, then we can be holy like the Lord is holy,” and speaks of obedience “so that He can sanctify you and make you holy.”[@ot-seminary-student-2018-lev-64] Gerald N. Lund, writing of the same commandment in modern revelation, says that it “does not suggest that we can make ourselves holy but only suggests that we must initiate the process for ourselves.”[@lund-sanctification] Gaye Strathearn: God is “the ultimate source of the power to sanctify,” and He “desires all Israel to become qadōš,” holy, “but they have to want it.”[@strathearn-holiness-lord] See [Leviticus 11:44](/leviticus/11/) and [Leviticus 20](/leviticus/20/).

## “I the LORD, which sanctify you, am holy”

In the laws for the priests the participle becomes a refrain. The Cambridge commentary counts it at 21:8, 15, 23 and 22:9, 16, 32, and reads it as a refrain, attaching no theology to it.[@cambridge-leviticus] Its object moves. “I the LORD, which sanctify you, am holy” (21:8) is said to Israel of the priest; “I the LORD do sanctify him” (21:15) of the high priest; “I the LORD do sanctify them” (21:23) of “my sanctuaries,” which the Cambridge commentary takes as the sacred building and its surroundings; and “them” at 22:9 of the priests the verse addresses.[@lds-scriptures][@cambridge-leviticus][@oshb-wlc] At 22:16 the commentators differ: Keil takes the “them” who bear the trespass to be the laymen who eat the holy gifts unawares.[@keil-leviticus] Whatever the object, the subject does not move. The one who makes the priest, the high priest, the sanctuary and the people holy is the LORD, and the book says so after each rule as the rule’s reason.

The verse that opens the refrain has two subjects in it. “Thou shalt sanctify him therefore; for he offereth the bread of thy God: he shall be holy unto thee: for I the LORD, which sanctify you, am holy” (21:8). The “thou” is singular, and the commentators ask who it is. Keil: “The Israelites as a nation are addressed in the persons of their chiefs,” and the sense is “not merely ‘respect his holy dignity’ … but take care that he did not desecrate his office by a marriage so polluted.” Ellicott: “the Jewish community,” so that “the Jewish priest is thus placed under the supervision of the people.” Cambridge: the verse “has all the air of an insertion,” and “Who is addressed?”[@keil-leviticus][@ellicott-leviticus][@cambridge-leviticus] On the first two readings the people sanctify the priest whom the LORD sanctifies, and the verse gives the second as the reason for the first. The Bible Dictionary cites this verse, with 11:44 and 19:2, for “holy” as moral character.[@bd-holiness] Bowen reads the weight the law puts on the priests as serving the whole people: priestly holiness “would necessarily serve as a kind of beachhead for the broader hallowing (or sanctification) of the entire community.”[@bowen-law-codes]

The same participle stands in the Sabbath law, “that ye may know that I am the LORD that doth sanctify you” (Ex. 31:13), and President Russell M. Nelson calls the Sabbath “a perpetual covenant, a constant reminder that the Lord may sanctify His people.”[@lds-scriptures][@oshb-wlc][@nelson-sabbath] One verse of the refrain is among the few in Leviticus that Joseph Smith revised: his revision makes 22:9 conditional, “if they profane not mine ordinances, I the Lord will sanctify them,” which Bowen reads as “divine reciprocity.”[@bowen-law-codes] The note at [Leviticus 22:9](/leviticus/22/) takes that up; see also [Leviticus 21:6](/leviticus/21/).

## “I will be hallowed among the children of Israel”

The refrain’s last verse is the book’s second “I will be sanctified.” “Therefore shall ye keep my commandments, and do them: I am the LORD. Neither shall ye profane my holy name; but I will be hallowed among the children of Israel: I am the LORD which hallow you” (22:31–32).[@lds-scriptures] The verb stands twice in the one verse, first of the LORD and then of Israel as His object, and the King James’s “hallow” is the word it rendered “sanctify” at 10:3.[@oshb-wlc]

What stands around it differs from chapter 10. There the sentence followed a judgment; here it follows a command not to profane the name, and the lexicon sets the verb against that profaning: to be hallowed is the opposite of being profaned.[@bdb] No older commentary read has a note on the clause; Keil’s and Ellicott’s whole comment is a cross-reference.[@keil-leviticus][@ellicott-leviticus] Bowen is the one reader found who takes it up. He suggests that Ezekiel may allude to it, “I will sanctify my great name, which was profaned among the heathen … when I shall be sanctified in you before their eyes” (Ezek. 36:23), where the LORD’s being sanctified is followed by gathering, clean water and a new heart, and he concludes: “the hallowing of the divine name is also the hallowing of Israel (see Leviticus 22:32),” so that the prayer “hallowed be thy name” is “a plea that God will hallow Israel and that Israel will hallow its God.”[@bowen-thy-will][@lds-scriptures]

That is where this page’s two verses meet, and the meeting is this page’s reading. In 10:3 the LORD is sanctified in near ones who had not sanctified Him, and nothing in that verse says that the sanctifying is His to give; in Leviticus, “I am the LORD which sanctify you” has not yet been said. In 22:32, after that participle has stood six times, His being hallowed among Israel and His hallowing of Israel are one sentence. What the book has added between the two is the refrain, the LORD’s repeated word that the holiness in question is His to give.

## “He that sanctifieth and they who are sanctified”

The LORD who says “I am the LORD which sanctify you” is Jehovah, the premortal Jesus Christ.[@gs-jehovah] Hebrews names the one who sanctifies. “For both he that sanctifieth and they who are sanctified are all of one: for which cause he is not ashamed to call them brethren” (Heb. 2:11). Bowen hears in its Greek the wording of the Septuagint, the Greek Old Testament, at Leviticus 11:44–45, where God commands “being holy … because I the Lord am holy.” He leaves open whether the sanctifier in Hebrews 2:11 is the Son or the Father; the “brethren” of the verse, and the letter’s later words, give the sanctifying to Jesus.[@lds-scriptures][@bowen-brethren-hebrews-2] The letter then says by what means: “we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (Heb. 10:10), and “Jesus also, that he might sanctify the people with his own blood, suffered without the gate” (Heb. 13:12).[@lds-scriptures] Moroni’s closing word has both agents in it: “if ye by the grace of God are perfect in Christ, and deny not his power, then are ye sanctified in Christ by the grace of God, through the shedding of the blood of Christ … that ye become holy, without spot” (Moro. 10:33), which Bowen calls “perhaps the best statement on holiness, perfection, the source of both in Yahweh.”[@lds-scriptures][@bowen-law-codes] The Guide to the Scriptures defines sanctification as “the process of becoming free from sin, pure, clean, and holy through the Atonement of Jesus Christ.”[@gs-sanctification]

Modern revelation keeps Leviticus’s two subjects. “Sanctification through the grace of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ is just and true, to all those who love and serve God with all their mights, minds, and strength” (D&C 20:31); “sanctify yourselves … that I may make you clean” (D&C 88:74); “I am able to make you holy” (D&C 60:7).[@lds-scriptures] *Come, Follow Me* reads the tabernacle, the laws and the sacrifices toward that last verse: the tabernacle, the covenants and the sacrifices were “meant to point their minds, hearts, and lives toward the Savior. He is the true path to holiness, for the Israelites and for us.”[@cfm-ot-2026-18] Sister Carol F. McConkie quoted 11:44 and then Elder Christofferson, “He proposes to make us holy,” and added that the Father “has provided for us all that is required so that we can become holy as He is holy.”[@mcconkie-beauty-holiness] Lund says how that offering reaches a person: in applying the blood of Christ, it is the Holy Ghost’s “power and influence that actually purges out the dross, cleanses us from sin, and makes us holy.”[@lund-sanctification]

For a disciple the book’s two subjects are two statements, and neither cancels the other. “Sanctify yourselves” is a command a person can begin to keep, and in Lund’s words it means “that we must initiate the process for ourselves.”[@lund-sanctification] “I am the LORD which sanctify you” is the reason the command is not hopeless: the holiness asked for is given by the one who asks for it, and, as Strathearn says, “they have to want it.”[@strathearn-holiness-lord]
