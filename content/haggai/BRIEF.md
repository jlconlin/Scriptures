## What a chapter is for here

Haggai is 38 verses in two chapters of prose with oracles in it: four messages, each dated to the day (1:1; 2:1; 2:10; 2:20), with the people's response between (1:12–15). A reader asks: *Who was Haggai, and why is so little known of him? When is “the second year of Darius,” and what had happened since Cyrus? Why had the building stopped, and why did the people say the time had not come? What are “ceiled houses”? Why does the Lord tie a bad harvest to an unbuilt temple? Who are Zerubbabel and Joshua, and why is Zerubbabel called the son of Shealtiel here and of Pedaiah in Chronicles? Who had seen the first house, and why did the second seem “as nothing”? Who or what is “the desire of all nations”? How could this plainer house have more glory than Solomon's? What is the point of the questions about holy flesh and a dead body? Why is Zerubbabel the Lord's signet, and did anything come of it? What does any of this mean for a Church that builds temples?* A chapter answers those from the text first.

**The book is about one thing.** Every source read says so: the house of the Lord, and the people's priorities. Chapter 1 is the call and the obedience; chapter 2 is encouragement, a lesson in holiness, and a promise. The `thread` of chapter 1 says what chapter 2 promises to those who obeyed; chapter 2's says what changed “from this day” (2:18–19).

**An obeyed prophet.** Huntington: “Although many of the Old Testament prophets' messages and exhortations went unheeded … Haggai's ministry appears to be an exception”; the Bible Dictionary: “His preaching produced the desired effect (1:14–15).” Chapter 1's setting says so once.

## Divisions

**Proposed: two divisions, one a chapter** (`book.yaml`: `hagbuild`, “Consider Your Ways,” chapter 1; `hagglory`, “The Glory of This Latter House,” chapter 2). The book's own structure is its four dated messages (1:1–15; 2:1–9; 2:10–19; 2:20–23; Ball and the Bible Dictionary follow it), but the book page divides by chapters, and the first message is chapter 1 while the other three are chapter 2. Chapter 2's `setting` names its three messages and their dates; its notes follow them. The keys repeat no other book's.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, which later scripture takes up which line) is the coordinator's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **When.** Haggai 1:1: “In the second year of Darius the king.” Bible Dictionary: “His prophecy was spoken about 520 B.C.”; *Scripture Helps*: “dated to 520 BC, about 18 years after King Cyrus of Persia allowed the exiled Jews to return”; Huntington: “for only three and one-half months—beginning in late August and ending in early December of 520 B.C.” **Proposed: every chapter's `when` is “Haggai spoke in Jerusalem in the second year of Darius I of Persia, 520 B.C.,”** with a ledger row from the Bible Dictionary or *Scripture Helps*. Modern dates for each message (late August to December) are Huntington's, cited as his; don't convert the other days without a source. Darius is Darius I, son of Hystaspes (the footnote at 1:1; `bd-darius`); the site's note at `[[Dan. 5:31]]` already separates him from Darius the Mede.
- **How long the work had stopped.** The sources count differently: Huntington, “sixteen years” (536 to 520); Scripture Central's *Minute*, two years of work and then “fourteen years”; *Come, Follow Me*, “roughly 15 years” since the return; Van Dyke and Ogden, “A decade and a half.” **Proposed:** say “about eighteen years after Cyrus's decree” (*Scripture Helps*) and that the work had stood still “unto the second year of the reign of Darius” (`[[Ezra 4:24]]`); give a count of years only as one source's. **The temple was finished** “in the sixth year of the reign of Darius” (`[[Ezra 6:15]]`): 516 (Bible Dictionary) or 515 (*Scripture Helps*); the persia guide gives both. Cite the verse and the guide.
- **Why the building stopped.** Ezra 4:1–5 and 4:24: the people of the land, refused a share in the work, opposed it. *Scripture Helps*: “because of opposition from the Samaritans and their own loss of enthusiasm”; Huntington, “Samaritan opposition and a good deal of apathy”; Van Dyke and Ogden add crop failure, poverty and the change of kings (Cambyses), so that “the edict of Cyrus was eventually forgotten” (`[[Ezra 5:17]]`–`[[Ezra 6:1]]`). Haggai himself names only the people's excuse (1:2) and their own houses (1:4, 9). **Proposed:** chapter 1's setting gives both causes in a sentence, from *Scripture Helps*, and lets Haggai's own charge stand: the text blames the people, not the Samaritans. Ezra 4:6–23 concerns later kings; don't cite it for Haggai's time.
- **Who.** Haggai: nothing is known of his life (Huntington; the Student Manual, printing *The New Bible Commentary*: “Nothing is stated about the private life of Haggai”); he is named with Zechariah at `[[Ezra 5:1]]` and `[[Ezra 6:14]]`. The name: “festal” (Ball, from *ḥ-g-g*, “to make a pilgrimage or keep a pilgrim feast”; Huntington); Huntington's “may indicate Haggai was born during one of three important Jewish feasts” is his. Whether Haggai had seen Solomon's temple (2:3): Jewish tradition, says Huntington; Scripture Central's *Minute* says he “seems to have personally remembered” it. Give it as a reading, once, in chapter 2. **Zerubbabel**, “governor of Judah”: Jehoiachin's grandson (the footnote; `bd-zerubbabel`; *Scripture Helps* 7). “Son of Shealtiel” here, son of Pedaiah at `[[1 Chr. 3:19]]`: the Bible Dictionary, he is called son of Shealtiel, “probably his nephew or legal heir”. Whether he is the Sheshbazzar of Ezra 1:8: the Bible Dictionary says yes; the persia guide sets it beside the Student Manual's “likelier, a second person”; Van Dyke and Ogden make Sheshbazzar his uncle. Haggai never names Sheshbazzar: don't raise it; link the guide if a reader would ask. **Joshua** the high priest, son of Josedech (Jehozadak, “the last high priest before the Babylonian captivity,” *Scripture Helps* 7; `[[1 Chr. 6:15]]`); the Jeshua of Ezra; Zechariah 3 and 6 are his (link Zechariah's chapters, don't explain them here).
- **“Consider your ways”** (1:5, 7; 2:15, 18). The Hebrew is “set your heart upon your ways” (`hebrew.mjs haggai 1:5`); Huntington says the verb “is used five times in the book.” One Language note, in chapter 1, at 1:5; chapter 2 points back at 2:15 and 2:18. The repetition across both chapters is the book's refrain: say so once.
- **Waste and drought.** Scripture Central's *Minute*: the words for “waste” (1:4, 9) and “drought” (1:11) share the root *ḥ-r-b*, “probably not a coincidence”: the house lies *ḥārēb*, so the land gets *ḥōreb*. Check with `hebrew.mjs` (1:4, 1:9, 1:11) and give it in chapter 1's note on 1:11, as the *Minute*'s observation. The curses of 1:6, 10–11 and 2:17 echo the covenant curses (the footnotes: `[[Deut. 28:38]]`, `[[Deut. 28:22]]`, `[[Amos 4:6–11]]`); one Related Scriptures note in chapter 1, and 2:17 points back.
- **“The LORD of hosts.”** Fourteen times in twelve of the 38 verses (`occurrences.mjs haggai "LORD of hosts"`; the *Minute* counts six in chapter 1 and nine in chapter 2, which is not the script's count: cite the verses, not a number). Explained once, in chapter 1, briefly; the site has explained the title before (`occurrences.mjs all "LORD of hosts" --site all`: link the best note).
- **“Haggai the LORD's messenger”** (1:13): *malʾak*, the word in Malachi's name (`[[Mal. 1:1]]`; Malachi's chapter 1 explains the name). One clause, if a source sets them together.
- **Other books on the site.** Link, don't retell: `[[Ezra 3:12–13]]` (the old men who wept at the foundation; the footnote at 2:3); `[[Heb. 12:26–27]]` (quotes 2:6; Scripture Central's *Minute*); `[[Matt. 1:12]]` and `[[Luke 3:27]]` (Zerubbabel in the Saviour's genealogies; *Scripture Helps* 10); `[[Dan. 2:44]]` (the footnote at 2:22); `[[Zech. 4:6–10]]` (Zerubbabel's hands lay the foundation and finish the house; `bd-zerubbabel`). The Book of Mormon has no Haggai passage; no `bom` note.
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** No change in Haggai (the footnotes list none). The RSC's search snippet from *Joseph Smith's Translation of the Bible* names Haggai in a list that appears to be of books left unchanged; read the page before saying so.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports what runs across the book; the coordinator adds it to `THEME-CANDIDATES.md` (not yet created). Leads from the sources, not choices: the house of the Lord and the people's priorities; “I am with you” (1:13; 2:4–5); the shaking of heaven and earth (2:6–7, 21–22) and Hebrews 12. With two chapters, a theme page is more likely shared with Zechariah than Haggai's own.

**Habits to avoid** are listed in Amos's brief and hold here. In Haggai especially: **don't retell the return from exile** (the guides have it); **don't turn every verse into a sermon on temple attendance**: the Restoration connection is made where the text and the Church's sources make it (below), and the rest of the book is given its own setting; and **don't promise prosperity for temple work**: Haggai's harvests are the covenant's terms for his people, stated as such.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that two chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-23.

- **“The desire of all nations” (2:7).** *The Church's reading:* the chapter heading, “The Desire of All Nations will come,” with the footnote at 2:7, “TG Jesus Christ, Second Coming”; *Scripture Helps* 8–9: the title “can refer to the Savior Jesus Christ,” and the prophecy “likely has multiple fulfillments”: His visits to the temple in His mortal ministry, His return to the temple in Jerusalem at His Second Coming, and “in a broader sense, all temples”; Huntington: “the Holy One of Israel—even Jesus Christ,” “probably dualistic”; the Student Manual (30-3): “a prophecy of Christ, who will bring a lasting peace to the world”; Scripture Central's *Minute* and the Interpreter study aid the same. *The Hebrew:* *ḥemdat kol-haggôyim*, a singular noun, “desire” or “desirable thing,” with a plural verb, “shall come” (`hebrew.mjs haggai 2:7`: *ûbāʾû*, 3rd plural). Keil (last, and older) argues from the plural verb against the Messiah as subject: the phrase is “the valuable possessions of” the nations, which fits the next verse, “The silver is mine, and the gold is mine” (2:8); many modern translations read so (ESV “the treasures of all nations shall come in”; NIV “what is desired by all nations will come”). **Proposed:** a Witness of Christ note at 2:7 gives the Church's reading first and fully, from the chapter heading and *Scripture Helps* (Holland's line, once), with Huntington's two fulfilments. It then says, in one or two sentences of language and not as an equal alternative (`STANDARDS.md` §1), that the Hebrew word means a desirable thing, that its verb is plural, and that many translations read “treasures,” as 2:8's silver and gold suggest; *Scripture Helps*' “can refer” leaves room for both, and the note doesn't set one against the other. The phrase in the hymn “Come, O Thou King of Kings” (the Student Manual) is reception history: not used.
- **The latter house greater than the former (2:3, 9).** Why the new house seemed “as nothing”: the Bible Dictionary's “Temple of Zerubbabel” gives the Jews' reckoning of five things it lacked (the ark, the Shechinah or glory, the Urim and Thummim, the holy fire, the spirit of prophecy): the irony that the house without the glory is promised, “I will fill this house with glory” (2:7; the footnote: `[[Ex. 40:34]]`), is worth the note. How it was greater: Huntington, because “the Lord's anticipated personal visits to the temple would glorify the current edifice beyond that of Solomon's temple”; Scripture Central's *Minute* adds Herod's rebuilding of the same temple and the Saviour's teaching in it (`[[Mark 14:49]]`); *Scripture Helps* 9 extends it to the Second Coming and to all temples; the Student Manual (30-3) reads it of “the latter-day temple that will be built on the same site.” **Proposed:** chapter 2's note at 2:9 gives Huntington's reading as the chief one (the Lord Himself in that house), Herod's rebuilding in a clause, and the latter-day readings as *Scripture Helps*' and the Student Manual's, each as its own; “in this place will I give peace” is the verse's last line and the note ends there. [Temple and Worship](/old-testament/guides/temple-and-worship/) already quotes 2:3 and 2:9: link it.
- **Clean and unclean (2:10–19).** The priests rule that holy flesh carried in a garment does not make other food holy (2:12; `[[Lev. 6:27]]`, which the site's note at `[[Lev. 6:18]]` already applies to Haggai), but a man unclean from a corpse makes unclean what he touches (2:13; `[[Num. 19:11–13]]`, `[[Num. 19:22]]`). Haggai's application: “So is this people … and that which they offer there is unclean” (2:14). The Bible Dictionary: “just as the touch of the unclean pollutes all about it, so the worldly spirit of the people had brought a curse on all their labor”; Huntington: “uncleanness is much more easily transmitted than holiness,” and the sacrifices on the rebuilt altar “could not transmit holiness to them”; the Student Manual prints Keil (the holy land does not sanctify its produce; neglect of the house makes everything unclean). Van Dyke and Ogden read the exiles' view of the land's inhabitants as unclean into 2:12–14; that is theirs, and the text says “this people.” **Proposed:** one note on the ruling (link `[[Lev. 6:18]]` for the holiness half, explain the corpse half from Numbers 19), one on 2:14 from the Bible Dictionary and Huntington: the altar and the land did not make the people holy while the house lay waste. Then 2:15–19: “from this day” (the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month) the Lord will bless them, though the seed is not yet in the barn (2:19; the *Minute*). Van Dyke and Ogden's reading, once at most, as theirs.
- **Zerubbabel the signet, and the Davidic hope (2:20–23).** The site already explains the signet and Jeremiah's reversal at `[[Jer. 22:24]]` and `[[Jer. 22:30]]`, quoting *Scripture Helps* 10: link, don't quote *Scripture Helps* again. What is Haggai's own: the promise comes “in that day,” after the Lord overthrows “the throne of kingdoms” (2:22; the footnote: `[[Dan. 2:44]]`), and Zerubbabel is called “my servant” and “chosen” (the footnote: `[[Ps. 89:3]]`, the oath to David). Zerubbabel did not become king; Van Dyke and Ogden: he “was the final Davidic descendant to govern in Judah,” and the hopes of the monarchy “would be extinguished at the death of Zerubbabel.” The Church's sources read the promise as fulfilled in Christ: the Student Manual (30-5): “The prophecy is messianic, and Zerubbabel … served as a type of Christ,” printing Keil (the promise was to his office and line, and “culminates in Jesus Christ, the son of David and descendant of Zerubbabel”); Ball: “Zerubbabel can be understood as a type for the Messiah”; Scripture Central's *Minute*: “to be a type of the coming Messiah.” **Proposed:** a Witness of Christ note at 2:23 says what the words promised Zerubbabel, that he never reigned (Van Dyke and Ogden), and that the promise passed through his line to Christ (`[[Matt. 1:12]]`; `[[Luke 3:27]]`), from the Student Manual and Ball; the Keil passage the manual prints is long: a phrase at most. Zechariah's “Branch” (Zech. 3:8; 6:12) belongs to Zechariah: link, don't explain.
- **Temple building in the Restoration (1:2–11; D&C 95).** Checked: the footnote at 1:8 gives `[[D&C 88:119]]` and `[[D&C 95:3]]`, and *Come, Follow Me* (lesson 50) says “The early Latter-day Saints faced a similar situation to the Israelites in Haggai's day (see Doctrine and Covenants 95).” D&C 95:3: “ye have sinned against me a very grievous sin, in that ye have not considered the great commandment in all things, that I have given unto you concerning the building of mine house” (Kirtland, June 1, 1833; the section heading); “considered” answers Haggai's “Consider your ways.” Van Dyke and Ogden cite the same verse. The Student Manual (30-2) adds Nauvoo (`[[D&C 124:31–55]]`) and that both temples were built “in times of great poverty and persecution”; Huntington sets 2:7–9 beside the Kirtland dedicatory prayer (`[[D&C 109:12–13]]`); the footnote at 2:7 gives `[[D&C 97:15]]`. **Proposed:** chapter 1's Restoration note at 1:4–8 sets D&C 95:1–3, 11 beside Haggai (the Lord's chastening for delay, the promise of power to build), from the footnote and *Come, Follow Me*; chapter 2 may link D&C 109:12–13 at 2:7 or 2:9, from Huntington. Each is said once. The site does not draw a lesson about the reader's temple attendance beyond a statement in `christ`.
