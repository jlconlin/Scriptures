In the ninth month of the second year of Darius, three months into the rebuilding of the temple, Haggai was told to put two questions to the priests. The first: if a man carries holy flesh in the fold of his garment and the fold brushes bread, or stew, or wine, or oil, does the food become holy? The priests said no. The second: if a man unclean from a corpse touches any of those, does it become unclean? The priests said yes ([[Hag. 2:11–13]]). Then Haggai turned the two answers on the people. “So is this people, and so is this nation before me, saith the LORD; and so is every work of their hands; and that which they offer there is unclean” ([[Hag. 2:14]]).

It is an odd passage to find at the heart of a book about building. The priests' two answers are a small lesson in the law of clean and unclean, and most readers pass over them to the promise that follows: “from this day will I bless you” ([[Hag. 2:19]]). But the two answers describe a rule, and the rule is the key to the whole book. Holiness, under the law, does not travel far. It passes to the first thing it touches and stops there. Uncleanness passes further than holiness does. A people who are unclean therefore cannot be made holy by anything they hold, however holy the thing is, and everything they hold becomes unclean instead.

My argument is that Haggai knows this and builds the book on it. Nothing Israel had, not the altar they had rebuilt and not the land they had returned to, could by itself pass holiness up to them. What the book offers in its place is given from the other direction. “I am with you,” the Lord says, when the people have turned but before the first stone is laid, and His Spirit, His glory and His blessing follow in the same order, each promised before the thing it would seem to depend on. Then the Gospels show what that presence is when it comes among the unclean in a body. A leper is touched and made clean. A bier is touched and the dead man sits up. A woman whom the law made contagious reaches for the corner of His garment, and power goes out of Him to her. The priests' rule is not overturned by this. It is answered by the one person the rule does not describe.

## One step, and no further

The priests were not improvising. The first answer rests on a law that appears several times in Exodus and Leviticus, and it is worth seeing exactly what that law says, because it is more generous than Haggai's “No” suggests.

Of the sin offering the law says, “Whatsoever shall touch the flesh thereof shall be holy” ([[Lev. 6:27]]). The same words are used of every offering made by fire ([[Lev. 6:18]]), of the altar, “whatsoever toucheth the altar shall be holy” ([[Ex. 29:37]]), and of the anointed furniture of the tabernacle ([[Ex. 30:29]]). So holiness does pass by touch. Keil takes the rule at its word: a layman who touched the most holy things “became holy through the contact,” and was thereafter bound to guard against defilement as a priest was.[@keil-leviticus] Ezekiel's priests feared that very thing. They were to leave the garments they ministered in inside the holy chambers and put on others before going out to the people, “and they shall not sanctify the people with their garments” ([[Ezek. 44:19]]). Holiness in a garment was real enough to be dangerous.

What Haggai's priests rule is that it goes no further than that. The flesh makes the garment holy; the garment does not make the bread holy. Keil, reading the two together, says the priests “answer the question laid before them quite correctly,” since under Leviticus 6 the fold of the garment “was made holy by the holy flesh, but it could not communicate this holiness any further.”[@keil-haggai] The Cambridge commentary says the same: holiness “could only extend… to that with which it came into first and immediate contact. Beyond that limit its efficacy did not reach.”[@cambridge-haggai] One step, and no further.

The second answer rests on a different law and runs the other way. “He that toucheth the dead body of any man shall be unclean seven days” ([[Num. 19:11]]), and then, “whatsoever the unclean person toucheth shall be unclean; and the soul that toucheth it shall be unclean until even” ([[Num. 19:22]]). The chain runs on, and on again, where holiness stopped at once. The same verb, *nāgaʿ*, “touch,” stands in Leviticus 6, in Numbers 19 and in both of Haggai's questions[@oshb-wlc], so the priests are being asked about one act with two opposite results. Ray Huntington draws the conclusion the two laws force: “uncleanness is much more easily transmitted than holiness.”[@huntington-consider-ways] The Bible Dictionary says it of the leper, who “communicated uncleanness to everything he touched,”[@bd-clean-unclean] and Leviticus says it of a woman with an issue of blood: “whosoever toucheth her shall be unclean” ([[Lev. 15:19]]). Hold on to that last case. It will matter.

## So is this people

Now the application. “So is this people,” Haggai says, and the “so” reaches back to both questions. In what sense were they like a man with holy flesh in his garment, and in what sense like a man unclean from the dead?

The first likeness is the one Keil, Huntington and the Bible Dictionary draw. The returned exiles had holy things. They had rebuilt the altar in their first year back and had offered on it ever since, though there was no house ([[Ezra 3:2–3]]). They stood on the land the Lord had chosen. Huntington thinks Haggai “may have been alluding to the fact that the Jews' sacrifices and offerings on the rebuilt altar of the temple could not transmit holiness to them,” and that the land, though “consecrated by the Lord,” could not by “that fact alone… make them a holy people”; the only way, he adds, “was to rebuild the temple and worthily participate in the temple service.”[@huntington-consider-ways] Keil, as the Student Manual prints him, says the same of the soil: no food is made holy by being planted in the land that encloses the sanctuary, any more than bread is made holy by a sanctified garment.[@ot-manual-30] The holy things they had were like the flesh in the fold. Real, and holy, and unable to pass it on.

The second likeness is the sharper one. A people unclean from neglect of the Lord's house, Keil writes, is “like a man who has become unclean through touching a corpse,” so that “everything that Israel takes hold of… is from the very first affected with the curse of uncleanness.”[@ot-manual-30] The Bible Dictionary puts it in a sentence: “just as the touch of the unclean pollutes all about it, so the worldly spirit of the people had brought a curse on all their labor and increase.”[@bd-haggai] That is what the next verses describe. Before stone was laid on stone a heap of twenty measures yielded ten, and the Lord smote “all the labours of your hands” with blasting and mildew and hail ([[Hag. 2:15–17]]). “Every work of their hands” in verse 14 and “all the labours of your hands” in verse 17 are one Hebrew phrase[@oshb-wlc], and the connection between them is mine: the works called unclean are the works that were smitten. Uncleanness had passed from the people to everything they touched, exactly as the second answer said it would.

So Haggai's two questions are not a riddle. They are a diagnosis. Holy things could not make this people holy, and this people made everything unholy. That is a closed circle, and nothing inside it can open it.

I should say at once what the book does not say. It does not say the people had nothing to do. They did fear the Lord ([[Hag. 1:12]]); they did come and work ([[Hag. 1:14]]); the blessing is dated from the day they laid the foundation ([[Hag. 2:18]]). Every Latter-day Saint source on these verses keeps that condition, and so does the text. The Student Manual: “when the Jews had repented and begun the work on the temple… the Lord promised His blessing.”[@ot-manual-30] Scripture Central's *Minute*: “they themselves needed to work on becoming clean.”[@breitenstein-haggai-minute] The Cambridge commentary: the blessing was promised “now that they had put away that sin, and were honestly giving themselves to the work.”[@cambridge-haggai] The people had to turn. What I am arguing is narrower: that their turning is not what made them holy, and the book is careful to show where holiness came from instead.

## Before a stone was laid

Look at the order of the book's first chapter. The Lord's charge comes first, with a promise attached: “build the house; and I will take pleasure in it, and I will be glorified” ([[Hag. 1:8]]). Then the people “obeyed the voice of the LORD their God… and the people did fear before the LORD” ([[Hag. 1:12]]). Then comes the whole of the Lord's reply to their turning, one sentence: “I am with you, saith the LORD” ([[Hag. 1:13]]). Only after that does the text say that “the LORD stirred up the spirit” of governor, priest and people, “and they came and did work” ([[Hag. 1:14]]).

The sequence is the text's and the observation is mine: “I am with you” answers their turning and stands before the work, not after it. Keil reads the verses the same way. The Lord met the people's penitence “with the promise of His assistance, in order to elevate this disposition into determination and deed,” and fulfilled the promise “first of all by giving to Zerubbabel, Joshua, and the people, a willingness to carry out the work.”[@keil-haggai] The willingness was given. The presence came first, and the building came out of it.

The second chapter keeps the order. When the new house looked “as nothing” beside the old ([[Hag. 2:3]]), the answer was not a better house but the same presence: “work: for I am with you, saith the LORD of hosts: According to the word that I covenanted with you when ye came out of Egypt, so my spirit remaineth among you” ([[Hag. 2:4–5]]). “Remaineth” is a participle that means “standing”;[@oshb-wlc] His Spirit stands in the midst of them, now, while the walls are half built. Then a promise for the house itself: “I will fill this house with glory” ([[Hag. 2:7]]), and Huntington reads it as the Lord's own coming, “Jehovah Himself would personally visit the temple and fill it with His glory.”[@huntington-consider-ways] The Church's heading reads the verse of the Messiah.[@lds-scriptures]

And then, after the two questions and their diagnosis, the blessing. “Is the seed yet in the barn? yea, as yet the vine, and the fig tree, and the pomegranate, and the olive tree, hath not brought forth: from this day will I bless you” ([[Hag. 2:19]]). The blessing is dated to a day in winter, three months into the work, with nothing in the barn and nothing on the trees. Keil reads the day as “the commencement of a new era,”[@keil-haggai] and the *Minute* notes that the blessings “perhaps would not come immediately.”[@breitenstein-haggai-minute] The people had begun to build, and the promise comes before the harvest, as “I am with you” came before the work.

## The Holy One of God

In the Gospels the presence comes in person, and an unclean spirit is the first to name it: “I know thee who thou art, the Holy One of God” ([[Mark 1:24]]). What the law had kept apart, the holy and the unclean, now meet in the street, and the meetings run the priests' rule backward.

A leper comes to Him. Under the law a leper was the unclean man par excellence, required to cry “Unclean, unclean” and to dwell alone ([[Lev. 13:45–46]]), and, as the Church's manual says, “considered to spread their uncleanness to anyone who came in contact with them.” The manual calls it “remarkable that Jesus touched this leper while healing him.”[@nt-student-manual-11] He “put forth his hand, and touched him, and saith unto him, I will; be thou clean. And as soon as he had spoken, immediately the leprosy departed from him” ([[Mark 1:41–42]]). Elder González has described what that touch would have been to a man whom no one touched.[@gonzalez-saviors-touch] Then the Lord sends him to the priest, to offer what Moses commanded ([[Mark 1:44]]). The law stands; the man is clean.

At Nain He meets a funeral, and “came and touched the bier” ([[Luke 7:14]]). This is the uncleanness of Haggai's second case, contact with the dead, which Keil calls “one of the strongest kinds.”[@keil-haggai] S. Kent Brown does not soften it: “His act of touching the dead brings ritual uncleanness upon Him.” But “as His actions show, the stain of ritual uncleanness does not adhere to Him as it does to others.”[@brown-nt-minute-luke] The dead man sat up ([[Luke 7:15]]). The chain that Numbers 19 said would run from the corpse to whoever touched it ran the other way.

And then the woman. She had had an issue of blood for twelve years, and the Church's footnote at the verse sends the reader to Leviticus 15[@lds-scriptures], the law that made her, and whoever touched her, unclean. She “came behind him, and touched the border of his garment: and immediately her issue of blood stanched” ([[Luke 8:44]]). In the New Testament the Greek word for that border stands for the *ṣîṣit*, the tassel the law required on the corners of every garment[@thayer], and the Bible Dictionary says it was “a tassel at each ‘wing’ or corner” of the mantle.[@bd-hem-of-garment] The corner it hung from is named, in the law of the tassels, by the Hebrew word *kānāp*[@oshb-wlc], the “skirt” of Haggai 2:12. So she reached for the corner of a garment, the place Haggai's priests had been asked about, and the priests had said that nothing passes that way. What happened next was not what the rule described. Jesus said, “Somebody hath touched me: for I perceive that virtue is gone out of me” ([[Luke 8:46]]), and the thing that went out of Him was not holiness lodged in a garment but power, drawn by her faith. Julie Smith notes the inversion on the law's own terms: her touch, “which under the law of Moses should have rendered Jesus ritually unclean, instead renders the woman healed.”[@smith-marks-unique-contribution] Brown reaches for Isaiah, who stood in the temple a man of unclean lips while the hem of the Lord's robe filled it, and was cleansed by a coal from the altar: she “is made clean, as the prophet is, by a touch of the holy.”[@brown-nt-minute-luke]

I know of no commentary that sets these three touches beside Haggai's two questions, and the comparison is mine. But the terms are the text's. Haggai asked about the fold of a garment, and about the dead, and he got a no and a yes. The Gospels show the corner of a garment and a bier, and in each the uncleanness that should have passed to Him did not, and cleansing went the other way, not because the law was wrong but because of who He was.

## What the touch shows

Two cautions keep the reading honest.

The first is that the power was not in the cloth. The Church's manual says the Savior's reply, “thy faith hath made thee whole,” helped the woman and the crowd “avoid the misconception that the miracle had resulted from any miraculous power in His garment itself,” and adds that “God's power can restore both purity and wholeness.”[@nt-student-manual-12] That is right, and the essay rests on it. Haggai's priests had said that holiness does not pass through a garment to a third thing, and nothing in the Gospel story says otherwise. What passed to the woman did not come from the tassel. It came, as He said, out of Him, and it was drawn by faith. The garment's corner was where her faith reached Him, not the source of what she received.

The second is that the law was not abolished by these touches. The leper was sent to the priest. The Bible Dictionary says of the whole system of clean and unclean that it was ceremonial, “introduced in the law of carnal commandments,” and that “being fulfilled by the Atonement of Jesus Christ,” these rules “are no longer required of the believers.”[@bd-clean-unclean] Hebrews says how far the old cleansings reached and how far His does: “the ashes of an heifer sprinkling the unclean, sanctifieth to the purifying of the flesh: How much more shall the blood of Christ… purge your conscience” ([[Heb. 9:13–14]]). Brown puts the matter of His person plainly: He “respects and honors the law,” but “it does not exercise any claim on Him. Instead, His divine authority simply overpowers aspects of ritual uncleanness.”[@brown-nt-minute-luke]

Put the two cautions together and the reading sharpens rather than weakens. The priests' rule described what holy things can do, and holy things can pass holiness one step and no further. The law, in Brown's words, has no claim on Him, and I take the reason to be the one the unclean spirit gave. He is not a holy thing. He is the Holy One, and holiness is not something He carries in a fold; it is what He is. Uncleanness that would pass to anyone else “does not adhere to Him,”[@brown-nt-minute-luke] and what goes out of Him to the unclean is not contagion but cleansing. Haggai's book had already shown the direction. The people's holy things could not make them holy; the Lord's presence came to them when they turned, before they had built anything, and made the building possible. The Gospels show the same presence walking into a crowd of the unclean.

## Where holiness comes from

The lesson Haggai's priests gave is still the lesson. A person is not made holy by nearness to holy things. The Church's Seminary manual says of these very verses that “attending the temple does not sanctify someone who is unworthy.”[@ot-seminary-resource-haggai] The altar alone did not sanctify Haggai's people, and the land alone did not. Elder Bednar says it of everything we bring: “All of our worthy desires and good works, as necessary as they are, can never produce clean hands and a pure heart. It is the Atonement of Jesus Christ that provides both a cleansing and redeeming power.”[@bednar-clean-hands] The Guide to the Scriptures defines sanctification as “the process of becoming free from sin, pure, clean, and holy through the Atonement of Jesus Christ.”[@gs-sanctification] The leper and the woman were made clean, not holy, and the cleansing the law gave was of the flesh. But Haggai himself moved from the law's rule to the state of “this people” before the Lord, and I take the same step: the cleansing that came to them from Him is the picture of the holiness that comes to a person from Him.

And the order of Haggai is still the order. A disciple who turns to the Lord is doing what Haggai's people did when they obeyed and feared, and the Lord's part is what it was then. “I am with you” was said to a people who had turned but had not yet laid a stone, and what came of it, Keil says, was the willingness to build. Elder Bednar says the Atonement supplies “a sanctifying and strengthening power that helps us to become better than we ever could by relying only upon our own strength.”[@bednar-clean-hands] The disciple's part is real, as the Seminary manual's heading says, “The Lord requires us to be worthy”;[@ot-seminary-resource-haggai] but the holiness that results is His and not the work's. The woman's part was to reach for Him in faith; the power that healed her was not hers. Moroni gives both halves in one verse, the reaching and the gift: “if ye by the grace of God are perfect in Christ, and deny not his power, then are ye sanctified in Christ by the grace of God, through the shedding of the blood of Christ, which is in the covenant of the Father unto the remission of your sins, that ye become holy, without spot” ([[Moro. 10:33]]).

The house Haggai's people built was finished in the sixth year of Darius ([[Ezra 6:15]]). The Church's heading on chapter 2 reads the book's promise of the Messiah[@lds-scriptures], and Huntington reads the Lord's “personal visits to the temple” in His ministry as the glory Haggai promised the house.[@huntington-consider-ways] President Nelson's promise about the Lord's houses now is a promise of the same kind: “Every sincere seeker of Jesus Christ will find Him in the temple.”[@nelson-come-again] Not holiness in the stones. Him.
