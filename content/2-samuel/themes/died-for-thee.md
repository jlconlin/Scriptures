Near the end of Absalom's revolt the king of Israel sits between the gates of Mahanaim and asks the two runners from the battle the same question: “Is the young man Absalom safe?” The second runner answers with a wish that the king's enemies might be as that young man is, and David goes up into the chamber over the gate. “O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! would God I had died for thee, O Absalom, my son, my son!” ([[2 Sam. 18:33]])

The Church's leaders have quoted that cry for what it plainly is, a father's grief over a rebel child.[@hinckley-what-god-hath-joined][@faust-father-come-home] It is that. But I want to read it as one of three, because three times in 2 Samuel the king asks for another's life, and each time in stronger words. For a dying child he “besought God for the child” ([[2 Sam. 12:16]]). For Absalom he wishes he had died “for thee,” which in Hebrew is “in the place of” thee. For a plagued people he asks the LORD outright, “let thine hand, I pray thee, be against me” ([[2 Sam. 24:17]]). A plea, a wish, an offer. The child dies. The wish for Absalom comes after Absalom is dead. And the people, in the end, are spared, though not by the king's life.

The book has also told us, twice, whose life this is. David's men will not let him go out to battle, because “thou art worth ten thousand of us” ([[2 Sam. 18:3]]), and because his life is “the light of Israel” ([[2 Sam. 21:17]]). The one life Israel would not spend is the one life the king keeps trying to spend.

No source I have found joins these passages, and the reading that follows is mine. Here is the claim. 2 Samuel shows its best king willing, three times over, to stand between death and the people he loved, and shows that his life was never the thing that saved them; and in each scene the text has already traced the death to the king's own sin. When the Son of David says that the good shepherd “giveth his life for the sheep” ([[John 10:11]]), He is doing what the shepherd of 2 Samuel 24 asked to do. The book has already shown that the shepherd who would die for his sheep was himself one of the sheep who needed Him.

## Worth ten thousand of us

Before the battle in the wood of Ephraim, David counts his men, divides them into three companies, and tells them, “I will surely go forth with you myself also” ([[2 Sam. 18:2]]). The people refuse him:

> Thou shalt not go forth: for if we flee away, they will not care for us; neither if half of us die, will they care for us: but now thou art worth ten thousand of us: therefore now it is better that thou succour us out of the city. ([[2 Sam. 18:3]])

They are not flattering him. They are counting. Half of them can fall and the enemy will not care; the king is the one loss that would count. So the king stays by the gate, and the one thing he sends into the battle in his place is a command, given in the hearing of everyone: “Deal gently for my sake with the young man, even with Absalom” ([[2 Sam. 18:5]]).

The book says the same thing a second time, in its closing chapters. After Abishai rescues David from a giant's son, “the men of David sware unto him, saying, Thou shalt go no more out with us to battle, that thou quench not the light of Israel” ([[2 Sam. 21:17]]). The two passages belong together, and the Church's footnote at the second joins them to the first;[@lds-scriptures] whichever oath was sworn first, the book sets both before its reader. Twice, then, Israel decides that the king's life is the one life that must not be risked.

There is nothing unusual in soldiers who will die for a king. Paul takes it as a known thing about men: “scarcely for a righteous man will one die: yet peradventure for a good man some would even dare to die” ([[Rom. 5:7]]). David's men are Paul's “some.” What is unusual is what the king does with the life they guard, and that is the rest of this essay.

## For the child

The first of the three scenes comes before the revolt. Nathan has told David that the child born of Bath-sheba “shall surely die” ([[2 Sam. 12:14]]), and the LORD strikes the child with sickness. Then:

> David therefore besought God for the child; and David fasted, and went in, and lay all night upon the earth. ([[2 Sam. 12:16]])

“For the child” is the Hebrew preposition *bĕʿad*,[@oshb-wlc] which the lexicon glosses “on behalf of” and lists this verse among its uses after verbs of entreating.[@bdb] He is pleading on the child's behalf. He keeps it up for seven days, and when the child dies and his servants cannot understand why he rises and eats, he tells them what the pleading had been: “Who can tell whether GOD will be gracious to me, that the child may live?” And then the limit: “can I bring him back again? I shall go to him, but he shall not return to me” ([[2 Sam. 12:22–23]]).

Two things should be said plainly about this scene. The first is that David offers nothing here but prayer; this is the weakest of the three, a plea and not yet a wish to take the child's place. The second is that the child's death was not a substitution, and this essay does not read it as one. The Church's study help is clear that infants who die “are not held accountable for their parents' actions,”[@scripture-helps-2-samuel-11] and the law of Ezekiel is that “the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father” ([[Ezek. 18:20]]). (The note “Why the child died, and where he went” on [chapter 12](/2-samuel/12/) gives the sources.)

The text gives a reason, and it concerns David and not the child: “because by this deed thou hast given great occasion to the enemies of the LORD to blaspheme, the child also that is born unto thee shall surely die” ([[2 Sam. 12:14]]). The death David lies on the ground to avert is a death his own deed brought on. He is praying against the consequence of himself. The same will be true of the other two scenes.

## Instead of thee

Nathan's sentence had a second half: “I will raise up evil against thee out of thine own house” ([[2 Sam. 12:11]]). Absalom is the evil out of his own house. The son who murdered a brother, stole the hearts of Israel and drove his father over the Jordan is the son David asked his captains to deal gently with, and the son Joab killed in the oak against the king's word. Then the cry in the chamber over the gate.

Its Hebrew is worth hearing. *Mî yittēn mûtî ʾănî taḥtêkā*: who would give my dying, I, in the place of thee.[@oshb-wlc] The word rendered “for thee” is *taḥat*, an ordinary word that the book uses elsewhere of one man succeeding another ([[2 Sam. 10:1]]; [[2 Sam. 17:25]]); here the lexicon reads it “in place of, instead of,” the sense it also has when the ram is offered “in the stead of his son” ([[Gen. 22:13]]) and when Judah asks to abide “instead of the lad” ([[Gen. 44:33]]),[@bdb] two verses the site's Genesis essay [One in Another's Place](/genesis/themes/in-anothers-place/) argues. The plain sense of David's wish is to have died in his son's place.

Readers have heard the cry differently. One hearing is “the excessive tenderness, or rather weakness, of David's paternal affection,” which kept him from seeing the justice of God in his son's death; that is Keil's.[@keil-1-samuel] The opposite hearing is that the cry may be one glimpse of why God called David a man after His own heart, since latter-day scripture shows God Himself grieving as a parent over children who reject Him; the note “O my son Absalom” on [chapter 18](/2-samuel/18/) quotes it.[@taggart-sworn-david] B. H. Roberts went further and heard a “soul cry” of one who identifies with Christ: “The more one identifies with Christ, the more one identifies with those who are his and also those who refuse thus far to become his.”[@madsen-roberts-atonement] I side with the second and third. The love in the cry is the right kind of love.

But the text adds something all three readers pass over, which is that the wish is impossible at the moment it is spoken. Absalom is already dead. David's command to deal gently was his one attempt to stand between his son and the sword, and it was sent in his place because he had been kept from the field; Joab heard it and killed the young man anyway. What is left is a wish after the fact. What David would have given at the climax, as Rasmussen puts it in the Student Manual, would have been needed much sooner.[@ot-manual-2-samuel-13]

The next chapter names what the wish amounted to. Joab, whose army had that day saved the king's life and his household, comes to the chamber and says it to his face:

> In that thou lovest thine enemies, and hatest thy friends. For thou hast declared this day, that thou regardest neither princes nor servants: for this day I perceive, that if Absalom had lived, and all we had died this day, then it had pleased thee well. ([[2 Sam. 19:6]])

Joab means it as an accusation, and his counsel, in the Student Manual's judgment, “was needed and justified.”[@ot-manual-2-samuel-13] A king cannot prefer his enemy's life to all his friends'. But look at what Joab has put into words. On his account the king would have had every one of his friends die so that his enemy might live. In a king that is a fault. Keep the sentence; Paul will have something to say about it.

## Against me

The last scene is the book's last chapter. David numbers Israel, and before any prophet speaks his own heart smites him: “I have sinned greatly in that I have done” ([[2 Sam. 24:10]]). Offered three judgments, he chooses the one that comes straight from God, “let us fall now into the hand of the LORD; for his mercies are great: and let me not fall into the hand of man” ([[2 Sam. 24:14]]). The pestilence runs from Dan to Beer-sheba. At Jerusalem the angel's hand is stayed: “the LORD repented him of the evil, and said to the angel that destroyed the people, It is enough: stay now thine hand” ([[2 Sam. 24:16]]). And then the king speaks:

> Lo, I have sinned, and I have done wickedly: but these sheep, what have they done? let thine hand, I pray thee, be against me, and against my father's house. ([[2 Sam. 24:17]])

Chronicles gives the other half of the sentence: “but not on thy people, that they should be plagued” ([[1 Chr. 21:17]]).

Set the three scenes in a row and the prepositions step up. In chapter 12 he pleads *for* the child, on his behalf. In chapter 18 he wishes he had died *instead of* his son. In chapter 24 he asks that the hand be *against* him.[@oshb-wlc] A plea, a wish, an offer: and this time the offer is made to God, in the moment, with the angel in sight.

Now notice the order of the verses, because it matters to what the essay can claim. In the order both tellings give, “It is enough” comes before the offer, yet when David speaks the angel is still “the angel that smote the people.” The text never says the offer was refused, and it never says the hand fell on the king. It says what the LORD commanded. Gad comes that day: “Go up, rear an altar unto the LORD in the threshingfloor of Araunah the Jebusite” ([[2 Sam. 24:18]]). David goes up, will not take the floor as a gift, “neither will I offer burnt offerings unto the LORD my God of that which doth cost me nothing” ([[2 Sam. 24:24]]), builds the altar, offers, and only then does the chapter say, “So the LORD was entreated for the land, and the plague was stayed from Israel” ([[2 Sam. 24:25]]). Chronicles puts the sword away at the same point: after the offering, “the LORD commanded the angel; and he put up his sword again into the sheath thereof” ([[1 Chr. 21:27]]). So the half of the prayer that Chronicles records, “not on thy people,” was granted. The people were spared. What spared them was not the king's life but an altar and an offering that cost him something. The text gives no reason why the one and not the other, and I will give none.

One qualification matters: the sheep were not simply innocent. The chapter opens, “again the anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel” ([[2 Sam. 24:1]]). The nation was innocent “not indeed free from every kind of blame, but only from the sin which God was punishing by the pestilence,” as Keil puts it,[@keil-1-samuel] and it would be wrong to infer from David's prayer that the people “had had to atone only for an act of transgression on the part of their king.”[@keil-1-samuel] But the census was David's, and David says so: “I have sinned greatly.” For the third time, the death David would take on himself is one his own act had brought on.

What spared the people was an offering that cost the king something, and here is the reading I offer. The king asked to be the offering; he was told to bring one. And the burnt offering, as the angel explained it to Adam, was “a similitude of the sacrifice of the Only Begotten of the Father” ([[Moses 5:7]]), and the Witness of Christ on [chapter 24](/2-samuel/24/) reads David's burnt offerings by that verse. On that reading the altar was not a smaller answer than the one David asked for. It pointed at the one Offering that would be accepted for the sheep, and the king who had asked to be the offering brought its figure instead, at a price.

## Why the king could not

Put the three scenes together and something appears that no one chapter shows. Each time, David's love is of the right kind: he would have given anything, and in the last two scenes he says so, himself. And each time the text has already told us where the death came from. “By this deed” ([[2 Sam. 12:14]]). “Out of thine own house” ([[2 Sam. 12:11]]). “I have sinned greatly” ([[2 Sam. 24:10]]). The one who asks to be struck is, in each scene, the one whose act brought the blow.

It is worth being exact about what a mortal can and cannot do here, because the book shows both. A man can die in another man's place. David's soldiers would have done it for him; Paul says some would “even dare to die” for a good man ([[Rom. 5:7]]); and Moses, who had no part in the calf, asked to be blotted out of the book for Israel's sake ([[Ex. 32:32]]). What no man can do is atone. Amulek sets the limit: “there is not any man that can sacrifice his own blood which will atone for the sins of another” ([[Alma 34:11]]), because what sin requires is “not a sacrifice of man, neither of beast,” but “an infinite and eternal sacrifice,” and “that great and last sacrifice will be the Son of God, yea, infinite and eternal” ([[Alma 34:10]]; [[Alma 34:14]]). The Bible Dictionary says the same of who could make it: Jesus Christ, “as the Only Begotten Son of God and the only sinless person to live on this earth, was the only one capable of making an atonement for mankind.”[@bd-atonement] And the Good Shepherd names the power that no mortal shepherd has: “I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again” ([[John 10:18]]). On Amulek's terms, no mortal's death, however willing, could have done what David's sheep needed, not even a sinless one's.

So the three scenes do not show a king who lacked a quality the Savior had. They show, I think, something humbler and more useful. The shepherd who would have died for his sheep was himself one of the sheep who needed the Shepherd. Peter's phrase for the Atonement is “the just for the unjust” ([[1 Pet. 3:18]]), and in these scenes David stands among the unjust, as his confession in the census account makes plain. Elder Oaks says it of all of us, reading the story of the ram: “Because of our sins and our mortality, we, like Isaac, are condemned to death.”[@oaks-1992-bible-stories] What David's story adds is a man who, standing there, wanted with his whole heart to be the ram, and whose own sons and people were the ones his sin had brought death upon. That reading is mine. It takes nothing from David's love; it says only whose side of the transaction he was on.

## The Good Shepherd

“These sheep,” David says at Araunah's floor, because the LORD had taken him “from following the sheep” to rule His people ([[2 Sam. 7:8]]). The shepherd-king became Israel's hope, Ezekiel's one shepherd, “my servant David,” and Jesus, coming as the Good Shepherd, fulfils that hope of a king who feeds and cares for his people;[@taggart-sworn-david] the note “These sheep” on [chapter 24](/2-samuel/24/) quotes the reading.

But listen to what He says the good shepherd does. “I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep” ([[John 10:11]]). “I lay down my life for the sheep” ([[John 10:15]]). “No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself” ([[John 10:18]]). That is what David asked for at the threshing floor, and could only ask for. The step from the one to the other is mine, but the acts are on both pages. The shepherd who said “let thine hand be against me” and was told to rear an altar is answered by the Shepherd who said “I lay it down of myself” and brought nothing in His place.

He was of David's house. Gabriel's word to Mary is that “the Lord God shall give unto him the throne of his father David” ([[Luke 1:32]]). David had asked that the hand fall “against me, and against my father's house,” and what came of that house, as Matthew Bowen notices, is this: “Jesus, one of David's ‘father's house,’ later suffers and dies very near this site both for David's sins and the sins of all humanity.”[@bowen-mount-lord-provided] I would put it with care, since the Atonement is no punishment laid on David's line. What came of the house of David was not the blow the king asked for. It was the gift the Father gave: “God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son” ([[John 3:16]]).

Now hear Joab's accusation again, next to Paul. Joab's complaint is that the king would have let all his friends die so that one who hated him might live. Paul: “scarcely for a righteous man will one die… But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us… when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son” ([[Rom. 5:7–8]]; [[Rom. 5:10]]). What Joab named as a fault in a king, Paul names as the love of God. The Cushite had already called Absalom what he was, “The enemies of my lord the king, and all that rise against thee to do thee hurt, be as that young man is” ([[2 Sam. 18:32]]); we were God's. David could wish, after the fact, that he had died for his enemy. Christ did it, and for His. I know of no source that sets Joab's sentence beside Paul's, and the pairing is mine.

The Lord has described the doing of it in His own voice. “I, God, have suffered these things for all, that they might not suffer if they would repent” ([[D&C 19:16]]). Christ, too, would have had it otherwise: He “would that I might not drink the bitter cup, and shrink—Nevertheless, glory be to the Father, and I partook” ([[D&C 19:18–19]]). The king and the Son were alike in willingness. What the Son had, and the king did not, is what Amulek and the Bible Dictionary name: a sacrifice infinite and eternal, made by the sinless Son of God, with power to lay His life down and take it again.

One last thing about the ground. Chronicles says that Solomon built the temple “in mount Moriah, where the LORD appeared unto David his father, in the place that David had prepared in the threshingfloor of Ornan the Jebusite” ([[2 Chr. 3:1]]). The name Moriah occurs only there and in Genesis 22,[@bowen-mount-lord-provided] and the identification of the two is “a tradition first recorded by the Jewish historian Josephus.”[@bd-moriah] It has been questioned,[@cambridge-1-samuel] and I do not claim that the two altars stood on one spot. But the Chronicler, naming the ground, set David's altar under the name of the place where a father was given a ram “in the stead of his son” ([[Gen. 22:13]]). Abraham did not supply that ram from his flock, and David did not supply a substitute from himself. Elder Oaks says who supplies it: the story “shows the goodness of God in protecting Isaac and in providing a substitute so he would not have to die,” and “when all other hope is gone, our Father in Heaven provides the Lamb of God, and we are saved by his sacrifice.”[@oaks-1992-bible-stories]

## What we bring

A parent who would die for a wandering child has David's wish and David's limit. That is exactly where the Church has taken David's cry. President Faust quoted it to parents whose children have rebelled, saying first that “the grief of a parent over a rebellious child is almost inconsolable,” and then, to those parents, “the Good Shepherd is watching over them. God knows and understands your deep sorrow. There is hope.”[@faust-dear-sheep] What the arc adds to that is the limit, and the limit is good news. The rescue is not the parent's to make, and the parent, like David, is among those it was made for. No one has traveled beyond the reach of divine love, as Elder Holland testifies in the words the Witness of Christ on [chapter 18](/2-samuel/18/) quotes.[@holland-laborers] The reach is not a figure of speech. Alma says the Savior took on Himself the infirmities of His people so “that he may know according to the flesh how to succor his people” ([[Alma 7:12]]): He knows the way to where the wanderer is because He has been there, which no parent, and no king, can say.

And what a disciple brings is what David brought at the end: not himself as a substitute for anyone, since no mortal can be that, but an offering that costs him something, brought to the One who was. “Come unto him, and offer your whole souls as an offering unto him” ([[Omni 1:26]]). What in that offering is actually ours to give, Elder Maxwell said, is one thing: “the submission of one's will is really the only uniquely personal thing we have to place on God's altar.”[@maxwell-swallowed-up]

David asked, at the gate of Mahanaim, to have died in the place of a son who had made himself his enemy. The Son of David did that, for every one of us, David among them, and laid down His life of Himself. What the king wished for is the thing the Savior is.
