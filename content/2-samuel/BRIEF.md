## What a chapter is for here

2 Samuel is 695 verses in 24 chapters: David's reign, his sin, and what it cost his house. Questions a careful reader would ask, by chapter (a start, not a limit):

- **1–4:** *Did the Amalekite kill Saul, when 1 Samuel 31 says Saul fell on his own sword? Why did David kill him? What is the book of Jasher? What did David mean by Jonathan's love “passing the love of women”? Why two kingdoms for seven years? Why did Joab kill Abner, and was David sincere in mourning him? Why did David demand Michal back? Why kill the men who brought Ish-bosheth's head?*
- **5–7:** *How did David take Jerusalem, and what are “the lame and the blind” (5:8)? Was Jerusalem Salem? Were David's many wives approved? Why was Uzzah struck, and why did David dance? What did Michal object to, and why was she childless? What is the “house” the LORD promised David, and is “for ever” conditional? Who is the son of 7:14 who will be chastened? How is Christ the Son of David?*
- **8–10:** *Were David's sons priests (8:18)? Why the kindness to Mephibosheth, and why “a dead dog”? Why did Hanun shame the envoys?*
- **11–12:** *Why was David at home “at the time when kings go forth to battle”? Was Bath-sheba to blame, and what does “purified from her uncleanness” mean? Why did Uriah not go home? What did Nathan's parable do? Did the LORD give David his wives (12:8)? Was David forgiven, given Psalm 51, D&C 132:39 and the Joseph Smith Translation? Why did the child die, and where is he? What is Jedidiah?*
- **13–14:** *What did Amnon do, and why did David do nothing? Why did Absalom wait two years? What did the woman of Tekoa's story do?*
- **15–19:** *How did Absalom steal hearts? Why did David leave Jerusalem, over the Kidron and up the Mount of Olives weeping? Was Ziba or Mephibosheth lying? Why did David let Shimei curse? Why did Absalom go in to the concubines? Why did Ahithophel hang himself? Why did Joab kill Absalom against the king's order, and why did David's grief anger him?*
- **20–24:** *Who was Sheba? Why were Saul's sons hanged for the Gibeonites, and what did Rizpah do? Who killed Goliath, David or Elhanan (21:19)? How does chapter 22 differ from Psalm 18? What are David's last words? Who were the mighty men, and why is Uriah among them? Why was the census a sin, and who moved David to it, the LORD or Satan? Why the threshing floor of Araunah?*

A chapter answers those from the text first. (The `christ` section then applies the chapter to the reader, in statements: `STANDARDS.md` §1.)

**Read the story as a story.** Nathan's sentence (12:10–12) is carried out scene by scene: the sword in David's house (Amnon, 13:28–29; Absalom, 18:14–15; Adonijah after), and the wives taken “in the sight of this sun” (16:22). David's “he shall surely die” over the parable's rich man (12:5) returns on the child (12:14, 18). David weeps for Abner (3:32), for the child before he dies (12:22), for Amnon (13:36–37) and for Absalom (18:33). Say what such a pattern shows where a source shows it (Taggart [25]: after the parable “David lives under a sort of curse”); don't build a scheme of the writer's own.

**Twenty-four chapters, one great promise.** Chapter 7 is the center of the book and of much of the Old Testament on the site; its notes are full, and later notes point to them. Chapter 22 is Psalm 18, already written: say only how the two differ. Chapter 23:8–39 is a list: one note on who the mighty men were and why Uriah and Eliam are on it (Shannon [5]), not a note per name.

## Book-wide conventions

1 Samuel's brief holds the shared ones (`when`, names of God, Hebrew, the Joseph Smith Translation, large numbers, scripture that reads Samuel, the guides, theme candidates). For 2 Samuel:

- **`when`.** From the text: “After the death of Saul” (1:1); “Seven years and six months in Hebron” (2:11; 5:5); “At the time when kings go forth to battle” (11:1); “After forty years” (15:7: the Hebrew has forty; some ancient versions four, a lead for a note only if a source says so); no B.C. year. The guide's dates (1055 Hebron; 1047 Jerusalem, “or about 1000”) may be given in chapter 2's and 5's `setting`, as the guide's.
- **Hebrew words that pay**, besides 1 Samuel's: *bayit*, “house” (7:2, 5–7, 11, 13, 16, 27: a palace, a temple and a dynasty; *Come, Follow Me*: “a ‘house,’ meaning a posterity,” `cfm-ot-2026-25` [23]); *ḥesed* (2:5–6; 3:8; 7:15; 9:1, 3, 7; 10:2; 22:51; Taggart); *ʿānâ*, “force” (13:12, 14, 22, 32); Jedidiah, “beloved of the LORD” (12:25; check the footnote and BDB); *kōhănîm* (8:18, “chief rulers,” Hebrew “priests”); Bath-sheba's name only if BDB gives it (Claybaugh's “daughter of the covenant” is unsupported).
- **The Joseph Smith Translation.** The Gospel Library prints it at 12:13 (`jst-2-sam-12`: “The Lord also hath not put away thy sin that thou shalt not die”; headnote: “David's grievous sin is not put away by God”) and 24:16 (“for the people repented”). There is **no JST of 24:1**; its footnote reads: “Apparently something is missing, and ‘Satan’ should be the antecedent of ‘he.’ See 1 Chr. 21:1.”
- **Chronicles tells much of it again** (1 Chr. 11–21): a note gives Chronicles' wording where it answers the reader's question (24:1; 21:19; 5:4–5), as the parallel, not as a correction.

## Contested and sensitive questions

1 Samuel's brief holds the shared ones (the writer; the text of Samuel; David and Jonathan, which governs 1:26). Quotations were copied from the pages named on 2026-11-04; re-read the page before using one.

- **Saul's death, told twice (1:1–16).** 1 Sam. 31:4–5: Saul falls on his own sword. The Amalekite says he killed Saul at Saul's request and brings the crown (1:6–10). The Student Manual relays Clarke: the account “is a fabrication”; he took the crown from the field and lied “to ingratiate himself with David” (`ot-manual-2-samuel-1` [15]). **Proposed:** 1:10 sets the two accounts side by side, gives the manual's reading as the usual answer, and says what the text gives as David's reason for the sentence (1:14, 16, “the LORD's anointed”; 4:10 recalls it). No verdict on the Amalekite beyond the text's.
- **David's grief and his politics (1:17–27; 3:31–39; 18:33).** Taggart: “in his public displays of grief one could perceive political cunning or sincere bereavement” (`taggart-sworn-david` [22]); the text says “all the people … understood that day that it was not of the king to slay Abner” (3:37). **Proposed:** the notes say what David did and what the text says it achieved; Taggart's two readings as his, once; no note calls David calculating or sincere in the writer's voice.
- **“The lame and the blind” (5:6–8).** The verse is obscure in Hebrew. Calabro reads it with 4QSam-a (“David's soul hates”) as condemning whoever kills a Jebusite to reach the water shaft, “or (who slays) the lame or the blind,” so that 5:8 and 9:3–7 “present the cultural hero David as a role model of the compassionate ideal” (`calabro-disability` [67], [79]–[81]); he gives his rendering as his own, “closest to the original” [80]. **Proposed:** 5:8 gives the King James, that the Hebrew is hard, and Calabro's reading as his, with the link to Mephibosheth (9:3, 13) that he draws. No note says David hated the disabled.
- **David's wives and D&C 132 (3:2–5; 5:13; 12:8; 15:16; 16:22).** *Scripture Helps*: “Latter-day revelation affirms that the Lord approved of David's concubines and wives, who were given by the hand of the prophet Nathan—except in the case of Bathsheba” (`scripture-helps-1-samuel-17` [25], citing D&C 132:38–39). D&C 132:39: “David's wives and concubines were given unto him of me, by the hand of Nathan, my servant, and others of the prophets who had the keys of this power; and in none of these things did he sin against me save in the case of Uriah and his wife.” Jacob 2:24 calls David's and Solomon's many wives “abominable.” The *Encyclopedia of Mormonism*: “David's blessings, including his wives, were given to him as a result of God's favor”; “But when David also acquired wives and concubines, apparently under his own authority, he was condemned by God (Jacob 2:23-24)” (`eom-david-king` [6]–[7]); Sears and Shannon on how D&C 132 “changes and expands what the Bible says” (`sears-shannon-polygamy` [45]–[46]). **Proposed:** 12:8 carries it (Nathan's words, D&C 132:39, *Scripture Helps*, Jacob 2:24, and the *Encyclopedia*'s way of joining them, as the *Encyclopedia*'s); the guide *Women and Marriage* (`#plural-marriage`) for the rest: link. **No note defends or criticizes plural marriage beyond its sources**, and none raises the nineteenth-century practice.
- **Uzzah (6:1–11).** *Scripture Helps*: “not even the Levite priests were permitted to come near it,” “any unauthorized person who touched it would die” (Num. 4:15), and Elder Maxwell: “surely He knew how to keep the ark in balance!” (`scripture-helps-1-samuel-17` [27–28]; endnote 25: D&C 85:7–8 and Brigham Young); the footnotes point to 1 Chr. 15:2 (David's own conclusion: “None ought to carry the ark of God but the Levites”) and Num. 1:51. **Proposed:** 6:6–7 says what the law required (Num. 4:15 on the site), that the ark was on a cart, not on the Levites' shoulders, and David's own lesson (1 Chr. 15:2, 13); Elder Maxwell at most once. **No note says the LORD was harsh, or excuses Uzzah, in the writer's voice**; 6:8 (“David was displeased”) and 6:9 (“afraid”) are David's. Uzzah is never called a Levite (Correa does: not in the text).
- **David's dance and Michal (6:14–23).** The text: David “danced before the LORD with all his might … girded with a linen ephod” (6:14); Michal “despised him in her heart” (6:16); “Michal … had no child unto the day of her death” (6:23). The Student Manual relays Keil: “The proud daughter of Saul was offended” (`ot-manual-2-samuel-1` [33]); the seminary manual: she “criticized David's joyous behavior, which had a negative effect on their relationship” (lesson 90 [38]); Correa: “we cannot know the exact reason” (`ot-minute-2-samuel` [24]). **Proposed:** 6:20 gives what each says (6:20–22), the seminary manual's sentence, and Keil's as “one older reading”; 6:23 says what the text says and **does not say her childlessness was a punishment**, since the text does not. Correa's speculation that David held “the priesthood of the kingly priests like Melchizedek” [23] is his and is not used.
- **The covenant with David (7).** The text: “thine house and thy kingdom shall be established for ever before thee: thy throne shall be established for ever” (7:16), and of the son, “If he commit iniquity, I will chasten him … But my mercy shall not depart away from him” (7:14–15). *Scripture Helps*: “the Lord promised to establish David's own house (his throne and kingdom) forever”; Ps. 132:11–12 “adds the important qualification that David's earthly kingdom would endure only if his descendants remained faithful”; the promise “was ultimately fulfilled through Jesus Christ, a descendant of David” (Luke 1:32–33; Rev. 22:16) [29–31]; *Come, Follow Me*: “This promise was fulfilled in Jesus Christ, our Eternal King” [23]; the Student Manual: “a dualistic prophecy … clearly a Messianic prophecy as well … only one person can sit upon the throne of David … forever and ever, and that one is Christ” (`ot-manual-2-samuel-1` [35]–[36], with Talmage); Taggart: “relatively little consideration has been granted it in Latter-day Saint writings” [5]; a “grant” covenant [13]; “Jesus is simultaneously the promisor and the fulfillment of the promise” [37]; David's failure “resulted in a loss of blessings (see Doctrine and Covenants 132:39), but the narrative asserts that God never abandoned David or the covenantal promise” [28]. Joseph Smith: “the throne & kingdom of David is to be taken from him & given to another by the name of David in the last days raised up out of his linage” (`jsp-discourse-1844-03-10-p210`; `eom-david-last-days` [6]). **Proposed:** 7:11–16 carry it in full (*bayit*; the son who builds; “for ever”; conditional or not, from *Scripture Helps* and Ps. 132:12; fulfilled in Christ); the latter-day David in a clause at 7:16, pointing to `[[Ezek. 34:23]]` and `[[Ezek. 37:24]]`, which already explain him. `christ` of chapter 7 is the Son of David. **Ps. 89 and 132 on the site already give the oath and its conditions: agree, don't repeat.**
- **Bath-sheba (11:1–5).** The text: David “saw a woman washing herself”; “David sent messengers, and took her; and she came in unto him, and he lay with her; for she was purified from her uncleanness” (11:2, 4); Nathan's parable makes her the “one little ewe lamb” (12:3). Shannon: “the text does not give us enough information to definitively say anything” about her part; “there was a power differential between him and Bath-sheba which must nuance any discussion of consent”; “The biblical authors do not use any of their words for rape”; “the biblical authors place the blame for the murder of Uriah squarely on the shoulders of David” (`shannon-complex-stories` [6]–[7]); Hock: the narrator “dehumanizes her by omitting a name” (`hock-narrating` [30]); Correa: “the text does not say that Bath-sheba was on a roof,” and the washing was a purification, so she “was not and could not have been pregnant at this time” (`ot-minute-2-samuel` [39]); Claybaugh judges her innocent (his). **Proposed:** 11:2 says what the text says David saw and where (he was on the roof); 11:4 explains “purified from her uncleanness” (Lev. 15:19, 28; Correa's point, which shows the child was David's) and gives Shannon on consent, as his; the guide *Women and Marriage* (Bathsheba) for her later part (1 Kgs. 1–2): link. **No note blames Bath-sheba, calls it rape, or calls it a love story, in the writer's voice.** The text blames David (11:27; 12:9), and so do the notes.
- **David's sin and forgiveness (12:9–14; Ps. 51; D&C 132:39).** The JST of 12:13: “The Lord also hath not put away thy sin that thou shalt not die”; its headnote, “David's grievous sin is not put away by God”; footnote 13b: “HEB hath caused to pass; i.e., he was not punished immediately by death; but he did not escape punishment. See D&C 132:39.” D&C 132:39: “he hath fallen from his exaltation, and received his portion.” The Bible Dictionary: “unlike Saul, he was capable of true contrition and was therefore able to find forgiveness, except in the murder of Uriah. As a consequence David is still unforgiven, but he received a promise that the Lord would not leave his soul in hell” (`bd-david` [1]); *Scripture Helps*: “Although David had ‘fallen from his exaltation’ because he had planned Uriah's death, the Lord promised David that his soul would not be left in hell (spirit prison)” (`scripture-helps-2-samuel-11` [5]); Joseph Smith, 10 March 1844: “David sought repentance at the hand of God carefully with tears but he could ownly get it through Hell, he got a promise that his soul should not be left in Hell” (`jsp-discourse-1844-03-10-p210`; quote the Papers' spelling); 16 May 1841: “Even David, must wait for those times of refreshing” (`wjs-1841-05-16` [5]); President Packer: “Forgiveness does not, however, necessarily assure exaltation, as is the case with David” (`packer-brilliant-morning`, note 15); the Gospel Doctrine manual: “adultery is a serious sin, but David forfeited his exaltation because the Lord held him accountable for the murder of Uriah” (`ot-gospel-doctrine-24` [43]); Nyman: “Beyond this, the eternal status of David has not been revealed” (`nyman-jst-historical` [17]); Taggart quotes Orson Pratt's “how much I do not know” as “a useful model of doctrinal humility” [65]. **Proposed:** 12:13 carries it: the King James, the JST and its footnote, D&C 132:39, the Bible Dictionary and *Scripture Helps*, the Prophet's words, and Nyman's limit, in that order; Psalm 51 on the site already sets the psalm beside the sin: point. **No note says more about David's eternal state than the Church's sources say**; the Bible Dictionary's line that he “will be resurrected at the end of the Millennium” is not repeated without its source and is given, if at all, as the Bible Dictionary's.
- **The child (12:14–23).** *Scripture Helps*: “infants who die are saved in the celestial kingdom and are not held accountable for their parents' actions” (Moro. 8:8; D&C 137:10) [7]; the Student Manual relays Rasmussen: no reason “to look upon that as ‘punishment’ of the child” (`ot-manual-2-samuel-1` [68]–[69]). **Proposed:** 12:23 (“I shall go to him”) gives *Scripture Helps*' sentence; 12:14 says what the text says and points.
- **Amnon and Tamar (13).** Deuteronomy's rule for graphic passages holds: say what the text says, plainly and briefly. The text names it: “being stronger than she, forced her” (13:14); “no such thing ought to be done in Israel: do not thou this folly” (13:12). Easton-Flake: “King David's decision to knowingly ignore Amnon's act of violence against Tamar and to eventually mourn the loss of Amnon's life rather than acknowledge Tamar's suffering is a terrifying example” (`easton-recognizing` [43]); Amnon “only gains access to Tamar through the help of his cousin Jonadab and the unwitting assistance of his father” [42]; the guide *Women and Marriage* already calls it rape: agree. At 13:21 the Septuagint and 4QSam-a add that David would not grieve Amnon, “because he loved him” (Bowen gives the reference, `bowen-faithfulness-ammon` if added: a lead; check the Septuagint on Bible Hub). **Proposed:** 13:14 says what Amnon did in the text's words and that Tamar resisted and spoke (13:12–13, 16); 13:21 says David was angry and did nothing, with Easton-Flake's reading as hers; `christ` of chapter 13 speaks to the one who is wronged, not about Amnon. `[[Gen. 34:7]]` on the site (Dinah, and Jacob's silence): agree.
- **Ziba and Mephibosheth (16:1–4; 19:24–30).** Ziba says Mephibosheth hoped for the kingdom; Mephibosheth says Ziba deceived him; David divides the land. No source read decides it. **Proposed:** 19:26 sets both stories side by side with what the text adds (19:24, he had not dressed his feet “from the day the king departed”), and does not decide.
- **The Gibeonites and Rizpah (21:1–14).** A famine “for Saul, and for his bloody house, because he slew the Gibeonites” (21:1), against Joshua's oath (Josh. 9 on the site); the Gibeonites ask for seven of Saul's sons and hang them “before the LORD” (21:6, 9); Rizpah keeps the birds from them “from the beginning of harvest until water dropped upon them” (21:10). The Student Manual relays Rasmussen: “This is apostate theology, comparable to that of the Canaanite-Baal religions” (`ot-manual-2-samuel-13` [70]–[71]), and that “Michal” at 21:8 “must be a mistake for Merab” (the Bible Dictionary agrees: `bd-michal`); the Hiltons on Rizpah: “her tireless efforts likely continued for six months” (`hilton-motherhood-ot` [75]–[76]); Taggart names the chapter among the tensions in David's story (n. 16). **Proposed:** 21:1 says what the text says (the oath, the famine, the Gibeonites' demand) and gives the Student Manual's judgment as the manual's; 21:10 gives Rizpah's watch and what it moved David to do (21:11–14); no verdict on David or on the LORD in the writer's voice.
- **Elhanan and Goliath (21:19).** The King James: “Elhanan … slew the brother of Goliath the Gittite,” the words “the brother of” in italics, supplied from 1 Chr. 20:5 (“Elhanan … slew Lahmi the brother of Goliath”). The Bible Dictionary: “In 2 Sam. 21:19 the reference is probably to a different man of the same name, or some error may have crept into the text” (`bd-goliath`). **Proposed:** 21:19 gives the King James and its italics, Chronicles' wording, and the Bible Dictionary's two explanations, without choosing.
- **The census (24).** “The anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel, and he moved David” (24:1); 1 Chr. 21:1: “Satan stood up against Israel, and provoked David to number Israel.” The footnote at 24:1 (above); the Student Manual relays Clarke: “God could not be angry with David for numbering the people if he moved him to do it” (`ot-manual-2-samuel-13` [76]); Taggart counts it among the tensions (n. 16). Why a census was sin: Ex. 30:12 on the site (the ransom, “that there be no plague”): agree; Joab's objection (24:3) and David's own confession (24:10). **Proposed:** 24:1 gives the two accounts and the footnote's reading; 24:10 why it was sin, from Ex. 30:12 and the text, without a theory of the writer's own; 24:16 the JST (“the people repented”) and a pointer to `[[1 Sam. 15:11]]`; 24:18–25 the threshing floor, the price (“neither will I offer … of that which doth cost me nothing,” 24:24), and Moriah (`[[Gen. 22:2]]` on the site). The 70,000 is the text's; no figure of the writer's own.
- **Christ in 2 Samuel.** The Son of David on David's throne (7:12–16; Luke 1:32–33; *Scripture Helps*, *Come, Follow Me*); “I will be his father” (7:14; Heb. 1:5); the king who goes out weeping over the Kidron and up the Mount of Olives (15:23, 30; John 18:1 and the Mount of Olives in a clause, only where a source draws it: Stirling's chapter on David as a type in Absalom's revolt is not readable); the shepherd (5:2; 24:17, “these sheep, what have they done?”); the offering that costs (24:24); and the Redeemer who keeps the promise when the king fails (Taggart [28], [37]). **Proposed:** in `christ` and at the notes named, in the source's words. **No chapter makes Absalom, the mule, the oak or the well of Bethlehem a type in the writer's words.**
