Jeremiah is the Old Testament prophet most immediately adjacent to the Book of Mormon’s opening. Lehi began to prophesy “in the commencement of the first year of the reign of Zedekiah” ([[1 Ne. 1:4]]); Jeremiah’s long ministry was already under way. They are not the same voice, but they speak from the same endangered city.

## Two prophets in Jerusalem

Jeremiah left the fullest surviving account of Jerusalem’s final years, and the two prophetic accounts begin with a divine call and commission.[@sc-knowhy-650] Both men warn a resistant people that Jerusalem will be destroyed. The Book of Mormon gives a family’s departure from that crisis; Jeremiah gives its long, public struggle inside the city.

That overlap helps us read both records with better historical imagination. Nephi’s account says that many prophets came among the people, while Jeremiah names some of the pressures—temple confidence, false assurances of peace, fear of Babylon, and hostility to an unwelcome word.

## The brass plates

Nephi says the brass plates contained “many prophecies which have been spoken by the mouth of Jeremiah” ([[1 Ne. 5:13]]). The Bible Dictionary likewise identifies Jeremiah’s prophecies among the material on the plates.[@bd-jeremiah] The phrase is carefully limited: it does not claim that the plates carried the book exactly as we have it, or that Nephi quotes Jeremiah at Isaiah-like length. It does mean the two books belong to the same scriptural world from the beginning.

## Nephi knew Jeremiah had been imprisoned

Nephi tells Laman and Lemuel that the people in Jerusalem sought Jeremiah’s life and had cast him into prison ([[1 Ne. 7:14]]). Jeremiah himself records more than one confinement. The best fit with Nephi’s wording is not certain: one proposal places it in Jehoiakim’s final year and another in the later, detailed imprisonment under Zedekiah.[@sc-knowhy-463]

That uncertainty is worth preserving. What is secure is the shared pattern: Jerusalem rejected prophets who told it what it did not want to hear.

## Jerusalem falls, and Mulek survives

Jeremiah records the capture of Jerusalem and the fate of Zedekiah’s sons ([[Jer. 39]]; [[Jer. 52]]). The Book of Mormon adds the crucial exception: Mulek, a son of Zedekiah, survived and was brought to the promised land ([[Hel. 8:21]]; [[Omni 1:15–16]]).

| Jeremiah’s world | Book of Mormon continuation |
|---|---|
| Zedekiah’s house falls | Mulek survives ([[Hel. 8:21]]) |
| Jerusalem is carried captive | Mulek’s people leave Jerusalem and cross the waters ([[Omni 1:15–16]]) |
| Babylon ends Judah’s independence | Zarahemla preserves a second Israelite colony in the promised land |

The connection is not an escape from Jeremiah’s judgment. It is one of the ways the Lord preserves a remnant while the judgment Jeremiah announced takes place.

## The Restoration reads Jeremiah as scripture

Jeremiah’s call is a major Restoration witness to premortal life and foreordination: “Before I formed thee… I knew thee” ([[Jer. 1:5]]). His new-covenant promise is quoted in the New Testament ([[Heb. 8:8–12]]) and directs readers toward the Lord’s work of writing His law within His people ([[Jer. 31:31–34]]).

The Joseph Smith Translation also clarifies a difficult phrase in the temple sermon. Where the KJV says the Lord will “repent” of evil if the people turn, the JST’s wording preserves the doctrine that God does not repent; men repent (JST Jeremiah 26:13). The warning remains conditional, but God is not fickle.

## A needed restraint

The Restoration gives Jeremiah a larger horizon, especially in the gathering and the covenant. It does not turn every image into a coded prediction of latter-day events. The best reading holds both things together: Jeremiah addressed real Jerusalem in the sixth century B.C., and the same covenant God continues His work of gathering and renewing His people.
