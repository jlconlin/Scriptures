Jeremiah lived through the hinge of Old Testament history: Assyria faded, Babylon rose, and Judah lost Jerusalem and the temple. The disaster he warned of was not distant: he preached through the final forty years before Jerusalem’s fall.[@bd-jeremiah]

The empires of Jeremiah's day (Babylon, Egypt, the last of Assyria), the nations around Judah, and a map of the period are in [Babylon and the Exile](/old-testament/guides/babylon-and-the-exile/), the guide to the whole period. This guide keeps what is Jeremiah's own.

## The story in one paragraph

Judah was a small kingdom caught between Egypt to the southwest and the Mesopotamian empires to the northeast. Josiah’s reform briefly renewed worship, but his successors pursued unstable alliances and resisted Babylon. Nebuchadnezzar’s armies took Jerusalem in stages; under Zedekiah, the city was besieged, breached, and destroyed. The exile that followed was the disaster Jeremiah had spent his ministry warning about.[@bd-captivities] How Judah came to this point, from the division of the kingdom through Assyria's conquest of the north, is told in [A Timeline of the Old Testament](/old-testament/guides/israels-story/#two-kingdoms).

## The kings Jeremiah knew

**Josiah** begins the book’s story. Jeremiah’s earliest prophecies belong to his reign, and the book does not deny that Josiah’s reform was real; it asks whether outward reform reached the heart.

**Jehoiakim** is the king who cuts up and burns Jeremiah’s scroll ([[Jer. 36]]). His reign makes the conflict between a prophet’s word and royal power visible.

**Jehoiachin** (also Jeconiah or Coniah) rules only three months before Babylon carries him away. The names change; the person does not.

**Zedekiah**, Jehoiachin’s uncle, is placed on the throne when Jehoiachin is deposed ([[2 Kgs. 24:17]]). He repeatedly asks Jeremiah for a word, but cannot bring himself to act on it. His reign supplies much of Jeremiah’s narrative drama.

## Places to keep in mind

**Jerusalem** is the center: temple, palace, prison, and besieged city. Jeremiah’s warning that the temple would not protect a people who refused repentance is therefore explosive.

**Anathoth**, Jeremiah’s priestly hometown northeast of Jerusalem, is where he buys a field during the siege—his stubborn sign that a future remains.[@bd-jeremiah]

**Ramah**, north of Jerusalem, is a staging place for captives and the setting of Rachel’s weeping in [[Jer. 31:15]].

**Babylon** lies far east, but its armies approach Judah from the north along the routes around the desert. That is why the invading threat is so often “from the north.”

**Egypt** is the destination of the survivors in chapters 43–44, where Jeremiah’s ministry ends in the biblical account.

This frame is no substitute for the book. Jeremiah’s world is political, but his central question is covenantal: will Judah hear the Lord before the consequences already gathering at the gate arrive?
