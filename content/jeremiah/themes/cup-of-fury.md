Jeremiah is handed a cup and told to make the nations drink it: “Take the wine cup of this fury at my hand, and cause all the nations, to whom I send thee, to drink it” (25:15). The chapter then gives what the other cup passages give only in part: a full list of the drinkers, in order. “Jerusalem, and the cities of Judah” come first (25:18), then Egypt and the nations east and west, then “all the kingdoms of the world,” and last, after all of them, “the king of Sheshach shall drink after them” (25:26), a cipher for Babylon.[@lds-scriptures] Those who refuse the cup are told, “Ye shall certainly drink” (25:28). Babylon, which the Lord calls “my servant” in the same chapter (25:9), is the instrument through which the judgment reaches everyone else; in the oracle against her she is the cup itself, “a golden cup in the LORD’s hand” (51:7); and then the Lord says of her people, “I will make them drunken … and sleep a perpetual sleep” (51:39, 57). In Jeremiah, that is, the cup is never set down. Every one to whom it is handed drinks it in turn, and the instrument through which it was poured drinks last.

The commentaries see the pieces. Keil says the list begins with Judah and ends “last of all” with the king of Babylon, and that “by ‘shall drink after them’ Babylon is sufficiently distinguished from the other kings and countries”; Ellicott calls the name Sheshach “the culminating point of the whole prophecy”; Barnes, at Habakkuk’s cup, says it “had gone round the circuit of the nations whom God had employed him to chasten, and now, the circle completed, it should be brought round to himself,” and cites this verse.[@keil-jeremiah][@ellicott-jeremiah][@barnes-habakkuk] The Student Manual gives the movement in one sentence, from Jerusalem through the nations to Babylonia.[@ot-manual-24] This page adds one step: read together, 51:7, 39 and 57 show Babylon as cup and drinker in turn, which raises the question of who is never handed the cup. The page ends where the Church’s edition does: its footnote from “the cup of mine indignation” in a latter-day revelation leads back to this chapter, and that revelation stands beside the Savior’s words about a cup He drank “that they might not suffer.”[@lds-scriptures]

## “Take the wine cup of this fury at my hand”

The cup is a figure. Keil: “the cup is no material cup, and the drinking of it no act of the outer, physical reality,” and Jeremiah performs the commission “when he announces what he is commanded”; Barnes, the same: “Not actually offering the wine-cup … but publicly proclaiming this prophecy in Jerusalem.”[@keil-jeremiah][@barnes-jeremiah] Donald Parry lists the scene among the Old Testament’s symbolic actions as prophecy, with the prophecy it carries: “Many nations will be destroyed by the sword.”[@parry-symbolic-action] The sword is named in the next verse, “because of the sword that I will send among them” (25:16), and the figure the cup carries is drunkenness: “they shall drink, and be moved, and be mad” (25:16); “Drink ye, and be drunken, and spue, and fall, and rise no more” (25:27). The book had already said this of Judah without a cup: “I will fill all the inhabitants of this land, even the kings that sit upon David’s throne, and the priests, and the prophets, and all the inhabitants of Jerusalem, with drunkenness” (13:13), the one place Jeremiah uses that noun.[@oshb-wlc] The word “fury,” *ḥēmâ*, stands seventeen times in the book, poured out, kindled, going forth like fire; only here is it in a cup.[@oshb-wlc]

The figure was not Jeremiah’s alone. Elsewhere in the Bible disaster is pictured as “a bitter and intoxicating draught,” as the Cambridge commentary notes, and the Church’s edition joins the other cups to this one: the footnotes at Isaiah 51:17 and 51:23, Lamentations 4:21, Ezekiel 23:31 and Obadiah 1:16 all lead to Jeremiah 25.[@cambridge-jeremiah][@lds-scriptures]

## “To wit, Jerusalem”

Judah drinks first. The list of nations opens “To wit, Jerusalem, and the cities of Judah, and the kings thereof, and the princes thereof” (25:18), and the Lord gives the order as His own: “lo, I begin to bring evil on the city which is called by my name, and should ye be utterly unpunished?” (25:29). The Student Manual: “Beginning in Jerusalem and the cities of Judah, the bitter cup will be drunk by Egypt, the nations of the west and east, and Babylonia.”[@ot-manual-24] The manual sends the reader to Isaiah, where the same cup moves: Jerusalem “hast drunk at the hand of the LORD the cup of his fury” (Isa. 51:17), and the Lord says, “I have taken out of thine hand the cup of trembling … thou shalt no more drink it again: But I will put it into the hand of them that afflict thee” (Isa. 51:22–23). The manual reads it so: “the cup of fury shall be given to those who have trampled on and walked over the covenant people of the Lord. It will then be their turn to know suffering.”[@ot-manual-17] Jeremiah 25 tells the same passing of the cup from the other end, from the hand that gives it.

Who carries it to the nations is also in the chapter. “Nebuchadrezzar the king of Babylon, my servant” is sent “against this land … and against all these nations round about” (25:9). The cup and the servant are two figures for one judgment: “Overthrow at the hands of the Chaldaeans is to be the fate of the nations,” yet the chapter keeps the figures apart;[@cambridge-jeremiah] it never says the sword is Babylon’s, only “the sword that I will send” (25:16, 27, 29). But the servant has a term: “when seventy years are accomplished … I will punish the king of Babylon, and that nation” (25:12).

## “Ye shall certainly drink”

The cup cannot be declined. “If they refuse to take the cup at thine hand to drink, then shalt thou say unto them, Thus saith the LORD of hosts; Ye shall certainly drink” (25:28); the Hebrew doubles the verb, drink you shall drink.[@oshb-wlc] The metaphors of 25:27, in Barnes’s reading, “denote the helplessness to which the nations are reduced by drinking the wine-cup of fury.”[@barnes-jeremiah] The book returns to the point against Edom: “they whose judgment was not to drink of the cup have assuredly drunken; and art thou he that shall altogether go unpunished? … thou shalt surely drink of it” (49:12). Obadiah says the same of the nations, “so shall all the heathen drink continually,” and the Church’s footnotes run both ways between his verse and these two of Jeremiah’s.[@lds-scriptures] The same verb describes Moab’s drunkenness and the command that the nations “be drunken” in 25:27: “Make ye him drunken: for he magnified himself against the LORD” (48:26).[@oshb-wlc]

## “The king of Sheshach shall drink after them”

The list ends with a name that is not a name. “Sheshach” is Babel written in a cipher that turns the alphabet end for end, so that the second letter becomes the second from last; Keil, the Cambridge commentary, Ellicott and the lexicon all read it so, and Matthew Bowen calls the disguised names “symbols of the Lord’s justice overtaking the Babylonians.”[@keil-jeremiah][@cambridge-jeremiah][@ellicott-jeremiah][@bdb][@bowen-ominous-onomastics] The book itself decodes it: “How is Sheshach taken! … how is Babylon become an astonishment among the nations!” (51:41), and the Church’s footnotes join the two verses.[@lds-scriptures] The clause is not in the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation. The Cambridge commentary calls it “doubtless a later insertion”; Keil answers that the reasons given “are valueless,” since “shall drink after them” is exactly what sets Babylon apart from the others.[@cambridge-jeremiah][@keil-jeremiah] On either view the Hebrew text as the Church’s edition prints it ends the list with Babylon, last and alone, and the arc does not rest on the clause: 25:12 puts Babylon’s punishment after the seventy years, and 51:39 and 57 make her drink.

Before Babylon drinks, the oracle against her makes her the cup itself. “Babylon hath been a golden cup in the LORD’s hand, that made all the earth drunken: the nations have drunken of her wine; therefore the nations are mad” (51:7). The lexicon calls it “bold metaphor”: in Jeremiah this is the only place where a nation is the cup, and it is the only example in the Old Testament books this site searches; Zechariah calls Jerusalem a “cup of trembling” with a different word, one meaning a basin.[@bdb][@oshb-wlc] The same root stands behind “mad” here and “be mad” in 25:16, where the nations drink the cup.[@oshb-wlc] Keil says the figure “refers us back to Jeremiah 25:15, where, however, it is applied in a different way,” and reads the gold as “the splendour and glory of Nebuchadnezzar’s dominion” and the hand as the Lord using Babylon “as His instrument for pouring out His wrath to the nations.”[@keil-jeremiah] Whose wine is in the cup divides the commentators. Keil and Barnes read it as the Lord’s wrath, “the wine whereof she makes the nations drink is the wrath of God”; the Cambridge commentary and Ellicott read it as Babylon’s own, “the influence for evil which her brilliance and luxury have upon the nations,” a wine that “was poisoned, intoxicating men with wild ambitions and dark idolatries.”[@barnes-jeremiah][@cambridge-jeremiah][@ellicott-jeremiah] The page needs only what the verse says: the cup is Babylon, the hand is the Lord’s, and the nations drank.

Then Babylon drinks. “In their heat I will make their feasts, and I will make them drunken, that they may rejoice, and sleep a perpetual sleep, and not wake, saith the LORD” (51:39); “I will make drunk her princes, and her wise men, her captains, and her rulers, and her mighty men: and they shall sleep a perpetual sleep” (51:57). The verb is the one that made the earth drunken in 51:7 and the nations in 25:27 and Moab in 48:26, now with the Lord as its subject and Babylon as its object; neither verse has the word “cup.”[@oshb-wlc] Ellicott supplies it: “The wine cup which He would give them would be that of His wrath (Jeremiah 25:16–17)”; Keil: in the midst of their revelry the Lord will “give them the cup of wrath to drink.”[@ellicott-jeremiah][@keil-jeremiah] John’s vision keeps the turn: “great Babylon came in remembrance before God, to give unto her the cup of the wine of the fierceness of his wrath” (Rev. 16:19), and “in the cup which she hath filled fill to her double” (Rev. 18:6); the New Testament Student Manual says John “drew upon their language,” Jeremiah 51:6–8 among the passages.[@lds-scriptures][@nt-student-manual-56] The Bible Dictionary says that in Revelation “Babylon probably denotes Rome” or “apostate Jerusalem,” and in the Doctrine and Covenants “the world”; whatever Babylon names, the turn of the cup is the same.[@bd-babylon] The cup in Revelation 17:4 is the woman’s own, “full of abominations”; the note on [[Jer. 51:7]] draws the difference.

So the cup in Jeremiah passes through the nations until Babylon, through whom it was poured, drinks. Habakkuk says it in one sentence to the same empire: “the cup of the LORD’s right hand shall be turned unto thee” (Hab. 2:16), and Barnes, at that verse, hears Jeremiah’s “the king of Sheshach shall drink after them.”[@barnes-habakkuk] In Jeremiah no one on the list is spared it, not Judah, who begins; not the nations, who refuse; not Babylon, through whom, on Keil’s and Barnes’s reading, it was poured. The one hand that is never handed the cup is the one that holds it out to Jeremiah: “at my hand” (25:15), “in the LORD’s hand” (51:7).

## “Would that I might not drink the bitter cup”

Jeremiah’s cup passage does not speak of the Savior, and the chapter’s note on [[Jer. 25:28]] says so; what follows is the Church’s edition and the page’s comparison. The edition reaches this chapter from the Lord’s own cup in two steps. In a revelation of 1830 He says, “the cup of mine indignation is full; for behold, my blood shall not cleanse them if they hear me not,” and the footnote on “cup” is Jeremiah 25:15. The footnote on “blood” leads to the revelation of 1829, where He says what His blood did: “I, God, have suffered these things for all, that they might not suffer if they would repent; But if they would not repent they must suffer even as I … and would that I might not drink the bitter cup, and shrink” (D&C 29:17; 19:16–18).[@lds-scriptures] King Benjamin’s “cup of the wrath of God,” which “justice could no more deny unto them than it could deny that Adam should fall” (Mosiah 3:26), carries the same footnote to Jeremiah.[@lds-scriptures] The Student Manual calls Jeremiah’s cup “the bitter cup” without naming the Savior; no Church source I read says that Jeremiah’s cup is His.[@ot-manual-24]

What the Restoration’s witnesses do say is that He drank a cup of this kind. At Bountiful He introduces Himself: “I have drunk out of that bitter cup which the Father hath given me, and have glorified the Father in taking upon me the sins of the world” (3 Ne. 11:11). Kevin Tolley hears in it “an old title used by ancient Israelite prophets like Isaiah and Jeremiah,” and says the title told the Nephites that “the man standing before them was the one who had taken their cup.”[@tolley-christ-cup] Elder Jeremy R. Jaggi, in the October 2025 general conference, used Isaiah’s names for it: “Because Jesus took of the bitter cup of trembling, the cup of fury—for me, for my family, for all of us!”[@jaggi-humble-souls] Jennifer Lane: the winepress of wrath “is the bitter cup that should have been ours, but Christ drank it for us.”[@lane-winepress] Scripture Central: “The Savior suffered the consequences of sin (drank the ‘bitter cup’) on behalf of all, so that those who repent may not have to drink of the bitter cup themselves.”[@sc-knowhy-341]

Set beside Jeremiah, the point is the hand. In Jeremiah the one hand never handed the cup is the Lord’s, who holds it out at 25:15 and holds Babylon as His cup at 51:7. The Restoration names Him: Tolley reads the cup in the Lord’s hand at 51:7 as Jehovah’s, and the title of 3 Nephi 11:11 as “a clear declaration that the great Jehovah stood before them”; in the revelation of 1830 He calls it “the cup of mine indignation,” and Lane writes, “Christ takes the cup of wrath, the cup of indignation.”[@tolley-christ-cup][@lds-scriptures][@lane-winepress] So the one hand in Jeremiah that gives the cup and never drinks is, in these witnesses, the hand that in Gethsemane received “the cup which my Father hath given me” (John 18:11) and drank. Setting Jeremiah’s hand beside Gethsemane’s is this page’s comparison; the sources name the drinker and the cup, not Jeremiah 25. The revelations keep Jeremiah’s rule as well: Jeremiah’s nations had been told, “Turn ye again now every one from his evil way,” and “ye have not hearkened” (25:5, 7), and in the revelation those who will not repent “must suffer even as I,” the cup of indignation still full for them (D&C 19:17; 29:17).[@lds-scriptures] The chapter note already says that Jeremiah’s cup is not a prophecy of the Atonement.

For a disciple the passages leave two statements. The same Lord commands, “repent, lest I smite you by the rod of my mouth, and by my wrath, and by my anger, and your sufferings be sore” (D&C 19:15).[@lds-scriptures] And Lane, from Isaiah 51:22, describes the response asked of the one whose cup He takes: “we must let him take out of our hand the cup of trembling. We must let go of the cup of wrath and accept instead the blood of the covenant.”[@lane-winepress] Elder Jaggi’s sentence was spoken of his own family’s afflictions, cancer and loss and “hell’s fury,” and because He took the cup, the burdens of those afflictions can be laid down.[@jaggi-humble-souls]
