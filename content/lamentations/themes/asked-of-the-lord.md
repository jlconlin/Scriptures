Lamentations is a book of prayers that are not answered on the page. *Scripture Helps* says it plainly: “unlike many books in the Old Testament, Lamentations does not contain any responses from the Lord. It captures only the suffering and longing that the people felt before the Lord later showed mercy to them.”[@scripture-helps-jer-31-33] The Hebrew verb for “answer” does not occur in the book.[@oshb-wlc] Read as prayer, the five poems ask the Lord three things. They ask Him to look: “O LORD, behold my affliction” ([[Lam. 1:9]]); “See, O LORD, and consider” ([[Lam. 1:11]]); “Behold, O LORD, and consider to whom thou hast done this” ([[Lam. 2:20]]); “Remember, O LORD, what is come upon us: consider, and behold our reproach” ([[Lam. 5:1]]). They ask, by saying five times that it is missing, for a comforter: “she hath none to comfort her” ([[Lam. 1:2]]). And at the end they ask Him to remember and to turn them: “Wherefore dost thou forget us for ever… Turn thou us unto thee, O LORD, and we shall be turned” ([[Lam. 5:20–21]]). The book stops there, with the three things asked and nothing said back.

My argument is that each of the three is answered elsewhere in scripture, in the Lord’s own voice and in the book’s own words. The Hebrew of “behold my affliction,” which is also the Psalms’ standard petition ([[Ps. 25:18]]), is the Hebrew of “I have surely seen the affliction of my people” ([[Ex. 3:7]]). The participle of “none to comfort” is the participle of “I, even I, am he that comforteth you” ([[Isa. 51:12]]). The “forget” of 5:20 is the “forget” of “yet will I not forget thee” ([[Isa. 49:15]]), and the “turn thou us” of 5:21 is the “turn thou me” that the Lord answers in Jeremiah with “I do earnestly remember him still” ([[Jer. 31:18]]; [[Jer. 31:20]]). The Church’s edition makes the last of these joins in its footnotes and sends the first “comfort” of the book to a revelation in which the Lord says why He is slow to answer and promises to remember mercy ([[D&C 101:7–9]]). None of those answers was spoken to this city at this hour, and the essay does not say they were. It says that the words Lamentations uses to ask are the words the Lord uses, elsewhere, to answer, and that the book itself knows this once, in the middle poem, where it speaks of His heart. The commentators carry the pairing of 5:21 with Jeremiah 31:18 and of 2:13 with Isaiah 51:19; the rest of the pairings, and the reading of the three asks as one prayer, are mine.

## Behold, O LORD

The city’s first words to God are a request to be looked at. “O LORD, behold my affliction: for the enemy hath magnified himself” ([[Lam. 1:9]]). Keil takes the line as the daughter of Zion’s own sigh, “humbled through shame and repentance,” and reads 1:11 as the same sigh “repeated in an intensified form”: “See, O LORD, and consider; for I am become vile.”[@keil-lamentations] She asks again at 1:20, “Behold, O LORD; for I am in distress,” and at 2:20 the two verbs of 1:11 return in the same order, “Behold, O LORD, and consider to whom thou hast done this.”[@oshb-wlc] The man of the third poem waits, “Till the LORD look down, and behold from heaven” ([[Lam. 3:50]]), then says it has happened, “O LORD, thou hast seen my wrong… Thou hast seen all their vengeance” ([[Lam. 3:59–60]]), and then asks again with the city’s imperative, “Behold their sitting down, and their rising up” ([[Lam. 3:63]]).[@oshb-wlc] The last poem widens the voice to “we” and puts the two verbs in the other order: “Remember, O LORD, what is come upon us: consider, and behold our reproach” ([[Lam. 5:1]]).[@oshb-wlc] The site’s notes on [Lamentations 2:20](/lamentations/2/) and [Lamentations 3:48](/lamentations/3/) follow the asking through the poems. Two verses describe the Lord withholding His attention: in the second poem the Lord “remembered not his footstool in the day of his anger” ([[Lam. 2:1]]), and of the priests and elders in the fourth, “he will no more regard them” ([[Lam. 4:16]]), the same verb for “consider,” now of the Lord not looking.[@oshb-wlc]

The book never has the Lord say that He has seen. The man says it for Him. But the Lord has said it, of another affliction, in the same words. “I have surely seen the affliction of my people which are in Egypt” ([[Ex. 3:7]]). The verb is the city’s “behold,” doubled for emphasis, and “affliction” is the noun of 1:9.[@oshb-wlc] Exodus speaks of Egypt and not of this city, and no commentary I have read puts the two verses together; the pairing is mine. What I claim for it is that Lamentations asks in the vocabulary the Lord Himself used when He announced that He had looked, and that the site’s chapter on [Exodus 3](/exodus/3/) and the Genesis essay [The God Who Sees, Hears, and Remembers](/genesis/themes/god-who-sees/) have already followed that vocabulary from Hagar to the bush. The city asks for the thing He is known for.

## None to comfort her

The first poem says it five times, and the Hebrew is the same each time, a participle: one who comforts.[@oshb-wlc] “Among all her lovers she hath none to comfort her” ([[Lam. 1:2]]); “she had no comforter” ([[Lam. 1:9]]); “the comforter that should relieve my soul is far from me” ([[Lam. 1:16]]); “Zion spreadeth forth her hands, and there is none to comfort her” ([[Lam. 1:17]]); “there is none to comfort me” ([[Lam. 1:21]]). Keil notes that the thought of 1:2 “is repeated in Lamentations 1:9, Lamentations 1:16, Lamentations 1:17, and Lamentations 1:21,” and that the faithless lovers are the nations Jerusalem had allied with.[@keil-lamentations] The Student Manual puts the refrain in a sentence: “none appeared to comfort Judah. She put forth her hands in a plea for help, but no one responded.”[@ot-manual-24] The root is used once more, as a verb, when the poet turns to her: “what thing shall I take to witness for thee? what thing shall I liken to thee… that I may comfort thee, O virgin daughter of Zion? for thy breach is great like the sea: who can heal thee?” ([[Lam. 2:13]]). Keil supplies the answer the verse withholds: “The answer to the last question, ‘Who can heal thee?’… is, ‘no man.’”[@keil-lamentations] After that sixth use the word is gone from the book.[@oshb-wlc] The site’s note on [Lamentations 1:2](/lamentations/1/) has the refrain.

The one who comforts is named in Isaiah, and He names Himself with the city’s word. “I, even I, am he that comforteth you” ([[Isa. 51:12]]). The Hebrew is the participle of 1:2 with a suffix, “your comforter,” and Delitzsch says of the verse, “Jehovah Himself begins to speak now… ‘I am your comforter.’”[@oshb-wlc][@delitzsch] The Greek Bible, the Septuagint, makes the match visible even to a reader without Hebrew: it renders “none to comfort her” with the participle *ho parakalōn*, “the one comforting,” and Isaiah’s “he that comforteth you” with the same participle.[@swete-lxx-lamentations][@swete-lxx-isaiah] Pairing the two verses is mine. But I am not the first to hear Lamentations in that chapter of Isaiah. Seven verses on, Isaiah asks Jerusalem the poet’s own question, “by whom shall I comfort thee?” ([[Isa. 51:19]]), and Delitzsch, who takes the speaker there as the prophet rather than the Lord, writes, “This is the real explanation, according to Lamentations 2:13,” and of the whole passage, “The whole of the group is in the tone of the Lamentations of Jeremiah.”[@delitzsch] The Cambridge commentary on Isaiah calls the resemblances to Lamentations in these verses “so striking.”[@cambridge-isaiah] So the poet’s helplessness to comfort in 2:13 is voiced again in Isaiah 51:19 by a human voice, on Delitzsch’s reading, seven verses after the Lord has named Himself the comforter. President Hinckley used the verse for mourners: “May they be comforted, sustained, and blessed by Him who declared, ‘I, even I, am he that comforteth you.’”[@hinckley-work-of-the-master] Jacob read the chapter to the Nephites, and the Book of Mormon doubles the pronoun once more, “I am he; yea, I am he that comforteth you” ([[2 Ne. 8:12]]). The site’s chapter on [Isaiah 51](/isaiah/51/) has the setting.

*Come, Follow Me* sends a reader of the middle poem to the Sermon on the Mount, “Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted” ([[Matt. 5:4]]).[@cfm-ot-2026-44] The tense is the right one for Lamentations. Within the book the comfort is future or absent; the middle poem speaks of mercies and hope and waiting, “It is good that a man should both hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD” ([[Lam. 3:26]]), and never uses the word “comfort.”[@oshb-wlc]

## Remember, and turn thou us

The last poem ends in three verses that ask and are not answered. “Thou, O LORD, remainest for ever; thy throne from generation to generation. Wherefore dost thou forget us for ever, and forsake us so long time? Turn thou us unto thee, O LORD, and we shall be turned; renew our days as of old. But thou hast utterly rejected us; thou art very wroth against us” ([[Lam. 5:19–22]]). Keil reads the sequence as a rising: because the throne endures, “He cannot let His kingdom perish on the earth,” and so the people ask Him not to forget, and then to “lead us back to Thyself, that we may return,” which Keil takes as “the re-establishment of the gracious relation.”[@keil-lamentations] The last verse is read in different ways. The King James has “But”; Keil, the Cambridge commentary, Ellicott and the modern translations have “unless.”[@keil-lamentations][@cambridge-lamentations][@ellicott-lamentations][@nrsvue][@esv][@niv] Cambridge: “The whole sentence is an hypothesis not to be accepted as fact. God’s anger cannot last for ever, and thus there is yet hope.”[@cambridge-lamentations] Ellicott: “it is stated as per impossible; God has not rejected, and therefore He will renew.”[@ellicott-lamentations] The Septuagint has neither “but” nor “unless” but “for.”[@swete-lxx-lamentations] Keil adds that many Hebrew manuscripts repeat verse 21 after verse 22 so that the synagogue reading would not end on wrath, and that the ending needs no such help: its hope, in a phrase he borrows, “merely glimmers from afar, like the morning star through the clouds.”[@keil-lamentations] The site’s notes on [Lamentations 5:21](/lamentations/5/) and [Lamentations 5:22](/lamentations/5/) set out the readings. However the verse is read, no reply follows it.

A reply to the same prayer exists, in the book’s own words, in Jeremiah, spoken to Ephraim. Ephraim prays, “turn thou me, and I shall be turned; for thou art the LORD my God” ([[Jer. 31:18]]), and the Hebrew is the Hebrew of 5:21, the same request to be turned back, in the same two verb forms.[@oshb-wlc] This join is not mine. The Church’s edition footnotes each verse to the other; the Cambridge commentary calls 5:21 “suggestive of Jeremiah 31:18”; Ellicott says the cry of Jeremiah 31:18 “is reproduced in Lamentations 5:21”; and the site’s note on [Lamentations 5:21](/lamentations/5/) already follows it.[@lds-scriptures][@cambridge-lamentations][@ellicott-jeremiah] What Jeremiah has that Lamentations lacks is the Lord’s answer, two verses later, which the site’s note already sets beside 5:21: “Is Ephraim my dear son?… since I spake against him, I do earnestly remember him still: therefore my bowels are troubled for him; I will surely have mercy upon him, saith the LORD” ([[Jer. 31:20]]). Ellicott hears the Hebrew doubling in both verbs, “Remembering, I remember; pitying, I pity,” and reads it as a God who “could not bring himself to utter the sentence of rejection. His love turned to the penitent who turned to Him.”[@ellicott-jeremiah] Keil calls the troubled bowels “the most heartfelt sympathy.”[@keil-jeremiah] The “remember” is the verb of 5:1 and the opposite of the “forget” of 5:20; that Hebrew match, beyond the pairing of request and reply, is mine.[@oshb-wlc]

The answer is given to someone who repents, and Lamentations also repents. Ephraim’s “turn thou me” is followed by “after that I was turned, I repented… I was ashamed” ([[Jer. 31:19]]), and the Cambridge commentary hears Ephraim “confessing that his punishment was the just consequence of his sin, and praying for acceptance.”[@cambridge-jeremiah] Keil’s sentence on 5:21 says the restored relation is “impossible without repentance and conversion on the part of Israel.”[@keil-lamentations] Lamentations supplies both. The city says, “The LORD is righteous; for I have rebelled against his commandment” ([[Lam. 1:18]]); the people say, “Woe unto us, that we have sinned!” ([[Lam. 5:16]]); and the middle poem has already used the verb of 5:21 of the people themselves, “Let us search and try our ways, and turn again to the LORD… We have transgressed and have rebelled” ([[Lam. 3:40–42]]), where “turn again” is the same cohortative as “we shall be turned” in 5:21.[@oshb-wlc] The book asks to be turned after it has resolved to turn. The site’s note on [Lamentations 1:5](/lamentations/1/) follows the book’s account of the cause.

The same complaint is answered once more, to Zion by name. “But Zion said, The LORD hath forsaken me, and my Lord hath forgotten me. Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee. Behold, I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands” ([[Isa. 49:14–16]]). The verb is the verb of “dost thou forget us.”[@oshb-wlc] Shon Hopkin reads the passage as the Lord’s reply to a Zion that “is still not confident that the Lord will remember her,” and no source I have read sets it beside Lamentations 5:20; that pairing is mine.[@hopkin-caph] The Book of Mormon’s Isaiah adds a clause to Zion’s complaint that reads like a gloss on this whole essay: “my Lord hath forgotten me—but he will show that he hath not” ([[1 Ne. 21:14]]). The site’s note on [Isaiah 49:15](/isaiah/49/) has the verse.

## Not from his heart

Lamentations does once say what the Lord is like inside, and it is the only place the book speaks of His heart. “For the Lord will not cast off for ever: But though he cause grief, yet will he have compassion according to the multitude of his mercies. For he doth not afflict willingly nor grieve the children of men” ([[Lam. 3:31–33]]). “Willingly” is literally “from his heart,” as Ellicott notes, “as being the centre of volition as well as emotion,” and as the ESV renders it, “he does not afflict from his heart.”[@ellicott-lamentations][@esv] Keil calls the three verses “the grounds of comfort,” the second of which is that “when He has caused sorrow, He shows pity once more… Compassion outweighs sorrow.”[@keil-lamentations] The site’s note on [Lamentations 3:33](/lamentations/3/) reads the verse the same way, and the seminary manual sums up 3:31–39 as “the Lord does not take pleasure in punishing people,” before turning to 3:40–41 and “the afflictions we experience as a result of sin”; its lesson also cautions, of the book as a whole, that suffering does not mean “that all who experience trials somehow deserve those trials.”[@ot-seminary-teacher-lesson-138] “Have compassion” in 3:32 is the one use in the book of the verb *raḥam*, the Old Testament’s usual verb for the Lord’s compassion, and the one Jeremiah 31:20, “I will surely have mercy upon him,” and Isaiah 49:15, “that she should not have compassion,” also use.[@oshb-wlc] The word is common, and I do not claim a pointed echo; what carries the point is the plain sense of 3:31–33 and Keil’s “grounds of comfort.”

The Church’s edition reaches the same place by a footnote. At the first “comfort” of the book, 1:2, the footnote leads to a revelation of 1833 in which the Lord explains an unanswered prayer and then answers it. “They were slow to hearken unto the voice of the Lord their God; therefore, the Lord their God is slow to hearken unto their prayers, to answer them in the day of their trouble… Verily I say unto you, notwithstanding their sins, my bowels are filled with compassion towards them. I will not utterly cast them off; and in the day of wrath I will remember mercy” ([[D&C 101:7–9]]).[@lds-scriptures] The footnote is the Church’s; the reading of it against the end of the book is mine. “Slow… to answer” names the book’s silence; “not utterly cast them off” answers “thou hast utterly rejected us” and 3:31’s “will not cast off for ever”; “remember mercy” answers “dost thou forget us.” The other footnote that leads from the Lord’s hand to His heart is at 3:33 itself, where “afflict” leads to “In all their afflictions he was afflicted… in his love, and in his pity, he redeemed them” ([[D&C 133:53]]).[@lds-scriptures] That verse is about another day, and the book’s own verse does not say it; what the two share is the subject, where the Lord’s heart is while His hand is heavy.

One word of the Lord does stand in the book, and it should not be passed over. “Thou drewest near in the day that I called upon thee: thou saidst, Fear not” ([[Lam. 3:57]]). The man reports the Lord’s words, and the tenses are read two ways: as a rescue remembered, the reading of the NIV, ESV and NRSVUE, or as assurance that holds in the present, Keil’s reading, “Thou assurest me of Thine assistance,” for “we can discover no thanksgiving in Lamentations 3:55-58.”[@niv][@esv][@nrsvue][@keil-lamentations] The site’s note on [Lamentations 3:56](/lamentations/3/) weighs them. On either reading it is the man who reports it, and it is the one word of the Lord the book contains.

## The Comforter

The LORD these poems pray to is Jehovah, whom the Bible Dictionary identifies as the premortal Jesus Christ.[@bd-jehovah] That identification turns the answers of this essay into one voice. The One who said “I have surely seen the affliction of my people” at the bush, the site’s chapter on [Exodus 3](/exodus/3/) notes, is He. The One who says “I, even I, am he that comforteth you” and “yet will I not forget thee” is He, and Latter-day Saint readers have heard the next line of Isaiah 49 as His in a particular way. Elder Gong has the risen Lord say, “Yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee. Behold, I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands,” and then, “I am he who was lifted up. I am Jesus that was crucified.”[@gong-always-remember-him] Elder Andersen, to one who finds repentance too hard: “He knows. He has felt your pain. He has declared, ‘I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands.’”[@andersen-repent-heal-you] Hopkin reads the engraved palms as “a physical reminder of his love,” the wounds of the resurrected Christ.[@hopkin-caph]

Luke records Him looking on the same city. “And when he was come near, he beheld the city, and wept over it” ([[Luke 19:41]]). Luke is Greek and Lamentations Hebrew, and what joins them is not a word but an act and a place: the city that asked to be looked at was looked at, by the Lord in the flesh, with tears. It is not an answer to the prayer of chapter 5, since He went on to foretell another siege ([[Luke 19:43–44]]), and no source I have read sets the two beside each other; the comparison is mine.

## A prayer left open

Lamentations is in the canon as it is, three things asked and no reply. The revelation the Church’s footnote points to says why those Saints’ prayers went unanswered in “the day of their trouble,” that they “were slow to hearken,” and in the same breath that His bowels are “filled with compassion” ([[D&C 101:7]]; [[D&C 101:9]]). The middle poem had already said how to live inside that gap: “It is good that a man should both hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD” ([[Lam. 3:26]]). The man describes his prayer as shut out both before this statement ([[Lam. 3:8]]) and after it ([[Lam. 3:44]]), and as heard later ([[Lam. 3:55–57]]); the book keeps both. The Church’s footnote at his “Thou drewest near” leads to the Lord’s standing promise, “Draw near unto me and I will draw near unto you” ([[D&C 88:63]]).[@lds-scriptures]

A disciple who prays and hears nothing is praying inside a book of scripture. The words are already there, and so, in other books, is the answer in the same words: He has seen, He is the comforter, He does not forget, He will have mercy. Lamentations does not let its reader have the answer early; it makes him wait with the city. But it tells him, once, where the Lord’s heart is while he waits, and the rest of scripture tells him whose heart it is. The covenant Alma’s people made at the waters of Mormon asks them to be what the first poem could not find, “willing to mourn with those that mourn; yea, and comfort those that stand in need of comfort” ([[Mosiah 18:9]]), and such comfort is one way the book’s “none to comfort her” can be answered within the congregation while the Lord’s own answer is awaited; the application is mine.
