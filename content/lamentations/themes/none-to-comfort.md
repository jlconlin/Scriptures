Six times in the first two poems of Lamentations the word “comfort” is spoken, each time of comfort that is lacking. After the sixth it is not used again. The middle poem speaks of hope, and the book ends on a prayer with no reply recorded.

| Step | Passage | The words |

| Who can heal thee? | [[Lam. 2:13]] | “that I may comfort thee… who can heal thee?” |

| Therefore have I hope | [[Lam. 3:21–26]] | “This I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope” |

## None to comfort her

“Among all her lovers she hath none to comfort her” ([[Lam. 1:2]]); “she had no comforter” ([[Lam. 1:9]]). The poet says this of the city, and the city says it of herself: “the comforter that should relieve my soul is far from me” ([[Lam. 1:16]]); “there is none to comfort me” ([[Lam. 1:21]]).[@lds-scriptures] All five are one Hebrew word, *menaḥem*, “one who comforts.”[@oshb-wlc] See [Lamentations 1](/lamentations/1/).

## “Who can heal thee?”

The poet speaks to her: “what shall I equal to thee, that I may comfort thee, O virgin daughter of Zion? for thy breach is great like the sea: who can heal thee?” ([[Lam. 2:13]]). He asks how he may comfort her and gives no answer. The verb has the same root as chapter 1’s *menaḥem*, and this is the root’s sixth and last use in the book.[@oshb-wlc]

## “My hope is perished”

“He shutteth out my prayer” ([[Lam. 3:8]]). “Thou hast removed my soul far off from peace… And I said, My strength and my hope is perished from the LORD” ([[Lam. 3:17–18]]).

## “Therefore have I hope”

“This I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope. It is of the LORD’s mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning… The LORD is good unto them that wait for him… It is good that a man should both hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD” ([[Lam. 3:21–26]]).

The hope that perished in verse 18 is a noun, *tokhelet*; the hope of verses 21 and 24 is the verb *ʾoḥil*, from the same root.[@oshb-wlc][@biblehub-interlinear] Verses 19–20 tell of no rescue, only of remembering his affliction; the “therefore” of verse 21 follows what he recalls. He hopes and waits for a salvation, and does not say it has come.

## Shut out, and heard

“Thou hast covered thyself with a cloud, that our prayer should not pass through” ([[Lam. 3:44]]). Then: “I called upon thy name, O LORD, out of the low dungeon. Thou hast heard my voice… Thou drewest near in the day that I called upon thee: thou saidst, Fear not” ([[Lam. 3:55–57]]). The book quotes no other words of the Lord, and these come in the man’s own telling.[@lds-scriptures] The tenses of these verses are read two ways: as a rescue remembered (“You came near when I called”),[@niv][@esv][@nrsvue] or as present assurance, as an older commentary takes it.[@keil-lamentations] See the note on [[Lam. 3:56]]. For the appeals to be seen, see [“See, O LORD, and Consider”](/lamentations/themes/look-upon-us/).

## No reply

“There is none that doth deliver us out of their hand” ([[Lam. 5:8]]). The last verses: “Thou, O LORD, remainest for ever… Wherefore dost thou forget us for ever, and forsake us so long time?… Turn thou us unto thee, O LORD, and we shall be turned; renew our days as of old. But thou hast utterly rejected us; thou art very wroth against us” ([[Lam. 5:19–22]]). No response from the Lord follows, here or anywhere in the book.[@scripture-helps-jer-31-33] In verse 22 the King James has “But”; the NRSVUE, ESV and NIV have “unless.”[@nrsvue][@esv][@niv] See the note on [[Lam. 5:22]].

## The word that stops

“Comfort” stands at 1:2, 9, 16, 17, 21 and 2:13 and nowhere after. The “none” of “none to comfort” is *ʾayin*, the ordinary Hebrew word for “there is not,” found in eleven verses of the book; the “none that doth deliver” of 5:8 is one of them.[@oshb-wlc] The middle poem speaks of hope, mercies and waiting, and not of comfort. Setting these side by side is this page’s own.[@lds-scriptures]

## Hope, not yet comfort

Readers of Lamentations 3:20–33 are asked what messages of hope in Christ they find there, and are sent also to Matthew 5:4.[@cfm-ot-2026-44] That verse puts the comfort in the future: “Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted” ([[Matt. 5:4]]).[@lds-scriptures]

Isaiah has a question put to Jerusalem: “by whom shall I comfort thee?” ([[Isa. 51:19]]). The verb is in the same form as “that I may comfort thee” ([[Lam. 2:13]]), and “destruction” in Isaiah’s verse is *sheber*, the “breach” of Lamentations, a word used five times in Lamentations and nine in Isaiah.[@oshb-wlc] Seven verses earlier in Isaiah the Lord says, “I, even I, am he that comforteth you” ([[Isa. 51:12]]). That sentence is Isaiah’s; Lamentations has none like it. Setting the two books side by side is this page’s own comparison.[@lds-scriptures] See [Isaiah 51](/isaiah/51/).

Within Lamentations no comforter is named, and the hope of the middle poem is a hope that waits. The last word is the prayer of [[Lam. 5:21–22]], and the book records no reply.
