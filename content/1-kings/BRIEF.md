# 1 Kings brief

Book-level decisions for 1 Kings. The site-wide standard is in `STANDARDS.md`; this file records what is particular to 1 Kings. Writers, checkers, and reviewers read both before starting a chapter. **1 and 2 Kings are one book in Hebrew**, and this brief holds what both share: the book itself, its text, the Joseph Smith Translation, the conventions and the sources. `content/2-kings/BRIEF.md` holds 2 Kings' own questions and points here for the rest. 1 Samuel's brief is the nearest model (the same kind of book, the same approach to its questions). **Much of Kings is already told elsewhere on the site**: Isaiah 36–39 is 2 Kgs. 18:13–20:19, Jeremiah 52 is 2 Kgs. 24:18–25:30, Malachi 4 explains why Elijah was sent, Hosea 1 tells Jehu's house, Jonah 1 and Amos 7 set Jeroboam II, and the *Temple and Worship* guide describes Solomon's temple. ## What a chapter is for here

1 Kings is 816 verses in 22 chapters: Solomon's reign and temple, the kingdom torn in two, and Elijah against Ahab and Jezebel. The reader knows the wise judgment, the temple, Carmel and the still small voice, and asks grown-up questions. Those a careful reader would ask, by chapter (a start, not a limit):

- **1–2:** *Who wrote Kings, and when? Why was Abishag brought to David, and why did asking for her cost Adonijah his life? Why Solomon and not the elder son? Was David's charge to kill Joab and Shimei just? Why were the horns of the altar no refuge? What was Abiathar's fault, and how does 2:27 fulfill 1 Sam. 2?*
- **3–4:** *What were the high places, and why did Solomon sacrifice at one? How old was he (“a little child,” 3:7)? What is an “understanding heart”? What does the Joseph Smith Translation change at 3:14? Why did Solomon marry Pharaoh's daughter? How large was the kingdom, and do the numbers (4:26) hold?*
- **5–7:** *Who was Hiram, and why did Israel need Phoenicians? Why “neither hammer nor axe” (6:7)? How was the temple laid out, and how does it compare with the tabernacle and with ours? What were Jachin and Boaz? Why twelve oxen under the sea? Why does the palace take longer than the temple?*
- **8–9:** *What was in the ark? What was the cloud? What does “the heaven of heavens cannot contain thee” (8:27) mean for a house of God? Why pray toward the temple? What is D&C 109's debt to this prayer? What does it mean that the LORD put His name there (9:3), and was the promise conditional (9:4–9)?*
- **10–11:** *Who was the queen of Sheba, and where was Sheba? Where did the gold come from? How many wives, and what does latter-day revelation say of them (D&C 132:38; Jacob 2:24)? Who were Ashtoreth, Milcom and Chemosh? What does the Joseph Smith Translation change in chapter 11? Why does Jeroboam get ten pieces and Judah one?*
- **12–14:** *Why did Rehoboam answer harshly, and what did “my little finger” mean? Why were the calves at Bethel and Dan a sin, when Jeroboam named them the gods that brought Israel out of Egypt? Why did the man of God die, when the old prophet lied to him? What did the Joseph Smith Translation change at 13:18? Who was Shishak? What is “the book of the chronicles”?*
- **15–16:** *What does “did evil in the sight of the LORD” measure, and why is David the standard? Why so many coups in the north and so few in Judah? Who was Omri, and why does the text give him six verses? Who was Jezebel, and why does her marriage matter so much? What is the curse of Jericho (16:34)?*
- **17–19:** *Who was Elijah, and how could he shut the heavens? Why a widow of Sidon? Why fire on Carmel, and why kill the prophets of Baal? Why did Elijah flee after the victory? What is the still small voice? Why forty days to Horeb, and why the cave? What does the mantle mean?*
- **20–22:** *Why was Ahab condemned for sparing Ben-hadad? Why couldn't Naboth sell his vineyard? Why was Ahab's repentance heard? Why did four hundred prophets say “Go up”? What is the lying spirit in Micaiah's vision, and what does the Joseph Smith Translation say of it?*

A chapter answers those from the text first. (The `christ` section then applies the chapter to the reader, in statements: `STANDARDS.md` §1.)

**Read the history as the writer framed it.** Kings judges every king by one measure: whether he did “that which was right in the sight of the LORD” as David did, or walked “in the way of Jeroboam” (15:3, 11, 26, 34; JST 11:33, 15:5). It ties events to the word spoken before them: “according to the word of the LORD” (13:26; 15:29; 16:12, 34; 22:38; and in 2 Kings), so a note can show where a prophecy is said to be fulfilled when the text says so. The temple is promised on a condition (6:12–13; 9:4–9), and Solomon's heart is the turning point (3:3, 9; 11:4, 9). Say what a repetition shows where a source shows it; don't build a scheme of the writer's own.

**Twenty-two chapters, a few great places.** Chapters 3, 8, 11, 12, 17–19 and 22 carry the book's questions and the most notes; 4, 7, 10, 14–16 and 20 are where readers know least, and lists (4:1–19; 7:13–51; 15–16's reigns) need one note on what the list is for, not a note per name.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Quotations were copied from the page named, with its paragraph number; the rest is the drafter's direction of where to look. Nothing goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **`when`.** The text dates by reigns (“in the fourth year of Solomon's reign,” 6:1; “in the twentieth year of Jeroboam,” 15:9), and the B.C. years differ by source: the division is “975,” “931” or “about 925 BC” (the *Two Kingdoms* guide sets the three side by side: `bible-chron-ot`, `ot-manual-kings-a`, `bd-israel-kingdom`), and 1 Kings runs from David's death “around 968 BC” to Ahaziah “around 852 BC” (`scripture-helps-2-samuel-11` [12]). **Proposed:** `when` is from the text (the reign, the year of the other kingdom's king), with no B.C. year; a `setting` may give a date in a clause as its source's, and never one of the writer's own.
- **The synchronisms.** The text's cross-dating of the two kingdoms' reigns does not add up as written. No Latter-day Saint source read treats it. **Proposed:** no note reconciles the chronology; where a reader would stumble on a figure, say that the years in Kings are counted differently in different sources and point to the guide's dates.
- **Large numbers.** 4:26 (40,000 stalls; 2 Chr. 9:25 has 4,000), 5:13–16, 8:63 (22,000 oxen and 120,000 sheep), 11:3 (700 wives and 300 concubines; `ot-minute-1-kings-11` [5]: “nice round numbers … may indicate an exaggeration or linguistic stylization,” his reading), 20:15, 29–30. The Student Manual's enrichment (`ot-manual-large-numbers`) gives the general case. A note gives the figure as the text has it and what a source says, never a figure of the writer's own.
- **Names of God.** Copy any phrase with LORD or GOD from `data/kjv/1-kings.json` (it was checked against the Gospel Library: “the Lord GOD” at 2:26 and 8:53; “the Lord” at 3:10 and 22:6). That the LORD is the premortal Christ is said where it answers a question, in a clause, pointing to `[[Ex. 3:14]]`.
- **Hebrew only where it answers a reader's question** (`STANDARDS.md` §3). Words that may pay: *lēb šōmēaʿ*, “understanding heart,” literally a hearing heart (3:9; `ot-minute-1-kings-3` [8]: “better translated as ‘discerning mind,’” his); *bāmôt*, “high places” (3:2); *bayit*, “house,” for temple, palace and dynasty at once (`ot-minute-1-kings-8` [6]; point to `[[2 Sam. 7:11]]`); *šēm*, “name” (8:29; 9:3); *ʾăšērâ*, “grove” (14:15; `bd-grove`); *qôl dᵉmāmâ daqqâ*, “still small voice” (19:12; footnote; `ot-minute-1-kings-19` [10]); *naḥălâ*, “inheritance” (21:3). No more than about a quarter of a chapter's notes are Language notes.
- **The Joseph Smith Translation.** The Gospel Library prints eight readings, all in 1 Kings: 11:4, 6, 33, 38, 39; 13:18; 15:3, 11 (`church.md` §0.1 has the wording). Three more are in the manuscript and in *Scripture Helps*' endnote but have no footnote: 3:14, 14:8, 15:5 (`church.md` §0.2; Old Testament Revision 2 on the Joseph Smith Papers, images 83–84). Cite those as the manuscript and *Scripture Helps*, and give them in the manuscript's wording with its spelling modernized only where the note says so. Most of them turn on one point: David is the standard of a good king except “in the matter of Uriah” (JST 15:5), and the Lord used him as an example of what kings should and should not do (`scripture-helps-2-samuel-11` [14]; `ot-manual-kings-1` [41]). That is explained once, at 3:14; the rest point. Nyman counts the changes (`nyman-jst-historical` [5]); give his count as his. 2 Kings has none in the footnotes.
- **Scripture reads Kings.** Jesus cites the widow of Zarephath and Naaman (`[[Luke 4:25]]`–27), Solomon's glory (`[[Matt. 6:29]]`) and the queen of the south (`[[Matt. 12:42]]`); Elijah stands with Moses at the Transfiguration (`[[Matt. 17:3]]`) and comes to Kirtland (`[[D&C 110:13]]`); Paul reads the seven thousand (`[[Rom. 11:2]]`–4); James reads Elijah's prayer for rain (`[[James 5:17]]`–18); Nephi built “after the manner of the temple of Solomon” (`[[2 Ne. 5:16]]`; `sc-knowhy-716`); Jacob rebukes the Nephites' excuse from David and Solomon (`[[Jacob 2:23]]`–24); 3 Nephi 8–11 is told in Elijah's storm, earthquake, fire and voice (`peterson-small-voice`, his observation). Where scripture quotes or names Kings, say so; an echo someone identifies is that author's. **No comparison panel.**
- **Other books on the site and the volume guides.** Besides the table: *The Conquest, the Judges, and One Kingdom* (`#rulers`: David's last years and Solomon), *Two Kingdoms and Assyria* (`#rulers` lists the kings; `#nations`: Phoenicia, Aram, Assyria), *A Timeline of the Old Testament* (`#united-kingdom`, `#two-kingdoms`), *Temple and Worship* (`#temples`, `#worship`), *Women and Marriage* (`#plural-marriage`: Solomon's wives; Jezebel, the widow), *The Land*. The *Proverbs*, *Ecclesiastes* and *Song of Solomon* pages say what is and isn't Solomon's: agree, link. Point in a clause; don't retell.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports an arc a reader would not see chapter by chapter (the heart “perfect with the LORD,” 8:61, and the heart turned away, 11:4; the word that is fulfilled; David as the measure of every king; the house that is built and the house that is torn); `propose-themes` and Codex propose each book's list with its last batch. No theme page is written until the author chooses it.
- **The older commentaries.** Latter-day Saint sources are thin on 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 10 and 14–16. In Samuel the first batch leaned on Keil and the Cambridge Bible until the author said “cutting back is probably a good idea” (1 Samuel's brief). Before citing either, try the text itself, BDB, the Church's footnotes and Bible Dictionary, the Chronicles parallel (2 Chr. 1–18, read in the King James), the guides and the site's other books; when only Keil answers, ask whether a careful reader would ask the question at all. Keep them under a third of a chapter's notes (no script counts this: the writer and reviewer do); a note that rests on Keil alone is “one older reading.” In chapters 1–11, 29 of 87 notes cited one, and 4, 7 and 10 had half, and were reworked. Use one key each for both books, **already in `sources.yaml`: `keil-kings` and `cambridge-kings`; never propose them again** (three chapters of the first batch did, with their own chapter's URL, and could not be committed). A ledger row carries the chapter's own page, `biblehub.com/commentaries/kad/1_kings/<c>.htm`, `…/cambridge/1_kings/<c>.htm`, and `2_kings` for 2 Kings.
- **Archaeology** answers a reader's question in Kings more than in most books: Shishak's campaign (14:25; `bd-shishak` names the Karnak relief), Omri in the Mesha stele (16:23; 2 Kgs. 3), Ahab at Qarqar. **Proposed:** a note may use an artifact where it answers the reader's question about the verse, from a museum's own catalog page or a source in the hierarchy (`STANDARDS.md` §9), and says what the artifact shows, not what the writer infers from it; no note is about an artifact for its own sake.

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter; point with a `[[reference]]` rather than re-explain; a note's first sentence gives the answer; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; don't join two verses yourself and call it the text's meaning; application only in `christ`, as statements. **No moral verdict in the writer's own words** on Solomon's executions, his wives, Jeroboam's motives, Elijah at Carmel, or Ahab; let the text and the Church's sources judge. **No gore** beyond the text (the dogs and Jezebel, 21:23; 22:38). **No psychology** the text does not give (“burnout,” “depression” for 19:4): the text says what Elijah said and what the LORD answered.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that forty-seven chapters say the same thing. Quotations were copied from the pages named on 2026-11-05; re-read the page before using one.

- **Who wrote Kings, and when (1 Kgs. 1:1; 2 Kgs. 25:27–30).** The Bible Dictionary: “Regarded by the Jews as forming one book. The Greek version divided the book of Samuel and the book of Kings each into two parts”; “The books were compiled by some unknown writer from a variety of written documents, including the state chronicles” (`bd-kings-books-of`); the seminary manual: “It is unclear when and where the books of 1 and 2 Kings were written,” and the state chronicles “were not the books of 1 and 2 Chronicles but rather a collection of records maintained under the direction of the kings” (`ot-seminary-1-kings-intro` [4]–[5]). *Scripture Helps* says the books were one until the Greek translation (`scripture-helps-2-samuel-11` [9]). The book ends with Jehoiachin released in Babylon (2 Kgs. 25:27–30), so in its finished form it is no older than that. Eliason, in *BYU Studies*, relays the scholarly view that Joshua through 2 Kings was compiled with Deuteronomy as its measure (the “Deuteronomistic history”) and that doubting 2 Kgs. 22–23 puts much of the Bible in doubt (`eliason-josiah` [18]–[20]); Hamblin uses the term too (`hamblin-vindicating-josiah`). **Proposed:** chapter 1's `setting` says the two books were one, the writer is not known, he names his sources (the acts of Solomon, 11:41; the chronicles of the kings of Israel and of Judah, 14:19, 29), and the history runs from David's death to the exile. The Deuteronomistic history may be named once, at 2 Kgs. 22:8, as Eliason's account of what scholars hold; it is not argued in any chapter. **No chapter treats the people of the book as other than real.** The Nephites likely had Kings on the brass plates (`sc-knowhy-410` [2], its reading): say so in chapter 1's `setting` if it helps.
- **The executions (1 Kgs. 2).** David charges Solomon to deal with Joab and Shimei “according to thy wisdom” (2:6, 9); Solomon kills Adonijah after the request for Abishag (2:22–25), Joab at the altar (2:28–34) and Shimei for leaving Jerusalem (2:46). No Church source read comments beyond the chapter heading. **Proposed:** each note says what the text gives as the reason (Joab's “blood of war in peace,” 2:5, 31–33; Shimei's oath and its breaking, 2:42–44; Abishag as a claim to the throne, which a source must support: the Student Manual's notes `ot-manual-kings-1` [26]–[37] are where to look first), and the chapter's last line, “the kingdom was established in the hand of Solomon” (2:46). No verdict and no excuse in the writer's voice.
- **Solomon's wives and his fall (3:1; 11:1–13).** Latter-day revelation speaks twice. D&C 132:1 names Solomon among those the Lord “justified … as touching the principle and doctrine of their having many wives and concubines,” and 132:38 says “in nothing did they sin save in those things which they received not of me”; Jacob 2:24: “David and Solomon truly had many wives and concubines, which thing was abominable before me, saith the Lord.” *Scripture Helps*: “When Solomon married ‘many strange women,’ he violated the Lord's commandment to not marry outside of the covenant…. While some of Solomon's marriages were authorized by the Lord, many were not” (`scripture-helps-2-samuel-11` [31], citing both verses in endnotes 38–39); the Student Manual relays Joseph Fielding Smith: Solomon “later in his life … took wives that the Lord did not give him” (`ot-manual-kings-1` [98]–[100]). The text's own charge is the foreign wives who turned his heart (11:2–4; `[[Deut. 17:16]]` (“Horses, wives, silver and gold”) on the site). **Proposed:** 11:3 gives the text's charge, then *Scripture Helps*' sentence, then both verses of latter-day scripture with what each says, without a reconciling scheme beyond *Scripture Helps*' and the manual's; the JST of 11:4, 6, 33 at 11:4. Link the *Women and Marriage* guide (`#plural-marriage`). **No note judges plural marriage itself.**
- **The temple and the Church's temples (6–8).** The chapter heading calls the molten sea a “baptismal font,” and *Scripture Helps* compares the fonts on twelve oxen in temples today ([23]); the footnotes set D&C 109 beside Solomon's prayer (8:23a); the Bible Dictionary says the temple doubled the tabernacle's dimensions (`bd-temple-of-solomon` [3]); Madsen compares the 1893 Salt Lake dedication (`madsen-solomons-temple` [8]); the *Encyclopedia of Mormonism* lists shared features and cites 8:27 (`eom-temples` [86]). **Proposed:** a note says what the text describes, then what the Church's sources compare; it does not say Solomon's temple had the ordinances of ours unless a source does, and gives no reconstruction of first-temple ritual of the writer's own (Larsen's from the Psalms, `larsen-ascending-hill`, is his).
- **Jeroboam's calves (12:25–33).** “Behold thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt” (12:28) are Aaron's words (*Scripture Helps* 25 endnote 6; `[[Ex. 32:4]]` on the site). *Scripture Helps*: “Jeroboam may have intended for the golden calves to serve as substitutes for the ark of the covenant. Regardless of Jeroboam's intentions, his actions led his people into apostasy” (25 [6]); the Bible Dictionary: the calves were “supposed to be images of Jehovah” (`bd-jeroboam` [1]). **Proposed:** 12:28 gives both and the text's reason (12:26–27, fear that the people would return to Jerusalem); “the sins of Jeroboam” is explained there once.
- **The man of God and the old prophet (13).** The old prophet lies (13:18), and the man of God who believed him is killed by a lion (13:24), “according to the word of the LORD” (13:26). The Joseph Smith Translation of 13:18: “that I may prove him; and he lied not unto him” (footnote 18b). The Student Manual, under “Do Prophets Ever Lie or Disobey the Lord?”, relays Rasmussen: “Some help is available in the [Joseph Smith Translation] of verse 18 … ‘he lied not unto him.’ … The young prophet should have obeyed God” (`ot-manual-4` [74]); it also reports a change at 13:26 that no footnote carries; Nyman lists the changes (`nyman-jst-historical`). *Scripture Helps* treats only the warning to Jeroboam (25 [7]). **Proposed:** 13:18 gives the King James, the footnote's JST, and the manual's reading as the manual's (Rasmussen through it); 13:21–22 gives the word the man of God broke; the chapter's lesson is the one its sources draw, that the word the Lord spoke directly was not to be set aside on another's say-so. No note decides more than the sources do about the old prophet's motive.
- **Carmel and the prophets of Baal (18:19–40).** The text says the prophets of Baal (450) were slain (18:22, 40); the 400 prophets of the groves are summoned (18:19) but not named again. The Law's penalty for a prophet who turns Israel to other gods is `[[Deut. 13:5]]` (on the site). *Old Testament Minute* says the number “may have been exaggerated by a later writer” and counts 850 (`ot-minute-1-kings-18` [17]); that is his, and the 850 is a slip (`sc-interpreter.md` §10). **Proposed:** 18:40 says what the text says and what the Law said; no verdict, and no doubt about the number in the writer's voice. *Scripture Helps* 25 [19] (Jehovah's power shown on Baal's own ground, the storm god's) leads.
- **The lying spirit (22:19–23).** *Scripture Helps*: “The Joseph Smith Translation of 2 Chronicles 18:20–22 clarifies that the Lord found a lying spirit among Ahab's prophets rather than put a lying spirit in them,” and its endnote: “It is unclear why Joseph Smith did not alter the same passage in 1 Kings 22:23. However, the scriptures clearly teach that God is ‘a God of truth, and [cannot] lie’” (25 [26], endnote 25); the footnotes at 22:22–23 point to the Chronicles reading. The Student Manual relays Clarke: the Lord “hath permitted or suffered a lying spirit” (`ot-manual-5` [56]). *Scripture Helps* endnote 26 calls the scene a “divine council”; Halverson reads it so (`halverson-path` [31]). **Proposed:** 22:22 gives the King James, then *Scripture Helps* and the JST of Chronicles, then the manual's older reading; the divine council in a clause, pointing to the site's note on it if one exists (`occurrences.mjs` for “council”). **No note says God lies or deceives.**
- **The Church's Elijah.** Elijah is the prophet who held the sealing power (BD “Elijah” [4]; GS “Elijah” [2]: “the last prophet to do so before the time of Jesus Christ”), who was translated, stood with Moses at the Transfiguration and came to Kirtland (D&C 110:13–16). **Proposed:** 17:1 explains the sealing of the heavens as priesthood power, from the Church's sources (`ot-manual-5` [12]–[13] relays Joseph Fielding Smith; `sc-knowhy-348` [11]; Hel. 10:7); the keys are 2 Kings 2's note. **Elias:** the Bible Dictionary's four uses (`bd-elias`) are the frame; Joseph Smith's 10 March 1844 discourse (`jsp-discourse-1844-03-10`) for Elijah's work; Francisco's Elias as Noah (`francisco-elias`) is his and not needed.
- **Christ in 1 Kings.** Jesus' own use of Kings first (Luke 4:25–26; Matt. 6:29; 12:42). Then the Church's: the temple where the Lord puts His name (Oaks), the oxen that “can also represent the strength and power of Jesus Christ” (*Scripture Helps* [23]), the widow who gives her last (Holland, `holland-handful-meal-1996` [13]), Elijah as a type (Parry's table, `parry-elijah-symbol`; Read, `read-elijah-elisha`), the voice of perfect mildness (Hel. 5:30; `davis-zeal`). Solomon as Son of David and a king who reigns in peace is 2 Sam. 7:12–14's (on the site: agree, link). **Proposed:** each in `christ` or at the note named, in the source's words, after the story's meaning. **No chapter makes the temple's furniture, the ravens or the barrel of meal a type of Christ in the writer's words.**

## Settled without the author

Decided by the standard, not asked of the author (Psalms' brief has the rule):

- **No Church, BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* source answers a reader's real question, and an older commentary does:** give its answer as “one older reading,” within the limit of a third of the notes. If the question isn't one a reader would ask, there is no note.
- **A scholar's reading that an RSC, BYU or *Interpreter* publication prints** (Meyers in the RSC's temple volume; Cogan in Rappleye) is cited as that publication's author's, or as quoted in it, beside the Church's, never above it. Barker is not a Latter-day Saint; her chapter in *Glimpses of Lehi's Jerusalem* (`barker-josiah-reform`) is hers.
- **Two Church sources read a verse differently** (Baal: *Scripture Helps* and the Bible Dictionary; the lying spirit: *Scripture Helps* and the Student Manual): give the newer first; where *Scripture Helps* speaks, it leads.
- **A source quoted inside a Church manual** whose work can't be read (Rasmussen, Clarke, Dummelow, Talmage) is cited as the manual; Keil, whom it relays, may be read on Bible Hub and cited as himself.
- **The Church's footnote points somewhere the note does not go:** leave it.
- **A slip in a source** (`sc-interpreter.md` §10: the *Old Testament Minute*'s “1 Samuel 17” headings, “twelve oxen” for twelve yoke, 850 slain; Claybaugh's anointings “in 1 Kings 21”): don't repeat it, and don't cite the page for the sentence that has it.
