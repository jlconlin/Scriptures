At Gibeon the LORD tells a young king to ask for whatever he wants, and the king asks for a heart. The King James calls it “an understanding heart” ([[1 Kgs. 3:9]]), but the two Hebrew words are narrower: *lēb šōmēaʿ*, a hearing heart.[@oshb-wlc] The verb is the ordinary word for hearing, to perceive by ear.[@bdb] Solomon asks for a heart that listens.

He receives it, and then he turns the same verb on the LORD. In the prayer at the temple's dedication the verb occurs fourteen times,[@oshb-wlc] and at its end he asks that the LORD “incline our hearts unto him” ([[1 Kgs. 8:58]]). The LORD answers, “I have heard thy prayer,” and promises that His own heart will be in the house ([[1 Kgs. 9:3]]).

What the rest of the book does with that exchange is the subject of this essay. Solomon's heart is inclined away, by other hands and in the very word he had prayed. His son will not hear the people, and the kingdom tears. And then, with the man who asked for a hearing heart gone, the book does not stop: a prayer is granted at the rival altar in Bethel, the LORD hears in a widow's house in Zidon and answers by fire on Carmel, and He sees a king in sackcloth. Kings never says that those later scenes answer the prayer of chapter 8; that reading is mine, and I will argue it from the words. What it shows, I think, is that the heart Solomon asked for was never his to keep by himself, that the LORD's hearing stops neither at the door of the house nor at the failure of its builder, and that the reason, which Kings does not give and Zenos does, is the Son.

## A heart that hears

Solomon's request is a participle, “a heart hearing,” and the verse says what the hearing is for: “to judge thy people, that I may discern between good and bad” ([[1 Kgs. 3:9]]).[@oshb-wlc] The listening serves judgment, and the next scene, the two mothers and the one child, shows the gift at work; the Student Manual says the prayer “was surely granted, as the incident of the two harlots demonstrates.”[@ot-manual-kings-1]

The Church's manuals keep the King James's “understanding,” and one Latter-day Saint commentator prefers “discerning mind.”[@ot-minute-1-kings-3] Keil keeps the literal word: “a hearing heart, i.e., a heart giving heed to the law and right of God.”[@keil-kings] I keep “hearing” because the book keeps it (the note on [chapter 3](/1-kings/3/) sets out the word).

Two things about the answer matter. The first is that the LORD repeats Solomon's verb: where the King James has “understanding to discern judgment,” the Hebrew is literally *to hear judgment* ([[1 Kgs. 3:11]]).[@oshb-wlc] The second is that the heart is given, not grown: “lo, I have given thee a wise and an understanding heart” ([[1 Kgs. 3:12]]). From chapter 3 the book treats a hearing heart as a gift the LORD gives to someone who asks for it.

## Hear thou in heaven

In chapter 8 the king stands before the altar of the finished temple. The verb occurs fourteen times in twelve verses of chapter 8,[@oshb-wlc] and its refrain is: “hear thou in heaven thy dwelling place: and when thou hearest, forgive” ([[1 Kgs. 8:30]]). The heaven of heavens cannot contain the LORD, let alone this house ([[1 Kgs. 8:27]]); the people will pray *toward* the place, and the LORD will hear *in heaven*.

The petitions reach outward as the prayer goes on: to the stranger “that is not of thy people Israel, but cometh out of a far country for thy name's sake” ([[1 Kgs. 8:41]]), and to Israel carried captive, who, if they “return unto thee with all their heart, and with all their soul, in the land of their enemies,” are to be heard there ([[1 Kgs. 8:48–49]]). *Come, Follow Me* quotes those last verses, under the week's title “Hear Thou in Heaven Their Prayer.”[@cfm-ot-2026-26]

Then, having prayed for the people's prayers, Solomon prays for their hearts: “The LORD our God be with us … That he may incline our hearts unto him, to walk in all his ways” ([[1 Kgs. 8:57–58]]). The verb is *nāṭâ*, to stretch out or bend, and here the LORD is the one bending the heart toward Himself.[@oshb-wlc] The lexicon files the verse under “incline one's own heart unto God and his commands,” with the LORD as subject.[@bdb] The heart the LORD gave in chapter 3 is now a heart the LORD is asked to bend: Solomon asks for Israel's hearts what the LORD had done for his.

The LORD's answer takes up the prayer's own words. “I have heard thy prayer and thy supplication … I have hallowed this house, which thou hast built, to put my name there for ever; and mine eyes and mine heart shall be there perpetually” ([[1 Kgs. 9:3]]). Elder Oaks explains the name: the Lord puts His name in a temple “because he gives authority for his name to be used in the sacred ordinances of that house.”[@oaks-name-1985] The word for “heart” here is the word of chapter 3.[@oshb-wlc] The king who asked for a hearing heart, and then asked the LORD to hear, is answered with “I have heard” and the promise of the LORD's own heart in the house. I read the answer as the prayer's own terms returned: the hearing heart Solomon wanted turns out to be, first of all, the LORD's.

## Inclined

Chapter 11 undoes the prayer of chapter 8 in its own vocabulary. The verb Solomon used for the LORD inclining Israel's heart is the verb the narrator uses, four times, for what happened to Solomon's. The LORD had warned that the nations' daughters “will turn away your heart after their gods” ([[1 Kgs. 11:2]]); “his wives turned away his heart” ([[1 Kgs. 11:3]]); “his wives turned away his heart after other gods” ([[1 Kgs. 11:4]]); and the LORD was angry “because his heart was turned from the LORD God of Israel” ([[1 Kgs. 11:9]]). All four are *nāṭâ*, and in the first three the wives are the ones turning it.[@oshb-wlc] The lexicon lists 11:2–4, “turn (away) heart (cause to apostatize),” in the line just above the one that lists 8:58.[@bdb] What I make of it is mine: the one thing Solomon asked the LORD to do for the people's hearts, other hands did to his, the other way.

What turned the heart, on the Church's reading, was marriage outside the covenant. *Scripture Helps* says that Solomon “violated the Lord's commandment to not marry outside of the covenant,” that some of his marriages were authorized and many were not, and that his “decision to marry outside of the covenant tragically led to him turning his heart away from God.”[@scripture-helps-2-samuel-11] What latter-day scripture says of his wives is the note on [chapter 11](/1-kings/11/).

One more of chapter 8's words comes back in chapter 11. Solomon's refrain had been “when thou hearest, forgive.” The Joseph Smith Translation of the LORD's sentence on him, printed in the Church's footnote at verse 33, reads: “his heart is become as David his father; and he repenteth not as did David his father, that I may forgive him.”[@lds-scriptures] The king who asked the LORD to hear and forgive is the king of whom the revised text says that he did not repent so that the LORD might forgive him. The condition Solomon attached to his own petition for exiles, “if they … repent, and make supplication unto thee” ([[1 Kgs. 8:47]]), is the one he did not meet.

## Hearkened not

The hearing verb next belongs to Solomon's son, with a “not” in front of it. All Israel comes to Rehoboam with a request, and “the king hearkened not unto the people” ([[1 Kgs. 12:15]]). The narrator says it twice ([[1 Kgs. 12:16]]), and both are *šāmaʿ*.[@oshb-wlc] The son of the man with the hearing heart would not hear, and the kingdom tore there.

Jeroboam, who got the ten tribes, then says what he is afraid of. “If this people go up to do sacrifice in the house of the LORD at Jerusalem, then shall the heart of this people turn again unto their lord, even unto Rehoboam king of Judah, and they shall kill me” ([[1 Kgs. 12:27]]). The return he fears is political, a heart turned back to Rehoboam, and the way he expects it to happen is the house. Solomon had prayed that exiles would “return unto thee with all their heart” and be heard when they prayed toward the house; the verb of 12:27, *šûb*, is the verb of 8:47–48.[@oshb-wlc] The exiles of 8:48 pray toward the house from far away; Jeroboam's people would go up to it. The link is the verb and the house, not the direction. So he builds two calves; *Scripture Helps* says he feared that the people “would travel south to worship in the temple in Jerusalem and eventually rejoin the Southern Kingdom.”[@scripture-helps-1-kings]

By the end of chapter 12, then, the hearing heart has been inclined away, the son's ear is shut, and a heart turning back by way of the house has become the thing a king builds idols to prevent. If the prayer of chapter 8 is to be answered now, it will be answered away from the house, for people who are not going up to it.

## Where the LORD heard

Here is the fact the rest of the essay rests on. The verb “hear” occurs sixty times in 1 Kings, and in most of them someone hears news, or a king hears or will not hear a petition.[@oshb-wlc] Only twice does the book say that the LORD has heard: 9:3 and 17:22. The first is spoken to Solomon over the temple. The second is in Zidon.

Before that, a prayer is granted at the altar the calves stand on: Jeroboam's withered hand is restored when the man of God from Judah “besought the LORD” ([[1 Kgs. 13:6]]). The text does not say that the LORD heard, and the king did not change;[@scripture-helps-1-kings] but the first prayer in the book after the dedication is answered at Bethel, for the man who built Bethel to keep hearts from turning back.

Then the word itself. Elijah has been sent out of Israel to Zarephath, “which belongeth to Zidon” ([[1 Kgs. 17:9]]), to a widow who is not an Israelite and whose country worships Baal.[@scripture-helps-1-kings] Her son dies, and Elijah prays over him. “And the LORD heard the voice of Elijah; and the soul of the child came into him again” ([[1 Kgs. 17:22]]). To hear someone's voice is the lexicon's idiom for hearing and granting.[@bdb] Nothing in the scene faces Jerusalem: no house, no altar, no Israelite but the prophet. The one time after chapter 9 that the narrator says the LORD heard, He heard for a Gentile widow in a foreign house.

Set that beside the prayer of chapter 8. Solomon had asked the LORD to hear “a stranger, that is not of thy people Israel” when the stranger “shall come and pray toward this house.” This stranger does not come; the hearing goes to her. I take that as the book's first answer to the prayer, larger than the petition.

Jesus used the scene at Nazareth. He said that “many widows were in Israel in the days of Elias … But unto none of them was Elias sent, save unto Sarepta, a city of Sidon, unto a woman that was a widow” ([[Luke 4:25–26]]), and the synagogue “were filled with wrath” ([[Luke 4:28]]). The Church's footnotes tie His words to this chapter,[@lds-scriptures] and *Scripture Helps* says what He was doing with them: He “used the widow's example to condemn His own people for their unbelief in Him.”[@scripture-helps-1-kings] What Luke records is the Savior setting the widow of Sidon before His own town, and the town's anger. That He chose her, of all the widows in Israel, is what this adds; the reading stays mine.

## Thou hast turned their heart back

At Carmel Elijah prays again. “Hear me, O LORD, hear me” ([[1 Kgs. 18:37]]) is not the hearing verb. It is *ʿănēnî*, “answer me,” twice,[@oshb-wlc] the verb of Baal's silence that morning, “nor any that answered” ([[1 Kgs. 18:26]]), and the lexicon counts this verse among those where God answers “by deed,” by fire.[@bdb] So Carmel is not a third “the LORD heard.” It is an answer, and the fire is the answer.

The second thing is what Elijah asks the answer to show: “that this people may know that thou art the LORD God, and that thou hast turned their heart back again” ([[1 Kgs. 18:37]]). Keil reads the last clause as back “from idols to Thyself,”[@keil-kings] and the Church's footnote at “heart” sends the reader to Nephi's “he did visit me, and did soften my heart” ([[1 Ne. 2:16]]) and Alma's “Behold, he changed their hearts” ([[Alma 5:7]]).[@lds-scriptures] The footnote only links the passages; reading Carmel's turning as the LORD's own act on a heart is my inference from the verses it chose.

That is what Solomon prayed for in 8:58, and I should be exact about the words. Elijah's verb for turning the heart, *sābab*, is neither Solomon's *nāṭâ* in 8:58, nor the *šûb* of 8:48 and 12:27, nor the verb of Malachi's promise that Elijah would “turn the heart” ([[Mal. 4:6]]).[@oshb-wlc] What 8:58 and 18:37 share is not a word but a subject: in both it is the LORD who turns a heart, and in both a man asks Him to. Malachi is different again; there the turning is an errand given to Elijah, and no one asks for it. Elijah on Carmel does not claim to have turned Israel's heart with his sermon or his altar; he prays that the fire will let the people know the LORD has done it, and when the fire falls they say what he asked them to know: “The LORD, he is the God; the LORD, he is the God” ([[1 Kgs. 18:39]]).

## When Ahab heard

The last king in the book of whom the narrator uses the word for hearing the LORD's word is Ahab, whom the Bible Dictionary calls “the most wicked and most powerful of the kings of northern Israel.”[@bd-ahab] Elijah has just pronounced the sentence for Naboth's murder. “And it came to pass, when Ahab heard those words, that he rent his clothes, and put sackcloth upon his flesh, and fasted, and lay in sackcloth, and went softly” ([[1 Kgs. 21:27]]). “Heard” is *šāmaʿ*.[@oshb-wlc]

Then the LORD speaks, though no one has prayed. At Bethel a prophet entreated; at Zarephath and Carmel Elijah cried out. The LORD turns to the prophet who has just delivered the sentence and points: “Seest thou how Ahab humbleth himself before me? because he humbleth himself before me, I will not bring the evil in his days” ([[1 Kgs. 21:29]]). The verb for the LORD is “see,” not “hear.”[@oshb-wlc] The king heard; the LORD saw.

The word for Ahab's humbling is the word of the LORD's answer to Solomon's prayer in its other telling. In Chronicles the night vision after the dedication runs on: “If my people, which are called by my name, shall humble themselves, and pray, and seek my face, and turn from their wicked ways; then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin” ([[2 Chr. 7:14]]). “Humble themselves” there is *kānaʿ*, the verb of 21:29, twice,[@oshb-wlc] and the lexicon lists the two verses together.[@bdb] The next king in Kings to humble himself with that verb is Josiah, to whom the LORD says, “because thine heart was tender, and thou hast humbled thyself before the LORD … I also have heard thee” ([[2 Kgs. 22:19]]).[@oshb-wlc] The pairing is mine; no source I have read makes it. But the verb is the text's: Ahab in sackcloth is doing, in the word Chronicles uses, the first thing the LORD named at the dedication, and the LORD says that He sees it.

What he receives must be stated exactly. The LORD does not say “I have heard thee,” as He will to Josiah, and the text does not say that Ahab was forgiven. What it records is a deferral: “I will not bring the evil in his days: but in his son's days will I bring the evil upon his house.” The Student Manual's one comment on the verses is just that: “Because Ahab repented, the ‘evil’ was delayed until Ahab's son was king.”[@ot-manual-5] The next chapter shows Ahab hating the prophet “for he doth not prophesy good concerning me, but evil” ([[1 Kgs. 22:8]]) and dying as that prophet foretold. (The note “Why the LORD saw Ahab's humbling” on [chapter 21](/1-kings/21/) gives the sequel.)

## Because of thy Son

Kings leaves one question unasked. Why is prayer that never faces the house heard at all? Solomon's petitions, even the exile's, were for prayers made toward the house, and the book shows a widow's son raised in Zidon, fire on Carmel and a king seen in sackcloth in Jezreel, without saying why. The reason is in the Book of Mormon, and the Church's own footnote sends a reader there.

To Zoramites cast out of their synagogues ([[Alma 33:2]]) Alma quotes Zenos, whose prayer is a list of places, none of them the temple: “thou hast heard my prayer, even when I was in the wilderness”; “when I did cry unto thee in my field”; “when I did turn to my house thou didst hear me”; “when I did turn unto my closet”; “heard my cries in the midst of thy congregations”; “thou hast also heard me when I have been cast out” ([[Alma 33:4–10]]). The Church's footnote at the first verse points to 1 Kings 8:47, Solomon's petition for Israel praying from far away.[@lds-scriptures] The footnote does not say why. I read Zenos's thanksgiving as an answer to Solomon's prayer for the exile.

And Zenos says what Solomon could not. “Thou didst hear me because of mine afflictions and my sincerity; and it is because of thy Son that thou hast been thus merciful unto me … for thou hast turned thy judgments away from me, because of thy Son” ([[Alma 33:11]]). Alma repeats it: “Thou hast turned away thy judgments because of thy Son” ([[Alma 33:13]]). The hearing in the wilderness and the field and the closet, the hearing that does not need the temple, is God's mercy because of the Son. That is the sentence Kings is missing. I read the widow's son and the fire on Carmel by it: scripture asks that every prayer be made in the name of the Son ([[Moses 5:8]]) and rests every mercy on “the merits, and mercy, and grace of the Holy Messiah” ([[2 Ne. 2:8]]), and what was granted so far from the house was granted, on my reading, by the mercy Zenos names.

Latter-day revelation then shows who was hearing. On 3 April 1836, in a house dedicated with a prayer the Church's footnotes set beside Solomon's,[@lds-scriptures] the Lord stood before two men and said: “I am he who liveth, I am he who was slain; I am your advocate with the Father. Behold, your sins are forgiven you; you are clean before me” ([[D&C 110:4–5]]). That is Solomon's refrain, “when thou hearest, forgive,” spoken by the Son in the first person, and the footnote at “forgiven” points to the question asked in Capernaum, “Who can forgive sins, but God alone?” ([[Luke 5:21]]).[@lds-scriptures] Then: “I have accepted this house, and my name shall be here; and I will manifest myself to my people in mercy in this house” ([[D&C 110:7]]), and the footnote at “accepted” points to the Chronicles twin of 9:3.[@lds-scriptures] The One who said “I have heard thy prayer” to Solomon, and who heard Elijah's voice in Zidon, is Jehovah, and “Jehovah is the premortal Jesus Christ,” the Bible Dictionary says, citing this very vision.[@bd-jehovah] Zenos was heard because of the Son, he says; and the LORD who answered in Kings is that Son, who stands in His house as advocate with the Father and forgives.

Then Elijah comes into the room. The prophet whose prayer was the second “the LORD heard” in Kings, and whose cry on Carmel was that the LORD turns hearts back, is given Malachi's errand: “To turn the hearts of the fathers to the children, and the children to the fathers” ([[D&C 110:15]]). The footnote at his name sends the reader to 1 Kings 17:1 and to Luke 4:25, to the widow.[@lds-scriptures] So the three things this book has been about since Gibeon, a heart that hears, a LORD who hears and forgives, and a heart turned back, stand together in one room; the One doing the hearing and forgiving is the Son, and the turning of hearts is handed to the man who said on Carmel that it was the LORD's to do.

## Lord, give me an understanding heart

The Student Manual quotes Elder Howard W. Hunter on how this reaches us: “If the Lord was pleased because of that which Solomon had asked of him, surely he would be pleased with each of us if we had the desire to acquire an understanding heart. This must come from conscious effort coupled with faith and firm determination. An understanding heart results from the experiences we have in life if we keep the commandments of God,” and, “Our prayer could well be as was Solomon's, ‘Lord, give me an understanding heart.’”[@ot-manual-kings-1] The first thing the book teaches a disciple: a hearing heart is asked for, and it is given, to one who seeks it with the effort, faith and obedience Elder Hunter names. “Lo, I have given thee” ([[1 Kgs. 3:12]]) is how the LORD describes it. The Book of Mormon's “mighty change in us, or in our hearts,” wrought by the Spirit ([[Mosiah 5:2]]), which “faith and repentance bringeth” ([[Hel. 15:7]]), is another gift; setting Solomon's beside it is my comparison, and I make it because both are given, not grown.

The second thing is Solomon's second prayer, and his failure. A heart given can be inclined away, and not only by its owner. *Come, Follow Me* points the reader of chapter 11 to the influences “that could turn your heart away from the Lord and toward ‘other gods,’”[@cfm-ot-2026-26] and the book's answer is that they turned the wisest heart in Israel. The prayer that meets that danger is 8:58, that the LORD “may incline our hearts unto him,” and Carmel shows that He does: Elijah says the LORD turned the people's hearts back, and the Church's footnote reads that turning as the softened heart of Nephi and the changed hearts of Alma 5.[@lds-scriptures] A disciple seeks a hearing heart as Solomon received one, by asking for it, with the effort Elder Hunter adds.

The third thing is for the disciple whose heart has already been turned, and who is not in the house. God hears, Zenos says, in the wilderness, the field and the closet, because of the Son. The advocate with the Father is the One who said “your sins are forgiven you” in His own house, and prayer in His name is heard where there is no house to face. Zenos was heard, he says, “because of mine afflictions and my sincerity,” and because of the Son: the sincerity is asked of the one praying, and the mercy rests on the Son.

Ahab marks the limit, and the limit is also a mercy. The LORD saw Ahab's humbling and answered it, and the text records nothing more of him than the humbling and the deferral. The promise of 2 Chronicles 7:14 goes on from humbling to prayer, to seeking the LORD's face and to turning from one's way, and the disciple who goes on to those is the exile of chapter 8 returning “with all their heart,” for whom the word is not “deferred” but “forgive.” The LORD who answered Solomon's “when thou hearest, forgive” is the Son, who stands as advocate with the Father and says “your sins are forgiven you,” and the heart that hears Him is His gift, asked for and given.
