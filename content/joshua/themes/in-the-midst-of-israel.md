The Hebrew word for “midst,” *qereb*, stands twenty times in Joshua, and most of them say who and what is inside Israel; the others are the midst of Egypt and of the nations Israel passed through (24:5, 17), a sign “among you” (4:6), and the Levites’ lack of a part “among you” (18:7), which the page [Ye Have No Part in the LORD](/joshua/themes/no-part-in-the-lord/) follows.[@oshb-wlc] The first thing in the midst is the LORD: “to morrow the LORD will do wonders among you” (3:5), “Hereby ye shall know that the living God is among you” (3:10). Then Rahab, a Canaanite, whose household is “left … without the camp of Israel” (6:23) and who two verses later “dwelleth in Israel,” in the Hebrew “in the midst of Israel” (6:25).[@oshb-wlc] Then Achan, of the tribe of Judah: “There is an accursed thing in the midst of thee, O Israel … until ye take away the accursed thing from among you” (7:13), and the LORD says of it, “neither will I be with you any more, except ye destroy the accursed from among you” (7:12). Then the stranger “that were conversant among them” at Ebal (8:35), the Gibeonites who “dwell among us” (9:7, 22), the Geshurites and Maachathites Israel “expelled not,” who “dwell among the Israelites until this day” (13:13), and at the end the “strange gods which are among you” that Joshua tells the people to put away (24:23).[@oshb-wlc]

Read together, these uses say where Israel’s center is and what may share it. The LORD is in the midst, and the camp is to be holy because of it: “the LORD thy God walketh in the midst of thy camp … therefore shall thy camp be holy: that he see no unclean thing in thee, and turn away from thee” (Deut. 23:14).[@lds-scriptures] A Canaanite harlot who confessed Him can be brought into that midst; an Israelite’s stolen accursed thing cannot stay there, or He will not. Latter-day Saint scholars have set Rahab and Achan against each other, the outsider who becomes “the true insider” and the insider who fails; this page adds the word that carries the reversal, *qereb*, and follows it to the book’s last command, to put the strange gods out of the same midst with the same verb that put out the accursed thing.[@calabro-herem][@ot-minute-josh-7] Birth did not keep Achan in it, and birth did not keep Rahab out. What the midst holds decides whether the LORD stays.

## “The living God is among you”

Before any outsider enters, Joshua names what is already in Israel’s midst. “Sanctify yourselves: for to morrow the LORD will do wonders among you” (3:5); “Hereby ye shall know that the living God is among you” (3:10). Both are *bĕqirbĕkem*, in your midst, and the second gives the consequence: He “will without fail drive out from before you the Canaanites, and the Hittites, and the Hivites” and the rest.[@oshb-wlc] Moses had tied the camp’s holiness to the same fact, in the same noun: the LORD “walketh in the midst of thy camp,” and so the camp is to be holy, “that he see no unclean thing in thee, and turn away from thee” (Deut. 23:14).[@lds-scriptures][@oshb-wlc] The law put outside that camp the leper, the person with a bodily discharge, and “whosoever is defiled by the dead” (Num. 5:2), and those who had killed in war, “both yourselves and your captives,” for seven days (Num. 31:19).[@lds-scriptures] This is the camp Rahab is set outside of.

## Without the camp, then in the midst

Rahab’s request was for life, and it was made in the LORD’s name: “swear unto me by the LORD, since I have shewed you kindness, that ye will also shew kindness unto my father’s house … and that ye will save alive my father, and my mother, and my brethren, and my sisters” (2:12–13). She had already said why: “the LORD your God, he is God in heaven above, and in earth beneath” (2:11). When Jericho falls the spies “brought out all her kindred, and left them without the camp of Israel” (6:23). The phrase is the law’s, “without the camp,” and Joshua has it only here.[@oshb-wlc] The text gives no reason. The Cambridge commentary and the Pulpit commentary supply the law’s: “As heathen, they were unclean, and must therefore remain a specified time, probably seven days, ‘without the camp,’” and “They were as yet, as Gentiles, unclean (cf. Numbers 5:2; Numbers 31:19).”[@cambridge-joshua][@pulpit-joshua] Keil reads it only as “a place of safety outside the camp of Israel.”[@keil-joshua]

Two verses later the distance is gone. “Joshua saved Rahab the harlot alive, and her father’s household, and all that she had; and she dwelleth in Israel even unto this day” (6:25). The King James “in Israel” is *bĕqereb yiśrāʾēl*, in the midst of Israel, the noun of 3:5 and 3:10.[@oshb-wlc] Amy Fisher notes that the Hebrew is “much stronger … than in the King James English,” a word for “the inward parts of a body,” so that Rahab “dwelled ‘in the midst’ of Israel, or even thickly in the midst, utterly embedded within the people, a far cry from her marginal existence in Jericho.”[@fisher-rahab] *Scripture Helps*: “Rahab and her family became part of the Israelite nation in the promised land,” and “she is mentioned in Matthew as an ancestor of Jesus Christ.”[@scripture-helps-josh] The text does not explain what happened between verses 23 and 25, and no source I read does; the Student Manual quotes an older encyclopedia that her later marriage “implies her previous conversion to Judaism.”[@ot-manual-josh] The note on [[Josh. 6:25]] gathers the traditions.

## “An accursed thing in the midst of thee”

In the next chapter the movement runs the other way, and the word is the same. Achan, “of the tribe of Judah, took of the accursed thing” (7:1), and the LORD tells Joshua, “There is an accursed thing in the midst of thee, O Israel: thou canst not stand before thine enemies, until ye take away the accursed thing from among you” (7:13). The phrases are *bĕqirbĕkā* and *miqqirbĕkem*, in your midst and from your midst.[@oshb-wlc] The verse before says what the thing in the midst costs: “neither will I be with you any more, except ye destroy the accursed from among you” (7:12). Keil: “until this ban had been removed from their midst, the Lord would not help them any further.”[@keil-joshua] *Scripture Helps*: “Even though Achan acted alone, the Lord attributed the sin to all of Israel. As a result, they lost the Lord’s protection.”[@scripture-helps-josh] Achan and all he had are taken out “unto the valley of Achor” (7:24), and the name stays “unto this day” (7:26); the note on [[Josh. 7:26]] follows the name.

The reversal has been noticed. David Calabro writes that Achan “appears at first to be the quintessential insider: an Israelite soldier of the tribe of Judah,” while Rahab, “a Canaanite harlot and thus the quintessential outsider, distinguishes herself as one who truly sees God’s work,” and concludes, “Rahab is thus revealed in the end to be the true insider, essentially taking the place of Achan in the very same tribe of Judah.”[@calabro-herem] The *Old Testament Minute* calls Achan’s fate “a somewhat ironic inversion of the fate of Rahab and her family, all Canaanites.”[@ot-minute-josh-7] Neither mentions the noun. The narrator uses one word, “midst,” for where Rahab comes to dwell and for where the accursed thing must not stay; the observation is this page’s. It is a common word, and the page does not claim the narrator planted it; it claims that the two verses, read side by side, say what the midst will and will not hold. Rahab is in the midst by an oath sworn in the LORD’s name and kept by Joshua; the accursed thing in the midst would keep Him from it (7:12).

## “Ye dwell among us”

The Gibeonites are the book’s next outsiders, and the book says of them what it said of Rahab. Israel asks the disguised Hivites, “Peradventure ye dwell among us; and how shall we make a league with you?” (9:7), and when the ruse is found, Joshua asks, “Wherefore have ye beguiled us, saying, We are very far from you; when ye dwell among us?” (9:22). Both are *qereb*, and the narrator adds that they “dwelt among them” (9:16) and “were among them” (10:1).[@oshb-wlc] The league is kept because it was sworn “by the LORD God of Israel” (9:19), and the same verb describes the sparing of the Gibeonites and of Rahab’s household: Israel made peace “to let them live” (9:15), as Rahab had asked the spies to swear to “save alive” her house (2:13) and “Joshua saved Rahab the harlot alive” (6:25). Deuteronomy forbids that sparing in the cities of the seven nations, with the same verb, “thou shalt save alive nothing that breatheth” (Deut. 20:16), and Gibeon is Hivite, one of the seven; Calabro notes that Rahab and Gibeon “are the only ones who ask for mercy in the text, and they obtain it.”[@oshb-wlc][@lds-scriptures][@calabro-herem] The book itself says it: “There was not a city that made peace with the children of Israel, save the Hivites the inhabitants of Gibeon” (11:19).

Gibeon’s place in the midst is not Rahab’s. Joshua says, “ye are cursed,” and makes them “bondmen, and hewers of wood and drawers of water for the house of my God” (9:23). The Pulpit commentary observes that “equality of rights had never been promised them,” and the Student Manual that they “became the perpetual slaves of Israel”; the *Old Testament Minute* reads the servitude as Joshua’s answer to a deception he could not punish with the ban “on account of the peace treaty.”[@pulpit-joshua][@ot-manual-josh][@ot-minute-josh-9] Keil holds that the princes “were not justified in taking the oath” without asking the LORD, and were bound to keep it once sworn.[@keil-joshua] Yet the Church’s footnote on “hewers” leads to the covenant in Moab, where “thy stranger that is in thy camp, from the hewer of thy wood unto the drawer of thy water” stands among those who “enter into covenant with the LORD” (Deut. 29:11–12), and Kerry Muhlestein reads the league as one that “incorporated them into the house of Israel.”[@lds-scriptures][@muhlestein-ruth] The note on [[Josh. 9:23]] weighs both.

The stranger is in the midst too, by the law. At Ebal the law is read to “all the congregation of Israel, with the women, and the little ones, and the strangers that were conversant among them” (8:35), in the Hebrew the stranger “who walks in their midst,” and “as well the stranger, as he that was born among them” stands before the ark (8:33).[@oshb-wlc] Muhlestein counts this among the ways in: “Resident aliens were among the group with whom Joshua reestablished that covenant at Mount Ebal (see Joshua 8:30–35),” so that “the foreigner could, through covenant, become part of Israel.”[@muhlestein-ruth] David and Jo Ann Seely read the stranger’s standing more narrowly, as one who “did not share full membership in the covenant community.”[@seely-cry-widow] On either reading the stranger stood where the native-born stood when the law was read.

## “Until this day”

The midst also holds what Israel failed to remove, and the book says so in the same words it used of Rahab. “The Geshurites and the Maachathites dwell among the Israelites until this day” (13:13); “the Canaanites dwell among the Ephraimites unto this day, and serve under tribute” (16:10). Both are *yāšab bĕqereb*, the verb and noun of 6:25.[@oshb-wlc] Being in the midst, then, is not of itself a blessing. Joshua’s last warning is about “these that remain among you” (23:7, 12), that “they shall be snares and traps unto you, and scourges in your sides, and thorns in your eyes” (23:13); the theme page [Rest Round About](/joshua/themes/rest-round-about/) follows that warning. What sets Rahab apart from the Geshurites in the same phrase is not where she lives but what she said at Jericho, “the LORD your God, he is God,” and the oath in His name that brought her in. The line, as this page reads it, runs between the outsider who confessed the LORD and the nations that did not, not between Canaanite and Israelite; Calabro draws it in the same place for the ban, which “had nothing to do with ethnic or national affiliation and everything to do with personal righteousness and willingness to maintain a covenant relationship with the Lord.”[@calabro-herem]

## “Put away the strange gods which are among you”

The book’s last use of the word is a command. At Shechem Joshua says, “Now therefore put away … the strange gods which are among you, and incline your heart unto the LORD God of Israel” (24:23). The verb “put away,” *hēsîr*, is the verb of 7:13, “until ye take away the accursed thing from among you,” and the noun is the same: the gods are to be removed from the midst as the accursed thing was.[@oshb-wlc] Twice, then, the book names something that must leave Israel’s midst, and both times it is a thing Israel brought in, stolen spoil and foreign gods. It never names a person who confessed the LORD as something to be removed. The note on [[Josh. 24:23]] asks what gods these were.

## “No more strangers and foreigners”

The New Testament names Rahab for faith: “By faith the harlot Rahab perished not with them that believed not, when she had received the spies with peace” (Heb. 11:31), and Matthew’s genealogy has “Salmon begat Booz of Rachab” (Matt. 1:5), so that, as *Scripture Helps* says, she is “an ancestor of Jesus Christ.”[@lds-scriptures][@scripture-helps-josh] Fisher: “Nothing demonstrates Rahab’s complete assimilation and unification into her new community more than her marriage and prestigious place in the genealogies of the people.”[@fisher-rahab] The Bible Dictionary names the way in: “A person who is of non-Israelite lineage becomes a member of the house of Israel through faith in Jesus Christ when it is accompanied by baptism in water and the reception of the Holy Ghost.”[@bd-adoption] President Russell M. Nelson: “The literal seed of Abraham and those who are gathered into his family by adoption receive these promised blessings—predicated upon acceptance of the Lord and obedience to his commandments.”[@nelson-1995-children-of-the-covenant]

Paul’s words to Gentile converts, which do not name her, fit Rahab’s movement: once “strangers from the covenants of promise,” they are “made nigh by the blood of Christ,” and “no more strangers and foreigners, but fellowcitizens with the saints, and of the household of God” (Eph. 2:12–13, 19).[@lds-scriptures] Reading Ephesians beside Joshua 6 is this page’s step. The LORD who is “among you” at the Jordan (3:10) is Jehovah, “the premortal Jesus Christ,” and the Nephites heard Him say,[@bd-jehovah] “as many as have received me, to them have I given to become the sons of God; and even so will I to as many as shall believe on my name” (3 Ne. 9:17).[@lds-scriptures]

For a disciple the two movements of the book stand as warnings and promises in one. Being born inside did not keep Achan in the midst, and being born outside did not keep Rahab out of it. *Come, Follow Me* turns the Lord’s words after Ai on the reader, to “determine if ‘there is an accursed thing’ in your life that you need to remove (Joshua 7:13).”[@cfm-ot-2026-21] Christopher Morgan, writing of Achan, says that Israel’s “main threat was not an outside enemy but corruption from within.”[@morgan-achan] The book’s question is not who was born in the camp. It is what is in the midst with the LORD, and whether He can stay.
