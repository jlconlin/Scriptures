## What a chapter is for here

Joshua is 658 verses in 24 chapters: two kinds of writing, story (1–12, 22–24) and lists of boundaries and towns (13–21). The reader's questions: *Why were whole cities, children too, to be “utterly destroyed,” and what does “accursed” mean? Rahab lied to save the spies; is she commended for it, and was she really a harlot? Why did Achan's sons and daughters die for his theft? Did the sun stand still, and what is the book of Jasher? Why keep an oath obtained by a lie (the Gibeonites)? Did the conquest happen as told, and what has been found at Jericho and Ai? Why does 11:23 say Joshua “took the whole land” when 13:1 says “very much land” remained? Why the long boundary lists, and why does Caleb get Hebron? What were the cities of refuge and the Levites' cities for? Why did the altar by Jordan nearly start a war? What does it mean that Joshua's name is the name Jesus? What happened at Shechem, and why does Joshua tell a willing people “Ye cannot serve the LORD” (24:19)?* (The `christ` section then applies the chapter to the reader, in statements: `STANDARDS.md` §1.)

**Say what the story is doing before what it means.** Joshua is told as the fulfillment of the LORD's promise and of Moses' charge, and it says so: “There failed not ought of any good thing which the LORD had spoken unto the house of Israel; all came to pass” ([[Josh. 21:45]]). *Scripture Helps* puts it the same way: the book “demonstrates the Lord’s faithfulness in keeping His covenant with Abraham” [4]. A note says first where the episode sits in that story (a promise kept, a command obeyed or broken, a memorial set up), then what it says, then how scripture and the Church's sources read it.

**Echoes of Moses are the book's own.** The LORD says it: “as I was with Moses, so I will be with thee” ([[Josh. 1:5]]; 3:7; 4:14). The Jordan answers the Red Sea (4:23), the shoes at 5:15 Exodus 3:5, the spear at Ai the raised hands against Amalek (`ot-minute-josh-8` [4]), the spies Numbers 13 (`ot-minute-josh-2` [5]). Esplin (`esplin-as-with-moses` [7]) reads these “parallels” as showing “God keeping His promise to be with His prophet and people.” **Proposed:** a note sets an echo side by side when the text or a source makes it, and says what it shows about the LORD; it does not build a Moses–Joshua typology of its own.

**The allotment chapters (13–21) get a thread, not a note per town.** As with Numbers' lists: a `thread` that says what the list is for (the promise of 1:6 carried out “By lot,” 14:2; what is still not taken, 13:1–6; 15:63; 16:10; 17:12–13), and notes only where a reader stops: Caleb at eighty-five (14:6–14); Achsah's request (15:16–19); Jerusalem not taken (15:63); Zelophehad's daughters (17:3–6); the house of Joseph wanting more (17:14–18); the tabernacle at Shiloh and the survey of the seven tribes (18:1–10); Dan moving north (19:47); Joshua's own city last (19:49–50); the cities of refuge (20) and the Levites (21), which point to Numbers and Deuteronomy; the summary of 21:43–45. Two to five notes and a clear `thread` are a full chapter; 12 and 16–19 may be shorter still. `setting` may fall under the checker's word count; ignore the warning. **Don't pad**, and don't explain a town no reader asks about. Where a place matters, the volume guide *The Land* (`#places`, `#inheritance`) and the period guide's map are linked, not retold.

**Graphic passages** (the slaughter at Jericho, Ai and the southern cities; Achan's stoning and burning, 7:25; the kings hanged and their necks trodden, 8:29; 10:24–27; the horses houghed, 11:6, 9): say what the text says plainly and briefly; describe rather than quote (`STANDARDS.md` §3).

## Divisions

The Bible Dictionary (“Joshua, book of”) gives two parts, “(1) the conquest of Canaan (Josh. 1–12); (2) the allotment of the land among the tribes and Joshua’s final exhortations (Josh. 13–24),” and inside the first, “Josh. 1–6” (the crossing, Gilgal, Jericho) set apart from “a condensed account of the victories” [1–2]; it ends the second with Josh. 22–24 [3]. *Scripture Helps* [5–7] divides 1–12, 13–22, 23–24; the *Old Testament Minute* (Smoot) 1–12, 13–21 and an epilogue 22–24 (`ot-minute-josh-22` [4]), and within the first, “the crossing of the Jordan [1:1–5:12]; the conquest [5:13–12:24]” (`ot-minute-josh-1` [5]). **Proposed:** four divisions, the Bible Dictionary's two parts with the conquest split where Israel turns from the crossing and its ceremonies to war, and the last three chapters together as the Bible Dictionary and Smoot have them. Chapter 5 stays whole in the first division; its `thread` says the captain of the host (5:13–15) opens the war, and chapter 6's `setting` picks up from him.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, which later scripture takes up which line) is the drafter's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **`when`.** The book gives few dates: the crossing on “the tenth day of the first month” (4:19), the Passover on “the fourteenth day” (5:10), Caleb's “forty and five years” since Kadesh (14:10), the farewell “a long time after” (23:1). **Proposed:** a chapter's `when` is from the text (“After the death of Moses, at the Jordan,” “At Gilgal, in the first month,” “Late in Joshua's life, at Shechem”); no B.C. year. The volume guide *The Conquest, the Judges, and One Kingdom* gives the one dating the Church's pages offer and calls it that (“between 1250 and 1200 B.C.,” `ot-manual-judges-1-12`); chapter 1's `setting` may point there in a clause.
- **Names of God.** Copy any phrase with LORD, Lord or GOD from `data/kjv/joshua.json` (22:22, “The LORD God of gods,” is on the site at `[[Ps. 50:1]]`). The LORD of Joshua is the premortal Christ: said where it answers a question, in a clause, pointing to `[[Ex. 3:14]]`.
- **Hebrew only where it answers a reader's question** (`STANDARDS.md` §3). Words that pay: *ḥērem*, “accursed” (6:17–18; point to Deut. 7:2 for the word itself); *zônâ*, “harlot” (2:1); *ḥesed*, “kindness” (2:12); “rolled away” and Gilgal (5:9); Achan, Achor and “troubled” (7:25–26); “Ed” (22:34); “flood” (24:2, *Scripture Helps* has it); Joshua's name (point to Num. 13:16). `hebrew.mjs` takes the King James number and prints the Hebrew number beside any verse that differs; cite the King James verse. No more than about a quarter of a chapter's notes are Language notes, and the list chapters need almost none.
- **The Joseph Smith Translation.** The Church's footnotes have one JST reading in Joshua, at 2:5 (“wot” becomes “know”), beside the verse: no note. Nyman (`nyman-jst-historical` [5], [8]) says “Joshua and Judges have only one verse changed in each,” and gives Joshua's as 11:20: “For it was of the Lord to destroy them utterly because they hardened their hearts.” That change is **not** among the Gospel Library's footnotes, so a note may use it (“Contested,” the hardened hearts); read it in the manuscript (Old Testament Revision 2 in the Joseph Smith Papers, `jsp-ot2`) before quoting it, and cite Nyman for what he says it shows.
- **Scripture reads Joshua.** The New Testament: Rahab's faith and works ([[Heb. 11:31]]; [[James 2:25]]), her place in Christ's line ([[Matt. 1:5]]), Jericho's walls ([[Heb. 11:30]]), Joshua as “Jesus” ([[Acts 7:45]]; [[Heb. 4:8]]), Joseph's bones ([[Heb. 11:22]]). The Book of Mormon: the Canaanites “ripe in iniquity” and the land given to the righteous ([[1 Ne. 17:32–35]]); the earth that “goeth back” ([[Hel. 12:14–15]], the Church's footnote at 10:12); the decoy of [[Alma 52:19–26]] beside Ai (`brandt-conquest` [9]); Zemnarihah's tree ([[3 Ne. 4:28]]) beside 8:29 (`ot-minute-josh-8` [6]; `belnap-fell-tree`); “we might have enjoyed our possessions” ([[1 Ne. 17:21]]) read through Josh. 1:15 (`bowen-yrs` [41–42]). Where scripture says what a passage meant, say so as scripture's reading; an echo someone identifies is that author's. **No comparison panel, and nothing is written for a Book of Mormon chapter** (`AUTHORING.md`).
- **Other books on the site and the volume guides.** Besides the table, `occurrences.mjs joshua "<phrase>" --site all` finds what the site already says (Deuteronomy 31 and 34, Numbers 13–14 and 26–36, Psalms 114 and 135, Micah 6:5, Hosea): read and agree. The guides: *The Conquest, the Judges, and One Kingdom* (`#rulers`, `#nations`, `#map`; its Joshua rows already summarize the book), *The Land* (`#places`, `#inheritance`), *The Law and the Covenant* (`#renewals`), *The Temple and Worship* (`#tabernacle`, `#priests`), *Women and Marriage* (`#the-women`, `#the-law`). Point from the chapter in a clause; don't retell.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports an arc that joins chapters in a way a reader would not see chapter by chapter; `propose-themes` and Codex propose the list at the end of the book. No theme page is written until the author chooses it.

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter; don't re-explain what another chapter explains, point with a `[[reference]]`; a Witness of Christ does not open by saying what the chapter lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer, since the site shows it alone as the note's card; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; “In plain words” adds nothing to the verse; Hebrew only where it changes what the reader understands; don't join two verses yourself and call it the text's meaning; application only in `christ`, as statements resting on what it cites, never questions or exhortation; **no military commentary** (tactics, numbers, routes) beyond what the reader needs to follow the story.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that 24 chapters say the same thing. Quotations were copied from the pages named on 2026-10-23; re-read the page before using one.

- **The herem: Jericho, Ai and the cities “utterly destroyed” (6:17–21; 8:24–26; 10:28–40; 11:11–21).** The Church's newest word is *Scripture Helps*' appendix: “From the Book of Mormon we learn that the destruction of the Canaanites was a consequence of their rebellion against the Lord” [20]; God “offered prolonged opportunities for them to repent” [21]; it “reflects both His justice and His desire to protect His covenant people from the corrupting influence of those who had completely rejected Him” [21]; and “we should not think that examples of violence in the Old Testament would ever be justification for committing violence today” [6]. Calabro (`calabro-herem`, RSC 2025) is the study for Joshua: the narrative leaves “its moral implications” unaddressed [82]; the war texts of the region show “hagiographic hyperbole,” so “God speaks in these texts according to the exaggerated idiom of that time” [142–143]; “What actually played out was likely not wholesale extermination” and “Many Canaanites likely fled” [143]; and the text's own tests: only Gibeon and Rahab's household “ask for mercy in the text, and they obtain it” [157], so the herem “had nothing to do with ethnic or national affiliation and everything to do with personal righteousness” [164]. Calabro grants that his reading “does not remove the charge of genocide raised by modern critics” [143]. **Proposed:** the full note in Joshua is at 6:21 (“utterly destroyed all that was in the city”): what the text says, briefly; the Church's reading first, with Nephi's ([[1 Ne. 17:35]]); then Calabro's readings as his (the idiom of the time; Rahab and Gibeon as the ones who asked); then what is not known. 6:17 explains “accursed” at Jericho (devoted, the silver and gold to the treasury, 6:19), pointing to `[[Deut. 7:2]]` for the word. The other conquest chapters say what happened in a clause and point to 6:21. **No chapter says the command was not given, that Israel misheard God, or that the account is fiction; none defends or condemns beyond its sources; none makes the Canaanites' sins vivid beyond the text.** The text's own counterweights are named where they fall: Rahab (2; 6:25), Gibeon (9; 11:19), the Lord's warning that Israel is subject to the same judgment (7:11–12; 23:15–16).
- **Did it happen as told? Jericho, Ai, and archaeology (6; 8; 11:23; 13:1).** Calabro [105]: the excavations at Tell es-Sultan (Jericho) and et-Tell (Ai) “showed an absence of fortified occupation levels at these sites during the latter part of the Late Bronze Age”; some read the evidence as showing the account “either fabricated or greatly exaggerated” [106], others defend it (Hess, that Jericho and Ai were fortresses [107]; Wood, that Ai has been misidentified [108]); his conclusion, “evidence from archaeology is still insufficient to prove or disprove that the ḥērem was actually carried out as described … but there are various ways in which the evidence could fit with aspects of the biblical account” [109]. Smoot reports that “most scholars” now question the battle's historicity, “with a minority of scholars arguing against this negative consensus” (`ot-minute-josh-6` [5]). KnoWhy #852 (`sc-knowhy-852` [8]): “The conquest of Canaan did not happen overnight,” citing 13:1–6, 17:14–18, 19:47 and Judges 1; Brandt (`brandt-conquest` [8]): “the conquest extended over many years and was never fully completed on the terms the Lord had originally specified.” **Proposed:** one note on Jericho's archaeology (6:20) and one on Ai's (8:28), each saying what was found and the readings, as their authors', in the order Calabro gives, ending on his conclusion; no note claims that archaeology proves or disproves the book. **The book's own two pictures, “Joshua took the whole land” (11:23; 21:43–45) and “very much land” left (13:1; 15:63; 16:10; 17:12–13; 23:4–13), are set side by side at 13:1** with KnoWhy #852 and Brandt; Smoot's reading of 21:43–45 as “overstatement” that “might be an attempt at deliberate irony” (`ot-minute-josh-21` [6]) is his, said once at 21:43. No chapter dates the conquest beyond the volume guide's sentence.
- **Who wrote Joshua, and when.** The Bible Dictionary: the book is named for Joshua “not because he is the author, but because he is the principal figure in it,” and is “more properly a continuation of the first Five Books” (`bd-joshua-book` [1], [4]). Smoot: the “book of the law” of 1:8 “is most likely an early form of the book of Deuteronomy,” and Joshua through 2 Kings “are widely understood as being the Deuteronomistic History” (`ot-minute-josh-1` [6]); KnoWhy #861 [4] uses the same name. The text marks a later vantage itself: “unto this day” (4:9; 5:9; 7:26; 8:28–29; 9:27), and the book of Jasher named as a source (10:13). **Proposed:** chapter 1's `setting` says in two sentences what the Bible Dictionary says, and that the narrator writes from a later day (“unto this day”); the scholars' name for Joshua–Kings is said once, at 1:8, as scholars'. **No chapter builds a reading on a Deuteronomist, argues the date, or assigns verses to sources** (Deuteronomy's brief, “Who wrote Deuteronomy,” for the same rule).
- **Rahab: harlot, liar, believer (2; 6:22–25).** The word: Smoot, “The ordinary and straightforward reading of the Hebrew text is that she was a prostitute,” with the Targum's “innkeeper woman” as a later softening (`ot-minute-josh-2` [9]). *Scripture Helps*: she “became an unlikely hero for Israel, considering her reputation as a harlot” and is named in the New Testament “as an example of both faith and good works” [12]. **The lie:** no Church source read addresses it. Fisher (`fisher-rahab` [12]) calls her words to the king “carefully chosen and courageous deceits uttered in a tense and potentially life-threatening moment.” **Proposed:** the note at 2:4–5 says plainly that she deceived the king's men and the text records it without comment; that Hebrews and James commend her faith and her receiving the spies, not the words she spoke; and gives Fisher's reading as hers. **No note says the lie was righteous, or that it was sin she later repented of**: no source read says either. Her confession (2:9–11) is the center of the chapter, and Calabro reads her request as covenant language, *ḥesed* (`calabro-herem` [158]). The scarlet line as a second Passover is Fisher's reading [37] and *Scripture Helps*' “interesting similarity” [13]: the note at 2:18 gives Fisher's evidence, since *Scripture Helps* is beside the verse.
- **Achan's household (7:24–26).** *Scripture Helps* [25]: Achan's theft “amounted to stealing from the Lord”; “Even though Achan acted alone, the Lord attributed the sin to all of Israel”; and “The scriptures do not clearly explain why Achan’s family shared his punishment.” Its endnote 36 gives two suggestions from works not readable here: the children “were brought to view his punishment,” or the family “may have been aware of Achan’s crime and did not report it.” Smoot: “in an ancient mindset it makes some sense,” the Israelites sought to “‘quarantine’ the effects of the violation of the ḥerem” (`ot-minute-josh-7` [6]); Calabro: Achan and his family “become objects of the ḥērem themselves” [159], and Rahab, the outsider, takes the insider's place [160]; Seely: “the entire community would bear the consequences of the sin of an individual that went unresolved” (`seely-covenant-renewal` [25]). **Proposed:** the note at 7:24 gives the text, then *Scripture Helps*' statement that the scriptures do not clearly explain it, first and plainly; then the two suggestions as *Scripture Helps*' endnote gives them (cite *Scripture Helps*); then Smoot's and Calabro's readings as theirs. Deuteronomy 24:16 (“The fathers shall not be put to death for the children”) may be set beside it as a question the text leaves open, not resolved in the writer's words. **No note says the family was guilty** unless as the endnote's suggestion.
- **The sun and moon stand still (10:12–14).** The Church's footnotes point to Hab. 3:11, Hel. 12:14 and 3 Ne. 1:15, among others. Readings: the Student Manual (21-21): the Book of Mormon “makes it clear that it was the earth, not the sun, that was involved” and quotes Petersen that God “would, when necessary—cause that the earth should stop in its rotation to lengthen a day”; Grandy (`grandy-helaman` [54–56]) reads Hel. 12:15 differently, that Mormon invokes Joshua's day “not because Joshua’s account is scientifically inaccurate and therefore in need of correction” but for its lesson on obedience; Smoot: “The precise phenomenon described in this scene is difficult to fully ascertain,” and verse 12 is poetry, “a parallelistic declaration” (`ot-minute-josh-10` [5], [8]); **Proposed:** the note at 10:12 gives the text (that the words are a poem quoted from Jasher, 10:13), says the Church's sources affirm a miracle and Mormon's comment ([[Hel. 12:15]]), and gives the readings above as their authors', in that order, without choosing a mechanism; the center is the text's own: “the LORD hearkened unto the voice of a man: for the LORD fought for Israel” (10:14). Habakkuk 3:11's note already quotes Hel. 12:15: agree. The book of Jasher at 10:13: the Bible Dictionary (“An early collection of Jewish national songs and stories of deeds of valor”; the books printed under that name “do not appear to be the one spoken of in the Bible,” `bd-jasher`) and Brandt [32]; no note quotes the 1840 *Book of Jasher*.
- **The Gibeonites (9; 10:1–6; 2 Sam. 21).** What the text says: the princes “asked not counsel at the mouth of the LORD” (9:14), and kept the oath because “We have sworn unto them by the LORD God of Israel” (9:19). Smoot: the ruse played on the law of Deut. 20:10–20, and the plan “worked due to the Israelites’ credulity and their failure to consult the Lord” (`ot-minute-josh-9` [5–6]); Brandt: “despite their deceit, the treaty, which was considered sacred, was honored” (`brandt-conquest` [11]); Calabro [157]: Gibeon is one of the two that asked for mercy and obtained it; Belnap [20]: Saul later broke the oath “in his zeal,” and Israel suffered for it (2 Sam. 21:1–2). **Proposed:** notes at 9:14 and 9:19; the oath's weight is shown from the text (Joshua fights for Gibeon, 10:6–14) and 2 Sam. 21, not argued in the writer's words; the servitude (9:21–27) is said plainly.
- **“it was of the LORD to harden their hearts” (11:20).** Smoot: the line “serves as both a narrative nod to Moses and Pharaoh” and “adds a sort of extra layer of justification for the conquest” (`ot-minute-josh-11` [9]); Nyman gives the JST, which makes it the Canaanites who hardened their hearts, and says it “shows that it was the people who had hardened their hearts and not the Lord” (`nyman-jst-historical` [8]). **Proposed:** one note at 11:20: the King James, then the JST (read in the manuscript first; “Book-wide conventions”), then Nyman's reading; Exodus's notes on Pharaoh's heart are pointed to, not retold. With 11:19 (Gibeon alone made peace) it answers why the cities fought.
- **Joshua as a type of Christ.** The name: *Come, Follow Me* (`cfm-ot-2026-21` [7]): “The name Joshua (Yehoshua or Yeshua in Hebrew) means ‘Jehovah saves.’ And the name Jesus comes from Yeshua”; *Scripture Helps* [17]: Joshua leading Israel into the land “can remind us of Jesus Christ leading all the faithful into the ultimate land of promise, the celestial kingdom”; the Bible Dictionary's “Joshua” gives the name as “God is help” and lists “Jesus” among its forms, and calls him “the highest type of the devout warrior” (`bd-joshua`). Lund (`lund-types-symbols` [83–88]) draws a longer typology (the call to “sanctify,” 3:5; circumcision at Gilgal). **Proposed:** the name and its meaning are explained once, at `[[Num. 13:16]]` (already written: agree); chapter 1's `christ` section gives the type as the Church's sources give it, with Acts 7:45 and Hebrews 4:8 (which the site's notes on Num. 13 and Ps. 95:11 already explain: point); later `christ` sections may name it in a clause. Lund's further correspondences are his and are used only where a `christ` section needs one, labeled as his. **No chapter makes Joshua's battles a picture of Christ's work in the writer's own words**, and none reads the herem as a type. The captain of the host (5:13–15) is “possibly Jehovah Himself” (*Scripture Helps* [20]): say “possibly,” as it does; the divine warrior who fights for His people is Muhlestein's subject (`muhlestein-savior-sword` [26]) if a `christ` section needs a source beyond scripture.
- **The Book of Mormon and the land of promise.** Nephi's account of the Canaanites ([[1 Ne. 17:32–35]]) is the Church's key to the herem (the appendix [20]): quoted once, at 6:21; elsewhere point. Lehi's land given on the same conditions (look at 2 Ne. 1:5–7) may be named in a sentence where a chapter's subject is the land's conditions (23:12–16), as scripture's parallel. Benjamin's assembly beside Joshua 24 is Seely's comparison (`seely-covenant-renewal` [17]; Welch's chart 101 on BYU Studies' page: look at it first): once, at 24:25, if at all. The rest is under “Scripture reads Joshua.”
- **“Choose you this day” and “Ye cannot serve the LORD” (24:15, 19).** *Scripture Helps* [28, 31] is beside the verses (Maxwell; the warnings “do not apply to other contexts”). Seely [27] sets out the covenant's parts at Shechem (preamble, prologue, stipulations, witnesses, deposit): the chapter's `setting` uses it once, in a sentence, since Deut. 27:9 already quotes him. **Proposed:** 24:19 says what Joshua's words do in the ceremony (the people answer three times, 24:21–24) from the text and Smoot (`ot-minute-josh-24` [5]); no note softens “he will not forgive your transgressions” beyond *Scripture Helps*.
- **The altar Ed (22).** Smoot (`ot-minute-josh-22` [5–6]): the dispute “hinged on whether the law of Moses allowed for multiple sacred sites,” and the altar was “for memorial purposes”; “Ed” is “the Hebrew word for ‘witness’ or ‘testimony’.” Say what the text says (“called the altar Ed: for it shall be a witness between us that the LORD is God,” 22:34); Phinehas's earlier zeal at Num. 25 (on the site) is pointed to.

## Settled without the author

Decided by the standard, not asked of the author (Psalms' brief has the rule):

- **No Church, BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* source answers a reader's real question, and an older commentary does:** give its answer as “one older reading,” within the limit of a third of the notes. If the question isn't one a reader would ask, there is no note. The Latter-day Saint studies cluster on 1–10 and 24; the *Old Testament Minute* covers every chapter but briefly; the allotment chapters will need this most, and need few notes.
- **A scholar's reading that a Religious Studies Center, BYU or *Interpreter* publication prints**, or that the *Old Testament Minute* reports (Hess and Wood on Jericho and Ai, Dunham on the “gradations” of herem, Younger on Achan), is cited as that publication's author's, as one reading, beside the Church's, never above it.
- **Two faithful sources read a verse differently** (the long day: the manual and Grandy): give both, the Church's first, without choosing.
- **Joshua and Judges, or Joshua and its own summaries, tell the conquest differently:** say what each says, side by side; a reason only from a source read. Don't harmonize in the writer's own words, and don't call it a contradiction.
- **A source quoted inside a Church manual** whose own work can't be read (Petersen's *Joshua: Man of Faith*; the study Bibles *Scripture Helps* cites) is cited as the manual, for what the manual prints, as one view.
- **The Church's footnote points somewhere the note does not go:** leave it.
