Amos has one funeral song, and it is sung over a nation that is still standing. “Hear ye this word which I take up against you, even a lamentation, O house of Israel. The virgin of Israel is fallen; she shall no more rise: she is forsaken upon her land; there is none to raise her up” ([[Amos 5:1–2]]). The song has three verbs. She is fallen; she will not rise; no one will raise her. Keil renders the last two, “she does not rise up again… no one sets her up,” and the Cambridge commentary pictures her “unable to rise by her own efforts (having none to assist her).”[@keil-amos][@cambridge-amos] In Hebrew the second and third verbs are one root, *qûm*, in two forms: she herself will not “rise,” and there is no “raiser,” no one to make her rise.[@oshb-wlc] “Fallen” is *nāpal*.[@oshb-wlc]

My argument is that the book answers its own song, and that the answer is the Lord’s voice alone. The verb of the song comes back twice more where it matters. Amos uses it when he pleads for the people and asks how Jacob can stand; the reprieves he wins are withdrawn two visions later, and the question is left open. And the Lord uses it, in the form of the song’s verb that means to make rise, with the song’s “fallen” beside it, in the book’s last promise: “In that day will I raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen” ([[Amos 9:11]]). The song said there was none to raise her; the Lord says, “I will raise up.” No commentary I have read sets 5:2 and 9:11 together, or hears Amos’s question in the promise; the reading is mine. What is not mine is the use James made of 9:11 at the Jerusalem council, to which the Church’s footnote at the verse points, or the reading of the promise as Christ’s work, which is Ellicott’s.

## How can Jacob stand?

The Lord shows Amos locusts, and Amos speaks for the people before the judgment falls: “O Lord GOD, forgive, I beseech thee: by whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small” ([[Amos 7:2]]). The Lord relents. He shows fire, and Amos pleads again in the same words, with “cease” for “forgive,” and the Lord relents again ([[Amos 7:4–6]]). The site’s notes on [Amos 7:2](/amos/7/) and [Amos 7:3](/amos/7/) follow the pleading and the Joseph Smith Translation of the Lord’s reply; the chapter’s Christ section carries the reading of the prophet’s intercession as a pattern of the Savior’s, which Joshua Sears offers to Latter-day Saint readers.[@sears-intercession] This page is about the question, not the pleading.

The King James has Amos ask “by whom shall Jacob arise?” The Hebrew asks whether Jacob can stand. The verb is the song’s *qûm* in its simple form, and the question is *mî yāqûm Yaʿăqōb*, which Keil renders “how… can Jacob (the nation of Israel) stand (not arise), since it is small?” and the Cambridge commentary “how (lit. as who) shall Jacob stand?”; Ellicott says flatly of the King James, “‘by whom,’ is incorrect.”[@keil-amos][@cambridge-amos][@ellicott-amos] The lexicon groups the verse with passages where the verb means to “maintain oneself,” and puts the song’s “rise” under a different sense, rising “after falling.”[@bdb] Amos is not asking who will lift Jacob. He is asking whether Jacob can stay on his feet under this judgment. The song had already answered that: “she shall no more rise.” The prophet’s question in the vision and the poet’s sentence in the song are the same verb in two senses, and the join between them, by root and by sense, is mine: together they say that Israel cannot stand by itself.

The English “by whom” agrees with the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Old Testament, and that bears on the argument. The Greek translators asked who would raise Jacob: *tis anastēsei ton Iakōb*, “who will raise up Jacob?”[@swete-lxx-amos] They used the same Greek verb for the song’s “none to raise her up” and for the Lord’s “I will raise up” in 9:11.[@swete-lxx-amos] So the Septuagint and the King James read the question the same way, as asking for a raiser, and in the Septuagint the book’s three passages share one Greek verb as well as one Hebrew root. In Hebrew the question is whether Jacob can stand; in Greek it is who will raise him. Either way no man is named. The Lord does answer Amos at once, “It shall not be” ([[Amos 7:3]]; [[Amos 7:6]]), but the answer does not hold. Two visions later Amos is shown a plumbline and a basket of summer fruit, names them, and does not plead; the Lord says, “I will not again pass by them any more” ([[Amos 7:8]]; [[Amos 8:2]]). Sears reads the arrangement as suggesting that the Lord is “denying any new attempts to intercede,” and the site’s note on [Amos 7:8](/amos/7/) gives the reading and its limits.[@sears-intercession] In my reading the reprieves are withdrawn and the question stands open after the fourth vision, until the book’s last promise.

## I will raise up

The book ends with the song’s verbs of falling and rising, but now the Lord promises to raise what has fallen. “In that day will I raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen, and close up the breaches thereof; and I will raise up his ruins, and I will build it as in the days of old” ([[Amos 9:11]]). “Raise up” is the causative of *qûm*, the song’s “none to raise her up,” and “that is fallen” is a form of the same verb as the song’s “is fallen.”[@oshb-wlc] The verb stands in ten places in the book; three are the Lord raising up prophets ([[Amos 2:11]]), raising up a nation against Israel ([[Amos 6:14]]), and rising against the house of Jeroboam ([[Amos 7:9]]).[@oshb-wlc] One more is the song’s verdict said again in the Lord’s own speech: those who swear by Samaria’s idol “shall fall, and never rise up again” ([[Amos 8:14]]), both verbs of 5:2 together, and the Cambridge commentary sends the reader of that verse back to the song; Keil takes the swearers, the young men and virgins, to stand for “the nation as a whole.”[@cambridge-amos][@keil-amos] So the book says twice that fallen Israel will not rise, and once that the Lord will raise up what has fallen. The pairing of the song with 9:11 is mine.

What is raised is not the virgin but a hut. The “tabernacle” is a *sukkah*, a booth of branches, and the site’s note on [Amos 9:11](/amos/9/) explains the picture: David’s house reduced to a fallen shelter.[@oshb-wlc] Keil reads the promise against the Lord’s promise to David through Nathan that his house would stand for ever ([[2 Sam. 7:16]]), and says the kingdom “would be raised up again from its fallen condition,” and “could only be raised up again through an offshoot from David’s family”; he adds that the earlier Jews “formed a name for the Messiah out of this passage,” *bar naphlin*, “He who had sprung from a fallen hut.”[@keil-amos] The Bible Dictionary says of the verses that “in the last days the kingdom will return to and even surpass its ancient glory,” and the Guide to the Scriptures that David’s reign became “the type of the more glorious age when the Messiah would come.”[@bd-amos][@gs-david] Three verses before the promise the Lord has already said what He will not do: “I will destroy it from off the face of the earth; saving that I will not utterly destroy the house of Jacob” ([[Amos 9:8]]). Keil: the house of Jacob “is not to be utterly destroyed, but simply to be shaken, as it were, in a sieve.”[@keil-amos] Keil joins the two verses: the “breaches” of 9:11 are plural because “the house of Israel, which was not to be utterly destroyed (Amos 9:8), consisted of the remnant of the people of the two kingdoms,” to be united again under one king.[@keil-amos] The house that could not stand is kept, and what is kept is raised.

Two things stand against the reading, and should be said. The first is that 7:2 in Hebrew asks about standing, not raising, so the tightest match is the one between the song and the promise, where both verbs recur, the raising in the same causative stem; the question joins them through the Greek and through the sense that Israel cannot stand alone. The second is the authorship of the ending. Sears reports that scholars usually assign the last five verses to a later editor, and Joseph Spencer notes one scholar’s suggestion that the book once ended at 9:4; neither rests on the view, and the Bible Dictionary reads the verses as Amos’s promise that “in the last days the kingdom will return.”[@sears-intercession][@spencer-remnant][@bd-amos] Whoever wrote them, the book as scripture ends with the Lord taking up the song’s verbs.

## The Son of David

The New Testament quotes the promise once, and the Church’s footnote at 9:11 leads to it.[@lds-scriptures] At the Jerusalem council, after Peter has told how the Gentiles received the Holy Ghost, James says that “to this agree the words of the prophets; as it is written, After this I will return, and will build again the tabernacle of David, which is fallen down; and I will build again the ruins thereof, and I will set it up: That the residue of men might seek after the Lord, and all the Gentiles, upon whom my name is called” ([[Acts 15:15–17]]). Frank Judd summarizes the speech: James “declared that they fulfilled Amos’s prophecy that non-Israelites would seek after the truth of the Lord.”[@judd-jerusalem-conference] The New Testament Student Manual says the same: James “first cited scriptural support for Peter’s words, referring to the prophecy in Amos 9:11–12 that Gentiles would seek after the Lord.”[@nt-student-manual-32] James quotes the Greek Bible, as Daniel McClellan shows, where “the remnant of Edom” has become “the residue of men.”[@mcclellan-ot-in-nt] James’s verbs for the raising also differ from the Septuagint’s: Acts has “build again” and “set up” where Amos’s Greek has “raise up,” and Keil suggests the apostle may be quoting from memory and altering the words “to give greater clearness to the allusion contained in the prophecy to the Messianic times.”[@biblehub-interlinear][@keil-amos] What James is recorded as drawing from the verses is that the Gentiles would seek the Lord. Ellicott goes a step further, and says so: the quotation “suggests, perhaps implies, a fuller interpretation than is given in the summary of St. James’s speech. It assumes that the ‘tabernacle of David,’ which to human eyes had been lying as in ruins, was being rebuilt by Christ, the Son of David, that He was doing the work which, in the prophecy, Jehovah claimed as His.”[@ellicott-acts] The Cambridge commentary on Acts says the booth “may be applied” to a people “brought very low, but yet should find in him a Saviour.”[@cambridge-acts]

The Lord who says “I will raise up” is Jehovah, the premortal Jesus Christ, and Gabriel told Mary that her son would be given “the throne of his father David” ([[Luke 1:32]]).[@gs-jehovah] Read with Ellicott, and with Keil’s “offshoot from David’s family,” the One who promised to raise the fallen hut is the One who came of it; that step is theirs. The step from the song to the promise is mine.

## He is small

Amos’s reason was a plain one: “for he is small.” Sears observes that the prophet does not plead the people’s righteousness or repentance, and the rest of the book gives him little to plead.[@sears-intercession] The book does ask Israel for something: “Seek ye me, and ye shall live” ([[Amos 5:4]]), and Spencer notes that Amos ties his one hope for the remnant to the commands to seek and to love the good, and still says only “It may be” ([[Amos 5:15]]); he accepts Paul Noble’s point that the doubt is not whether a remnant will exist but “whether or not it will enjoy God’s favor,” and adds that survival without that favor “would be mere survival—not life.”[@spencer-remnant] The favor comes at the end, in the first person, to a people the book has shown unable to stand. My reading of that shape is that what cannot stand is raised rather than told to stand harder, and the Lord has said as much of His own: “my grace is sufficient for all men that humble themselves before me… then will I make weak things become strong unto them” ([[Ether 12:27]]). Amos pleaded Jacob’s smallness, and in this reading the lasting answer is the Lord’s promise to raise what has fallen.
