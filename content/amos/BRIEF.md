## What the author has decided

Nothing for Amos in particular beyond choosing it. The author's standing rules are in `STANDARDS.md` §1 and `AUTHORING.md` and are not repeated here. How two of them land on this book:

## What a chapter is for here

Amos is 146 verses in nine chapters. A reader meets a herdsman from Judah preaching in the rich northern kingdom a generation before it fell, and asks plain questions: *Who was Amos, and why was a southerner sent north? What had the nations done? What exactly was Israel doing wrong, when its shrines were full? Does the Lord reject sacrifice? What is the “secret” He reveals? Did the Lord change His mind? Is the famine of the word the Apostasy? Does the book end in hope, and for whom?* A chapter answers those from the text first.

**Say what Amos said to Samaria before saying what it foretells.** The Church's sources read 8:11–12 and 9:11–15 of later days, and so do the chapters, but each first says what the words meant to people selling grain at Bethel about 760 B.C. Give the picture, then the reading, and keep them apart.

**The sins are concrete; name them.** Amos lists them: selling a debtor “for a pair of shoes” (2:6; 8:6), keeping a pledged cloak overnight (2:8), fines spent on wine at the shrine (2:8), bribes “in the gate” (5:12), the small ephah and the heavy shekel (8:5), ivory beds (6:4). Each has a law behind it (Exodus 22:26–27; Leviticus 19:35–36; Deuteronomy 24:12–13, 25:13–15). Say what the practice was and which law it broke; that is the commentary. Exodus on the site has notes on several of these laws: link with a `[[reference]]`.

**Nine chapters repeat a few things.** Each is explained once, at the place this brief names, and the other chapters point to it.

## Divisions

*Scripture Helps* divides the book in three, and `book.yaml` follows it: chapters 1–2, judgments on the nations, Judah and Israel (`amosnations`); 3–6, warnings to the northern kingdom and a plea to repent (`amoswords`); 7–9, the visions and a last word of hope (`amosvisions`). The Student Manual counts five visions (7:1–3; 7:4–6; 7:7–9; 8:1–3; 9:1–4); Sears treats the first four as two pairs and the fifth apart. The story of Amaziah (7:10–17) sits between the third and fourth.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, how a modern translation prints a verse, which later scripture takes up which line) is the coordinator's direction of where to look, some of it from memory. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **When.** The book dates itself: “in the days of Uzziah king of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash king of Israel, two years before the earthquake” (1:1). The Bible Dictionary gives Uzziah's death as “about 740 B.C.” and Jeroboam II's as “about 750 B.C.”; the Guide to the Scriptures has Amos prophesy “from approximately 792 to 740 B.C.”; Matson puts the ministry at “760–750 BC”; *Scripture Helps* says “the eighth century BC” and that Israel was “conquered by the Assyrians in 722 BC.” **Proposed: every chapter's `when` is “About 760–750 B.C.; the northern kingdom of Israel under Jeroboam II”** or the like, with a ledger row from the Bible Dictionary or Matson. Chapter 1's setting gives the kings and the earthquake; no chapter argues a narrower date.
- **Who.** A herdsman “of Tekoa” (1:1), “an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit” (7:14), from Judah, sent to Israel. The Bible Dictionary places Tekoa “12 miles south of Jerusalem” (there is no entry “Tekoa”; this is from the entry “Amos”). What the two Hebrew words for his work mean, and whether he was a poor laborer or an owner of flocks, comes from the Hebrew and a lexicon page; a lead from memory is that the word in 1:1 is used elsewhere only of a king of Moab (2 Kings 3:4). The full note is at 7:14–15, where he says it himself; 1:1 points there. The Student Manual's gloss of the name (“bearer” or “burden”) is the manual's; the Bible Dictionary's entry opens “Burden.”
- **The earthquake** (1:1; 8:8; 9:1, 5). `[[Zech. 14:5]]` remembers it. A lead from memory: excavations in Israel show earthquake damage from about this time. Use it only from a source read; otherwise the verse and Zechariah are enough.
- **The time is prosperous.** That is the whole point of the book's tone, and it is easy to miss. `[[2 Kgs. 14:23–29]]` is Jeroboam II's reign; Matson: “the Israelites were enjoying an era of peace and prosperity.” Explained once, in chapter 1's setting; chapters 3, 4, 6 and 8 point back in a clause.
- **Bethel, Gilgal, Beersheba, Dan, Samaria.** The shrines Amos names (3:14; 4:4; 5:5; 7:13; 8:14). Explained at 4:4 (Bethel and Gilgal) from the Bible Dictionary (`bd-bethel`, `bd-gilgal`, `bd-beersheba`, `bd-high-places`) and `[[1 Kgs. 12:28–33]]`; Hosea on the site has notes on Bethel as “Beth-aven” and on Gilgal: link. The volume guide [The Land](/old-testament/guides/the-land/) and [Israel's Story](/old-testament/guides/israels-story/) carry the map and the kings; point to them and don't retell them.
- **Names of God.** “The Lord GOD” (Adonai with the divine name) is Amos's usual form and “the LORD, the God of hosts” his second; copy every phrase with the name from `data/kjv/amos.json`, which has “Lord” (not “LORD”) at 5:16; 7:7, 8; 9:1. The Church's footnote at 5:8 reads “HEB Jehovah.” The Bible Dictionary's entry speaks of “the moral character of Jehovah, the righteous ruler of all nations and men”; say that the LORD of the Old Testament is the premortal Christ once, at 4:13 or 5:8 (the three hymns to the Creator: 4:13; 5:8–9; 9:5–6), from a Church source read.
- **Verse numbers.** The Hebrew chapters and verses agree with the King James in Amos, as far as the coordinator knows; `scripts/hebrew.mjs` prints the Hebrew reference beside any that differs.
- **Other books on the site.** Link, and don't say who borrowed from whom except as a source read says. 1:2 and `[[Joel 3:16]]` (the same line: the note there is written); 4:11 and `[[Gen. 19:24–25]]`; 4:13 and 5:8 beside `[[Job 9:9]]` (not on the site) and Genesis 1; 5:18–20 and `[[Joel 1:15]]`, `[[Isa. 13:6]]` (the day of the Lord, explained on the site at both; Amos is the earliest use, and Israel wants the day: say that here and link for the rest); 5:21–24 and `[[Isa. 1:11–17]]`, `[[Hosea 6:6]]`, `[[Jer. 7:21–23]]`, `[[Ps. 51:16–17]]`, and Hosea's theme page on mercy and sacrifice; 7:1–6 and `[[Gen. 18:23–33]]`, `[[Ex. 32:11–14]]`; 7:3 and the site's notes on the Lord “repenting” (`[[Gen. 6:6]]`, `[[Joel 2:13]]`, `[[Hosea 11:8]]`); 8:9 and `[[Joel 2:31]]`; 9:2 and `[[Ps. 139:8]]`; 9:13 and `[[Joel 3:18]]`. Hosea is Amos's contemporary in the same kingdom; Van Dyke and Ogden set them side by side (Amos “from the prosecutor's perspective,” Hosea from that of the wronged husband), which is theirs.
- **The New Testament quotes Amos twice,** both in Acts and both from the Greek. Stephen quotes 5:25–27 (`[[Acts 7:42–43]]`: “Remphan” for “Chiun,” “Babylon” for “Damascus”); James quotes 9:11–12 at the Jerusalem council (`[[Acts 15:16–17]]`: “the residue of men” for “the remnant of Edom”). Each note reads the verse beside Acts and Brenton's page (`lxx-rahlfs`), says what differs, and gives a reason only from a source read.
- **Modern revelation.** No comparison panel, and nothing moves to a Book of Mormon chapter. The Church's footnotes give the Doctrine and Covenants references; a cross-reference that only repeats the footnote is not a note.
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** Four changes, all in the footnotes: 3:6 (“done” becomes “known”), 3:7 (“but” becomes “until”), and 7:3 and 7:6 (“Jacob shall repent,” for “The LORD repented”). Each answers a question a reader asks, so each belongs in its note, with a ledger row from the footnote (`lds-scriptures`).
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports what runs across the book; the coordinator adds it to `THEME-CANDIDATES.md`. No theme page is written until the author chooses it.

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter; don't re-explain what another chapter explains, point with a `[[reference]]`; a Witness of Christ does not open by saying what the chapter lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer, since the site shows it alone as the note's card; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; “In plain words” adds nothing to the verse; Hebrew only where it changes what the reader understands; don't join two verses yourself and call it the text's meaning; no personal application.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that nine chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-13.

- **“For three transgressions … and for four” (1:3–2:6).** Eight times. The Student Manual (8-5) reads an idiom for sins “exceedingly abundant,” with Proverbs 6:16. One note at 1:3; the rest point back. Matson gives the word: “transgression” is *pešaʿ*, “an offence concerning persons and property,” and Amos uses it again of Israel at 3:14. Confirm it with `hebrew.mjs` and a lexicon page.
- **What had the nations done, and by what law are they judged (chapters 1–2)?** None had the law of Moses. Matson's answer, which frames these two chapters: the crimes are “martial brutality or human slavery,” and the nations were Israel's kin by treaty or by blood, so that Tyre is charged with forgetting “the brotherly covenant” (1:9) and Edom with pursuing “his brother” (1:11). Give that as his reading, with the treaties he cites (`[[1 Kgs. 5:12]]`). The Student Manual's table (8-6) names an occasion for each crime (Hazael, `[[2 Kgs. 10:32–33]]`; a Philistine raid under Joram); those are its identifications, and which raid or war is meant is mostly not known: say so. **The crimes against pregnant women (1:13) and the burned bones (2:1) are described, not dwelt on.**
- **The trap closes on Israel (2:4–16).** The order of the eight oracles is the argument: the hearers agree seven times and then hear their own name. Matson calls chapters 1–2 “a subtle and persuasive introduction.” This is chapter 1's or 2's thread and may be a Literary Structure note at 2:6, where the structure is the message. Judah is judged for despising “the law of the LORD” (2:4), Israel for what it does to the poor: the difference is worth a sentence.
- **“A man and his father will go in unto the same maid” (2:7).** Describe, don't quote at length. Whether the girl is a household servant or belongs to a shrine is not known; give what the verse says and the law it breaks (Leviticus 18), from a source read. The women guide's section on what the law protected may be linked if it fits.
- **“You only have I known of all the families of the earth: therefore I will punish you” (3:2).** The Bible Dictionary: “The fact that Israel is the covenant people would make their punishment the heavier (3:2; 9:7).” Spencer calls Amos's use of election “a radical reinterpretation.” “Known” is covenant language: Hosea on the site has a note on “know” from Benson and Ricks; link, don't repeat. D&C 82:3 is the footnote's.
- **“He revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets” (3:7).** The best-known verse in the book among Latter-day Saints, usually quoted alone. The note does three things the Gospel Library doesn't. (1) Its place in the argument: the seven questions of 3:3–6 each pair an effect with its cause, and 3:8 says the prophet can no more keep silent than a man can help fearing a lion; *Scripture Helps* reads the passage as an answer to Israel's “Prophesy not” (2:12). (2) “Secret” is *sôd*: *Scripture Helps* says it “can also be rendered ‘council,’ ‘counsel,’ or ‘plan,’” and in an endnote that “many scholars suggest” the verse refers to prophets taking part in a heavenly council. William Hamblin (*Interpreter*): “God reveals the sôd (secret plan) of his sôd (divine council) to his prophets.” Stephen Smoot (`smoot-council-hebrew-bible`): the *sôd* “is not just confidential instruction delivered by God but also the manifestation of God's heavenly court.” `[[Jer. 23:18]]`, 22 on the site has the same word; link. Give the council reading as scholars' and as what the Church's newest help allows. (3) The Joseph Smith Translation's “until,” and Joseph Smith's own statement of the principle (Joseph Smith Papers, History, vol. E-1, 1727; quoted once, short). **The verse is about warning before judgment, not a promise that prophets know everything**: the text supports the first; don't argue against the second.
- **“Shall there be evil in a city, and the LORD hath not done it?” (3:6).** A reader stumbles. “Evil” is calamity (Hebrew and a lexicon page; Isaiah 45:7 on the site has the same question: link), and the Joseph Smith Translation reads “known.” Both, briefly.
- **“Ye kine of Bashan” (4:1–3).** The wealthy women of Samaria. The Student Manual (8-11) opens, “The quality of life in any community is largely what its women make it”; **don't use that framing** (the women guide exists because the author's wife reads the Old Testament's women as objects). Amos charges men and women with the same thing, crushing the needy for their own comfort; here it is the women's turn, and 6:1–7 is the men's. Bashan from `bd-bashan`; the hooks of 4:2 and “the palace” of 4:3 are obscure: give the Hebrew and one or two translations, as renderings.
- **“Come to Beth-el, and transgress” (4:4–5).** Irony: a priest's call to worship, turned. Name each offering as the verse does (sacrifices every morning, tithes, a thank offering with leaven, freewill offerings), with the law for each from Leviticus and the volume guide's section on sacrifice. The Church's footnote reads “years” as “HEB days.”
- **“Yet have ye not returned unto me” (4:6–13).** Five times, after famine, drought, blight, pestilence, and overthrow, then “prepare to meet thy God.” *Scripture Helps* reads these famines as sent “to help turn the people back to Him” and sets them beside the famine of the word in 8:11: use that link in chapter 8. Deuteronomy 28 and Leviticus 26 are the covenant curses behind the list: say so from a source read. “Cleanness of teeth” is hunger.
- **Does the Lord reject sacrifice? (5:21–27).** “I hate, I despise your feast days.” The Bible Dictionary: “the offering the Lord most cares for is a righteous life—the sacrifices of animals lose their meaning if offered as substitutes for personal righteousness (5:21–27).” Matson answers the scholars who set worship against ethics here: the book “reconciles both.” **The site's reading: the Lord rejects these offerings from these hands, not offerings.** 5:25 (“Have ye offered unto me sacrifices and offerings in the wilderness forty years?”) is hard: say what the question expects and how readers take it, from sources read, and don't settle it. 5:24 (“let judgment run down as waters”) is the best-known line; “judgment” and “righteousness” (*mishpat*, *tsedaqah*) are worth the Hebrew once, here, since the English “judgment” misleads. No modern reception history (who quoted it in which speech).
- **Moloch and Chiun (5:26).** The Hebrew is disputed and the Greek differs. The Student Manual (8-14) says they “were heathen gods that the Israelite women had adopted” and that people “carried miniature replicas”; neither claim has a source on its page, so **neither is used**. `bd-molech` exists; there is no entry “Chiun.” Give the Hebrew words from `hebrew.mjs` and a lexicon page, Brenton's Greek, and Stephen's quotation, as a verse whose names are uncertain.
- **“Woe unto you that desire the day of the LORD” (5:18–20).** *Scripture Helps*: Israel “assumed that the day of the Lord would bring them victory over their enemies”; for them it “is darkness, and not light.” The lion, the bear and the serpent of 5:19 need no gloss beyond the verse.
- **“At ease in Zion” (6:1–7).** Addressed to both capitals. `[[2 Ne. 28:21–25]]` takes up the phrase (the footnote and the Student Manual, 8-15): a good Related Scriptures note, saying what Nephi does with it. Calneh, Hamath and Gath (6:2) from a source read; which conquests are meant is uncertain. “The affliction of Joseph” (6:6) is Matson's third “Joseph” text.
- **The uncle and the bones (6:9–10); “with silence” (8:3).** Both obscure. Sears reads the Hebrew *has* in 8:3 as a command, “Be silent!”, and argues that God says it to Amos to end his interceding; he says his reading “runs against” the common one, which ties 8:3 to 6:10. Give the King James, what the word is, and Sears's reading as his.
- **Did the Lord repent? (7:1–6).** *Scripture Helps* has a section. The verb is *nḥm*: “to regret,” “to feel sorrow or sympathy,” “to comfort,” or “to relent or forebear” (Sears, and *Scripture Helps* after him). The Joseph Smith Translation: “Jacob shall repent for this, therefore I will not utterly destroy him.” Sears on what Amos argues: “By whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small” appeals to no righteousness in the people. The site's earlier notes on the same verb are linked, and this note does what they don't: the prophet as intercessor, with Abraham and Moses.
- **Why the pleading stops (7:7–9; 8:1–3).** Sears's reading frames chapters 7 and 8: two visions where Amos pleads and the Lord relents, then two where the Lord asks “what seest thou?” and Amos says a word. “I will not again pass by them any more”: “pass by” (*ʿbr*) is to forgive. Sears counts the Hebrew words spoken (Amos 10, 10, 1, 2; God 2, 4, 26, 28). And his last section, on what intercession shows of God, quotes `[[Ex. 34:6–7]]`: “not really a fight to change God's mind.” All as his. **The plumbline**: *Scripture Helps* and Sears both read a builder's line against a wall; a lead from memory is that the Hebrew word (*ʾănāk*) occurs only here and some read “tin.” Mention that only if the lexicon page shows it, and briefly. The summer fruit and “the end” (*qayits*, *qets*) is a wordplay the English loses: this is where Hebrew pays.
- **Amos and Amaziah (7:10–17).** The one story in the book. “I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son” (7:14): the Hebrew has no verb, so “was” or “am” is the translator's; “prophet's son” is a member of a prophets' guild (a source read; `[[2 Kgs. 2:3]]`). Amaziah calls him “seer” (7:12; KnoWhy #457 on the word) and tells him to earn his bread in Judah; Bethel is “the king's chapel.” The sentence on Amaziah's wife and children (7:17) is described plainly.
- **The famine of hearing the words of the Lord (8:11–12).** The Church's reading is settled and is given as such. *Scripture Helps*: “One fulfillment of this prophecy occurred after the ministry of the prophet Malachi, when more than 400 years passed without divinely called prophets,” and it was “fulfilled again” after the deaths of the Savior and His Apostles; the Student Manual (8-17) calls it “prophetic dualism” and quotes President Kimball. **First what it meant to Israel**: a people who told the prophets “Prophesy not” (2:12; 7:13, 16) are given their wish. Then the Church's reading, once, with one short quotation. The Interpreter Foundation's study aid notes that in context the famine applies to ancient Israel; that page is not readable by the script, so the point is made from the text itself.
- **The small ephah and the sabbath (8:4–6).** Merchants impatient for the new moon and sabbath to end. Matson; Leviticus 19:35–36. “The refuse of the wheat” is sweepings sold as grain.
- **The sun at noon (8:9–10).** The Student Manual (8-16) reads a nation destroyed in its prosperity and also the darkened sun before the Second Coming. The darkness at the Crucifixion (`[[Matt. 27:45]]`) is a connection many make with “mourning of an only son”: **use it only if a Church or Latter-day Saint source read makes it**, and then as that source's; it may be the chapter's Witness of Christ if one does.
- **The fifth vision (9:1–6).** “I saw the Lord standing upon the altar.” Which altar (Bethel's or Jerusalem's) is not said. Skinner (RSC, on seeing God in the temple) cites the verse. The Student Manual reads the Second Coming here; that is its reading. 9:2–4 beside `[[Ps. 139:7–10]]`, turned from comfort to threat.
- **“Are ye not as children of the Ethiopians unto me?” (9:7).** The Lord brought the Philistines from Caphtor and the Syrians from Kir as He brought Israel from Egypt. It says the Lord governs every nation's history, and that the Exodus alone will not save Israel. **Nothing about the Ethiopians' color or standing is said or implied; the verse compares peoples as peoples**, and `[[2 Ne. 26:33]]` may stand beside it if a source read joins them.
- **The sieve and the remnant (9:8–10; 5:15; 3:12).** Spencer: Amos takes a hope his hearers already hold and turns it. 3:12, the shepherd who rescues “two legs, or a piece of an ear,” is rescue as proof of loss (Matson sets it beside the hired shepherd's duty to show what is left of an animal; a lead from memory is Exodus 22:13: read his paragraph and the verse); 5:15 is “It may be”; 9:8 is “not utterly.” Spencer's reading is his; the Bible Dictionary says only that “Israel will not be utterly destroyed (9:8).”
- **“The tabernacle of David that is fallen” (9:11–12).** A booth (*sukkah*), not the Mosaic tabernacle: the Hebrew and a lexicon page. The Bible Dictionary: “in the last days the kingdom will return to and even surpass its ancient glory (9:11–15).” James applies it to the Gentiles entering the Church. The Student Manual (8-18) reads “the raising of the temple in Jerusalem”; that is its reading, and no other source read says it. **This is chapter 9's Witness of Christ**: the house of David raised is the Son of David's kingdom, said from a source read (the footnotes; the Topical Guide; `[[Luke 1:32–33]]`).
- **“The plowman shall overtake the reaper” (9:13–15).** The chapter heading: “In the last days, the people of Israel will be gathered again into their own land, and it will become productive.” Van Dyke and Ogden read the gathering. The curse of 5:11 (houses built and not lived in, vineyards planted and not drunk) is reversed line for line in 9:14: the text's own, and worth showing.
- **Whether Amos wrote the ending.** Many scholars assign 9:11–15 (and other passages) to a later hand; Sears says only that “theories about its composition and editing vary considerably.” **Proposed: one sentence in chapter 9's setting, as scholars' view, and that the Church's sources read the book as Amos's; no chapter argues it.**
