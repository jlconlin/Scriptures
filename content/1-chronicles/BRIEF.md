# 1 Chronicles brief

Book-level decisions for 1 Chronicles. The site-wide standard is in `STANDARDS.md`; this file records what is particular to 1 Chronicles. Writers, checkers, and reviewers read both before starting a chapter. **1 and 2 Chronicles are one book in Hebrew** (`bd-chronicles` [1]; `scripture-helps-2-chronicles` [4]), and this brief holds what both share: the book itself, how it is written beside Samuel and Kings, its text, the Joseph Smith Translation, the conventions, the contested questions they share and the sources. `content/2-chronicles/BRIEF.md` holds 2 Chronicles' own questions and points here for the rest. 1 Kings' brief is the nearest model (the same kind of book, the same approach to its questions), and 2 Kings' brief is the precedent for a passage already told elsewhere (Isaiah 36–39). **Almost all of Chronicles' narrative is already told on the site**: 1 and 2 Samuel, 1 and 2 Kings, Isaiah 36–39 and Jeremiah 52 are written with full notes, Psalms 96, 105 and 106 hold David's psalm of 1 Chr. 16, Ezra 1 repeats the decree that ends 2 Chronicles, and Nehemiah 11 repeats the list of 1 Chr. 9. Read “How Chronicles is written beside Samuel and Kings” and “Explained once: where” before writing.

**Status (2026-11-13):** drafted from the sources named below, read in the session with the research scripts on 2026-11-13. The author chose these books (“Let's do first and second Chronicles”) and has not read this brief. Where a section says “Proposed,” that is the approach the chapters follow; it is the drafter's decision, not a fact from a source, and the author can overturn it.

Related files: `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` at the top of the repository; `THEME-CANDIDATES.md` and `UNANSWERED.md` in this folder, once there is something to put in them.

## What the author has decided

Nothing for Chronicles beyond choosing it. The standing rules are in `STANDARDS.md` §1 and `AUTHORING.md`. How three land on these books:

- **Newest trumps oldest.** *Scripture Helps: Old Testament* (2025) has one chapter on Chronicles, `scripture-helps-2-chronicles` (chapter 28, “2 Chronicles 14–20; 26; 30”), and nothing on 1 Chronicles or on 2 Chronicles 1–13, 21–25, 27–29 or 31–36; its section “What are the books of 1 and 2 Chronicles?” ([4]–[10]) is the Church's newest word on the whole book. Then the seminary manual (2014: `ot-seminary-chronicles-intro` and `ot-seminary-lesson-102`, a review lesson), then the *Old Testament Student Manual*, which has no chapter on Chronicles and folds it into its Kings chapters (`ot-manual-4`, `ot-manual-12`, `ot-manual-19`; Enrichment A, `ot-manual-kings-a`, tables the kings of Judah with their Chronicles references). Where they differ, *Scripture Helps* leads (on the date: *Scripture Helps* “most scholars suggest … possibly in the fourth century BC” [5]; the seminary manual “We do not know when or where” [5]).
- **Don't replicate the Gospel Library.** Beside the verse the reader already has the chapter headings, the footnotes (the parallel in Samuel or Kings on most narrative chapters, and five Joseph Smith Translation readings), and *Scripture Helps* on 2 Chr. 14–20, 26 and 30. - **Quote the Church's modern material sparingly.** *Scripture Helps* quotes Elder Ulisses Soares on Uzziah's pride (28 [23]–[24]); *Come, Follow Me* (`cfm-ot-2026-30`) quotes President Nelson on following the prophet beside 2 Chr. 20:20, and *Scripture Helps* points to President Hinckley's “Believe His Prophets” (`hinckley-believe-his-prophets-1992`), a talk on that verse. ## How Chronicles is written beside Samuel and Kings

This is the decision every chapter turns on. **Proposed** (the precedent is 2 Kings' brief for Isaiah 36–39, adapted to a whole book):

- **Every chapter is written in full** (`setting`, `thread`, sections with plain words covering every verse, notes, `christ`, `explore`): the reader reads Chronicles on its own page and needs its own frame. What is cut is repetition in the notes, not the chapter.
- **A note goes first to what is Chronicles' own:** (1) what the Chronicler adds to a story told in Samuel or Kings (Uzza's death explained as the Levites' charge, 1 Chr. 15:2, 13; Abijah's speech, 2 Chr. 13; Hanani's rebuke of Asa, 2 Chr. 16:7–10; Elijah's letter, 2 Chr. 21:12–15; Zechariah's stoning, 2 Chr. 24:20–22; Manasseh's repentance, 2 Chr. 33:11–17; Necho's warning, 2 Chr. 35:21–22); (2) what he leaves out and what he changes (David's years as Saul's fugitive, Bath-sheba and Absalom; Solomon's wives and his turning; the northern kingdom except where it touches Judah; “Satan” for the LORD at 1 Chr. 21:1; numbers that differ), and why, **where a source says why**; (3) the material only in Chronicles: the genealogies (1 Chr. 1–9), the men who came to David at Ziklag (12), the Levites and singers who carry and praise the ark (15–16), David's preparations and his orders for the priests, Levites, singers, porters and officers (22–29), and in 2 Chronicles the reigns of Asa and Jehoshaphat in full (14–20), Hezekiah's cleansing of the temple and his Passover (29–31), Manasseh's repentance (33), Josiah's Passover (35) and the decree of Cyrus (36:22–23).
- **A passage told in parallel points to the Samuel or Kings note with a `[[reference]]`** and does not explain again what that note explains (the census's sin, Uzzah's error, the covenant with David, Hiram, the temple's plan, the lying spirit, Shishak, the Rab-shakeh). Read the two passages side by side first (the table below; `node scripts/occurrences.mjs 1-chronicles "<phrase>" --site all`). **Where a Samuel or Kings note already told Chronicles' own material** (2 Kgs. 21:17 on Manasseh's repentance; 2 Kgs. 12:2, 20 on Zechariah; 1 Kgs. 15:19 on Hanani; 2 Kgs. 15:5 on Uzziah; 1 Kgs. 22:22 on the JST of 2 Chr. 18), the Chronicles note is now its full place, agrees with the earlier note and links to it; the earlier note is not rewritten.
- **The differences are the Chronicler's emphasis, not errors to correct.** Say what Chronicles does differently and what a source says it means (*Scripture Helps* 28 endnote 2: Chronicles assumes its readers know Samuel and Kings; the compilers chose “such portions as suited the purpose of their composition” and “dwell with most satisfaction upon the ecclesiastical and religious aspects of the history, and the progress of temple worship,” `bd-chronicles` [1]; Kings asks why the nation fell, Chronicles “Are we still God's people and does the covenant still stand?”, `sc-knowhy-861` [4]). Don't explain a difference by the Chronicler's motive unless a source gives it.
- **A chapter that is almost all parallel** (1 Chr. 10, 14, 18, 19; 2 Chr. 9, 10, 23, 27) **may have few notes**: its `setting` and `thread` say where it sits in Chronicles and what the Chronicler changed, and its notes are the changes. There is no length target (`STANDARDS.md` §3).

## The genealogies (1 Chr. 1–9)

Nine chapters, about 400 verses, mostly names. **Proposed:**

- **Each of chapters 1–9 is a chapter like any other** (`setting`, `thread`, sections, `christ`, `explore`), but its sections' plain words summarize a list rather than restate it (“Verses 5–23: the nations descended from Noah's three sons, as in Genesis 10”), and **a list gets one note on what it is for, not a note per name.** A chapter of genealogy will usually have three to six notes.
- **What the lists are for is said once, in full, at 1 Chr. 1:1** (and the `setting` of chapter 1): the history begins with Adam and runs, through Israel's tribes, to the families who lived in Jerusalem after the exile (9:1–3), so that a people back from Babylon could see themselves in one line from the Creation (`gs-chronicles` [2]; `ot-seminary-lesson-102` [12]; `sc-knowhy-861` [4] on identity; `bd-genealogy` [1]: “Where certain offices or blessings are restricted to particular families, genealogies become of great importance”). Later chapters say in a clause what their list does and point there. The Restoration's use of genealogy for the temple (`bd-genealogy` [3]; D&C 128) belongs in a `christ` or `explore` section, once, without saying the Chronicler had it in mind.
- **Notes go to what a careful reader stops at:** names the reader knows (Adam to Abraham, Esau's kings, Er and Onan, Achar “the troubler of Israel,” 2:7, Jesse's sons, Zerubbabel, Saul's sons Esh-baal and Merib-baal, Samuel and Heman, Zadok); why Judah comes first and Levi in the middle, if a source says; Reuben's birthright given to Joseph (5:1–2); the eastern tribes carried away (5:26); the singers' lines and Samuel counted among the Levites (6:27–48); the women the lists name (`hilton-motherhood-ot` [87]–[127]); Ephraim and Manasseh in Jerusalem after the exile (9:3; *Scripture Helps* 28 [11] and KnoWhy #708 tie Judah's northern settlers to Lehi of Manasseh: theirs, in a clause at most); and the returned exiles of 9:2–34 beside Neh. 11. Where the site already has a note, the table below names it.
- **Jabez (4:9–10)** gets a note: his name and his mother's word (“with sorrow”: the Hebrew, if it answers the question), his prayer and “God granted him that which he requested.” **No Latter-day Saint source read treats Jabez**, and the Bible Dictionary has no entry. **Proposed:** the note says what the text says and why a prayer stands in a list (a list that pauses on a man because he called on God and was heard), from the text; if a source is needed for the name's sense, BDB; no popular reading of the prayer as a formula for prosperity, and no application outside `christ`.
- **Dan and Zebulun** are missing from the tribal lists of chapters 4–8 (both are among Israel's sons in 2:1–2, and Zebulun's land holds Levites' cities in 6:63, 77, but neither has a genealogy of its own). Say so plainly where the reader would notice (the `setting` of chapter 7); no source read explains it, so don't offer an explanation of the writer's own; an older commentary's reading is “one older reading.”
- **The Savior's line** (Matt. 1; Luke 3) runs through these lists; the line of David in 1 Chr. 3 is the place for the `christ` section to name it.

## What a chapter is for here

1 Chronicles is 942 verses in 29 chapters: the genealogies from Adam, Saul's death, David's reign, the ark brought to Jerusalem, and David's preparations for the temple. The reader knows David from Samuel, and comes to Chronicles asking why the story is told again and what is different. Questions a careful reader would ask, by chapter (a start, not a limit):

- **1–2:** *Who wrote Chronicles, and when? Why does it begin with Adam, and why nine chapters of names? How do these lists compare with Genesis? Why is Judah first? Who was Achar (2:7), and why is Er's death recorded?*
- **3–4:** *Why does David's line run past the exile (3:17–24), and who was Zerubbabel? Who was Jabez, and why does a prayer stand in a list? What were the “ancient things” (4:22)?*
- **5–7:** *Why did Joseph have Reuben's birthright, and Judah the ruler (5:1–2)? Who carried the eastern tribes away (5:26)? Why does Levi have the longest list, and who were Heman, Asaph and Ethan? Was Samuel a Levite? Where are Dan and Zebulun?*
- **8–9:** *Why Saul's line twice (8:33–40; 9:35–44), and why “Esh-baal” and “Merib-baal”? Who lived in Jerusalem after the exile (9:2–34), and why are Ephraim and Manasseh among them? Why does 9:1 say “Judah … carried away to Babylon for their transgression”?*
- **10–12:** *Why does Chronicles begin David's story with Saul's death, and what was Saul's sin “against the word of the LORD” and in asking “one that had a familiar spirit” (10:13–14)? Why does it skip David's seven years in Hebron? Who came to David at Ziklag, and what did Amasai's Spirit-given word mean (12:18)?*
- **13–16:** *Why did Uzza die, and what had David done wrong (15:13)? Who were the Levites who carried the ark, and why the singers and instruments? What is David's psalm (16:8–36), and why is it made of three Psalms? Why two places of worship, the ark at Jerusalem and the tabernacle at Gibeon (16:37–42)?*
- **17–20:** *How does the LORD's promise to David read here beside 2 Sam. 7, and what changed? Why are David's wars told so briefly, and where is Bath-sheba (20:1–3)? Why do the numbers differ from Samuel's? Who killed Goliath's brother (20:5)?*
- **21:** *Why does Chronicles say Satan provoked David, where Samuel says the LORD moved him? Why was numbering Israel a sin? What does the Joseph Smith Translation change at 21:15? Why six hundred shekels of gold, where Samuel has fifty of silver? Why did fire come from heaven, and why is this place the temple's (22:1; 2 Chr. 3:1)?*
- **22–24:** *Why could David not build (22:8; 28:3), and what was he allowed to do? How much gold, and can the figure be right (22:14)? Why number the Levites from thirty, then twenty (23:3, 24, 27)? What were the twenty-four courses of priests, and is Abijah's course the one in Luke 1:5?*
- **25–27:** *What did it mean that the singers should “prophesy with harps” (25:1)? Who were the porters and the treasurers? Why the army in twelve monthly courses, and the tribal princes (27)? Why did the census stop (27:24)?*
- **28–29:** *What was the pattern David gave Solomon, and how did he receive it (28:11–19)? What does “serve him with a perfect heart and with a willing mind” ask (28:9)? Why does David's prayer say “of thine own have we given thee” (29:14)? Why does Chronicles crown Solomon “the second time” (29:22)? What were the books of Samuel, Nathan and Gad (29:29)?*

A chapter answers those from the text first. (The `christ` section then applies the chapter to the reader, in statements: `STANDARDS.md` §1.)

**Read the history as the Chronicler framed it.** Chronicles measures kings by whether they sought the LORD: Saul died “for his transgression” and because he “inquired not of the LORD” (10:13–14); David “waxed greater and greater: for the LORD of hosts was with him” (11:9); and the charge to Solomon is “if thou seek him, he will be found of thee; but if thou forsake him, he will cast thee off for ever” (28:9). The ark, the Levites, the singers and the house are the center of David's reign as Chronicles tells it. Say what a pattern shows where a source shows it (the Bible Dictionary on temple worship; KnoWhy #861 on reward and punishment in a king's own lifetime, `sc-knowhy-861` [7]; Hopkin and Hopkin on the Levites' music, `hopkin-psalms-sung` [11]); don't build a scheme of the writer's own.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Quotations were copied from the page named, with its paragraph number; the rest is the drafter's direction of where to look. Nothing goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **“The Chronicler.”** The site already calls the writer “the Chronicler” (capital C) in some twenty notes; do the same, and say “Chronicles” for the book. Neither implies a known author (“Contested”).
- **`when`.** The narrative dates by reigns, and 1 Chronicles by David's (“David reigned over Israel … seven years reigned he in Hebron, and thirty and three years reigned he in Jerusalem,” 29:26–27). **Proposed:** `when` is from the text (the reign, the event), with no B.C. year, as in Kings; chapters 1–9 say “written after the exile; the lists run from Adam to the return” in words of the text's own. A `setting` may give a date in a clause as its source's, never one of the writer's own. Two times are in play in every chapter, the event and the writing; a `setting` keeps them apart.
- **Large numbers.** Chronicles' figures often differ from Samuel's and Kings' (1 Chr. 11:11 and 2 Sam. 23:8; 18:4 and 2 Sam. 8:4; 19:18 and 2 Sam. 10:18; 21:5 and 2 Sam. 24:9; 21:25 and 2 Sam. 24:24; 2 Chr. 3:15; 4:5; 8:18; 9:25; 22:2; 36:9 against Kings) or are very large (1 Chr. 22:14; 29:4, 7; 2 Chr. 13:3, 17; 14:9; 17:14–18; 25:5–6; 26:12–13; 28:6–8). **The site has already explained the general case at `[[2 Sam. 24:9]]`** (“One million three hundred thousand,” resting on `ot-manual-large-numbers`, the reading of the Hebrew *ʾlp* as a unit as well as a thousand), and individual pairs at `[[2 Sam. 10:18]]`, `[[1 Kgs. 4:26]]`, `[[1 Kgs. 5:11]]` and `[[2 Kgs. 8:26]]`. **Proposed:** a note gives the two figures as the texts have them, says what a source says, and points to the site's note for the general explanation; it never harmonizes in the writer's voice and never says which figure is right. The one difference Joseph Smith changed is 2 Chr. 22:2 (below).
- **Names of God.** Copy any phrase with LORD or GOD from `data/kjv/<book>.json` (checked against the Gospel Library: “the LORD” at 2 Chr. 3:1, 17:4 and 18:21, where the source file had “Lord”). That the LORD is the premortal Christ is said where it answers a question, in a clause, pointing to `[[Ex. 3:14]]`.
- **Hebrew only where it answers a reader's question** (`STANDARDS.md` §3). Words that may pay: *śāṭān* without the article at 1 Chr. 21:1 (“Contested”); *Yaʿbēṣ* and *ʿōṣeb*, “sorrow” (4:9); *dāraš*, “seek,” the book's verb (10:14; 16:11; 28:9; 2 Chr. 7:14; 15:2), only where the reader's question turns on it; *nāḇāʾ*, “prophesy,” of the singers (25:1). **Verse numbering differs** in 1 Chr. 6 (King James 6:1 is the Hebrew 5:27; 6:16 is 6:1) and 12 (12:4 is 12:5), and in 2 Chr. 2 and 14 (2:1 is 1:18; 14:1 is 13:23): `hebrew.mjs` prints the Hebrew reference beside the King James. No more than about a quarter of a chapter's notes are Language notes.
- **The Joseph Smith Translation.** The Gospel Library prints five readings: 1 Chr. 21:15 (Appendix), 2 Chr. 18:20, 21, 22 (18:22 in the Appendix, headed “The Lord does not put a lying spirit into the mouths of prophets”), and 2 Chr. 22:2 (“Two and twenty,” to agree with 2 Kgs. 8:26; KnoWhy #786 [13]). Nyman counts three changed verses in 1 Chronicles and twenty-one in 2 Chronicles, most “of little significance other than making the text more readable” (`nyman-jst-historical` [5], [48], [58], [68]); give his count as his. The manuscript (Old Testament Revision 2) was not opened for Chronicles. Use a reading where it answers the reader's question (`STANDARDS.md` §1): 21:15 and 18:20–22 always, 22:2 at its verse.
- **Scripture reads Chronicles.** Jesus names the blood of Zacharias (`[[Matt. 23:35]]`; `[[Luke 11:51]]`; 2 Chr. 24:20–22; the site's `christ` at 2 Kgs. 12 and the theme page “Innocent Blood”: agree, link); Matthew and Luke trace the Savior's line through these genealogies (`bd-genealogy` [2]); Hebrews quotes “I will be his father, and he shall be my son” (`[[Heb. 1:5]]`; 1 Chr. 17:13); Luke places Zacharias “of the course of Abia” (`[[Luke 1:5]]`; 1 Chr. 24:10); Ezra 1:1–3 repeats the decree of Cyrus; Lehi's family of Manasseh lived at Jerusalem (`[[Alma 10:3]]`; *Scripture Helps* 28 [11]). Where scripture quotes or names Chronicles, say so; an echo someone identifies is that author's. **No comparison panel.**
- **The volume guides.** *The Conquest, the Judges, and One Kingdom* (`#rulers`), *Two Kingdoms and Assyria* and *Babylon and the Exile* (`#rulers`), *Persia and the Return* (`#rulers`, `#books`), *A Timeline of the Old Testament* (`#united-kingdom`, `#two-kingdoms`, `#return`), *Temple and Worship* (`#temples`, `#priests`, `#worship`), *Law and Covenant* (`#feasts`, `#renewals`: 2 Chr. 15; 23; 29; 34). None names Chronicles yet; point to their sections, don't retell them.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports an arc a reader would not see chapter by chapter (seeking the LORD, from Saul who “inquired not” to Manasseh who “besought”; the house prepared, filled, cleansed, burned and decreed again; the Levites' songs; the prophets sent “because he had compassion on his people,” 2 Chr. 36:15); `propose-themes` and Codex propose each book's list with its last batch. No theme page is written until the author chooses it.
- **The older commentaries.** Latter-day Saint sources are thin on most of Chronicles (no *Scripture Helps* outside 2 Chr. 14–20, 26 and 30, no *Old Testament Minute*, one KnoWhy on the book). Samuel's and Kings' first batches leaned on Keil and the Cambridge Bible until the author said “cutting back is probably a good idea” (1 Samuel's brief). Before citing either, try the text, the parallel and the site's note on it, BDB, the footnotes and Bible Dictionary, the guides; when only Keil answers, ask whether a careful reader would ask the question at all. Keep them under a third of a chapter's notes (the writer and reviewer count); a note on Keil alone is “one older reading.” **One key each for both books, added 2026-11-13: `keil-chronicles` and `cambridge-chronicles`; never propose them again.** A ledger row carries the chapter's own page: `biblehub.com/commentaries/kad/1_chronicles/<c>.htm`, `…/cambridge/1_chronicles/<c>.htm`, and `2_chronicles`. The commentary on Chronicles is Keil's alone.

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter; point with a `[[reference]]` rather than re-explain; a note's first sentence gives the answer; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; don't join two verses yourself and call it the text's meaning; application only in `christ`, as statements. **No moral verdict in the writer's own words** on David, Solomon, the Chronicler's omissions or the kings; let the text and the Church's sources judge. **No “the Chronicler idealizes” or “whitewashes”** in the writer's voice: say what is left out and what a source says of it. **No psychology** the text does not give.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that sixty-five chapters say the same thing. Quotations were copied from the pages named on 2026-11-13; re-read the page before using one. 2 Chronicles' own questions (the lying spirit, Manasseh, Uzziah, Josiah's death, Elijah's letter) are in its brief.

- **Who wrote Chronicles, and when (1 Chr. 1:1; 2 Chr. 36:22–23).** The Bible Dictionary: one book in Hebrew; its named sources were “generally of the prophetic order” and, “along with Samuel and Kings,” the material it was compiled from; “The date of composition cannot be fixed with certainty”; Ezra–Nehemiah “may be regarded as a sequel” (`bd-chronicles` [1]–[2]). *Scripture Helps*: “The authorship of Chronicles remains uncertain, but most scholars suggest that the books were likely written after the Babylonian exile, possibly in the fourth century BC” (28 [5]). The seminary manual: compiled “sometime after 537 B.C.” (`ot-seminary-chronicles-intro` [5]). Hopkin and Hopkin: “likely written by a temple Levite around 350 BC” (`hopkin-psalms-sung` [11], theirs). **Proposed:** chapter 1's `setting` says the two books were one, the writer is not known, he names his sources and used Samuel and Kings, and the book was finished after the return; it gives *Scripture Helps*' “fourth century BC” as most scholars' view and no date of its own. A tradition that Ezra wrote it was not found in any source read: don't repeat it. **No chapter treats the people of the book as other than real.**
- **Why tell it again: the Chronicler's omissions (David's and Solomon's sins).** Chronicles leaves out David's years as Saul's fugitive, Bath-sheba and Uriah, Amnon, Absalom and Adonijah, and Solomon's foreign wives and his turning (1 Kgs. 11); it moves from the siege of Rabbah to the crown of its king with nothing between (1 Chr. 20:1–3). *Scripture Helps* says Chronicles assumes its readers know Samuel and Kings and gives these omissions as examples (28 endnote 2, relaying the *Jewish Study Bible*); KnoWhy #861 says the Chronicler wrote “to give hope, to create an identity based on worship and the ideals of the kingdom, and to legitimize their newly rebuilt temple” (`sc-knowhy-861` [4]). Chronicles keeps David's census and its plague (21), Uzza's death (13), and the reason David could not build, “Thou hast shed blood abundantly” (22:8; 28:3). **Proposed:** the `setting` of chapter 1, and the note at 20:1, say what is left out and give those sources' reasons, with the evidence that the Chronicler does not hide all of David's faults; the Bath-sheba story points to 2 Samuel 11–12 on the site. Jacob 2:23–24 and D&C 132:38–39 on David and Solomon are 1 Kings' (its brief) and 2 Samuel's notes: point, don't repeat. No note says the Chronicler “idealized” or “whitewashed” David in the writer's voice.
- **Satan in 1 Chr. 21:1, the LORD in 2 Sam. 24:1.** The site's note at 2 Sam. 24:1 already gives both and the Church's footnote there (that “apparently something is missing” and that Satan should be meant), and that the Joseph Smith Translation makes no change. Here the Church's footnote sends “Satan” to the Topical Guide, “Devil” (fn21). The Hebrew is *śāṭān* without the article (`hebrew.mjs`), where Job 1–2 and Zech. 3 have “the satan”; KnoWhy #43 notes that scholars argue whether the figure in such passages is “necessarily an evil entity opposed to God” ([5]), its report. **Proposed:** 21:1 says that Chronicles names Satan, that Samuel's verse is read by it in the Church's footnote, and points to the site's note for the rest; the Hebrew in a clause only if it answers the reader's question (what the word means), from BDB; **no theory of a developing idea of Satan** in the writer's voice, and no note that the two verses contradict each other.
- **The JST of 1 Chr. 21:15 and the LORD who “repented him of the evil.”** The Joseph Smith Translation (Appendix) reads, “for as he was destroying, the Lord beheld Israel, that he repented him of the evil; therefore the Lord stayed the angel,” so that the repenting is Israel's; Nyman: the change “corrects the concept of the Lord repenting” (`nyman-jst-historical` [48]–[52]). The parallel (2 Sam. 24:16) has no note on the site. **Proposed:** 21:15 gives the King James, the JST's wording, and Nyman's reading as his; the site's other notes on the LORD's “repenting” (`occurrences.mjs` for “repented” and “repent him”) are pointed to if one explains the word.
- **Moriah (2 Chr. 3:1).** Chronicles alone names the temple mount “mount Moriah”; the site has weighed whether it is Abraham's at Gen. 22:2 (“Where was Moriah?”) and 2 Sam. 24:18, and Bowen's link through the verb “see” is his (`bowen-mount-lord-provided` [18], [52]). **Proposed:** agree and link; the link to Abraham stays a tradition, as the site words it.
- **David's temple preparations and the pattern by revelation (1 Chr. 22; 28–29).** The text says the pattern was given “by the spirit” and “in writing by his hand upon me” (28:12, 19); Cowan, in the RSC, says the temple was built to a revealed pattern (`cowan-ot-temples` [14], as 1 Kings' brief cites it). **Proposed:** 28:11–19 says what the text says, sets beside it the revelations of temple patterns that scripture gives (Ex. 25:9, 40; D&C 95:14; 124:40–42; 97:10), and says no more about what the pattern contained than the text does. 22:14's sums (“Large numbers”).
- **The courses, the singers and the Levites (23–27).** The text gives the orders as David's (23:6; 24:3; 25:1; 26:1); scholars disagree whether these accounts reflect David's day or the Second Temple, while “the connection with temple worship is not under debate” (`hopkin-psalms-sung` [11]). **Proposed:** say what the text says David did; the scholars' question once, in Hopkin's words as theirs, not argued.
- **Christ in Chronicles.** Scripture's own use first (“Scripture reads Chronicles,” above: the Savior's line, Heb. 1:5, Matt. 23:35, Luke 1:5; KnoWhy #865 uses 1 Chr. 28:6). Then the Church's and faithful scholarship's: the LORD who heals a people who prepare their hearts (2 Chr. 30:18–20; `davis-bended-rules` [5], his); the prophets sent “because he had compassion on his people” (2 Chr. 36:15; `ot-seminary-lesson-102` [26]–[28]); the threshing floor where the destroying angel was stayed and the temple's sacrifices began (Bowen, his; the site's 2 Samuel theme page “died-for-thee” makes its own connection there: agree, link). **Proposed:** each in `christ` or at the note named, in the source's words, after the story's meaning. **No chapter makes the genealogies, the courses, the temple's furnishings, Jabez or Ornan's oxen a type of Christ in the writer's words.**

## Settled without the author

Decided by the standard, not asked of the author (Psalms' brief has the rule):

- **No Church, BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* source answers a reader's real question, and an older commentary does:** give its answer as “one older reading,” within the limit of a third of the notes. If the question isn't one a reader would ask, there is no note.
- **A Samuel or Kings note on the site already answers the question:** point to it and agree. If a Chronicles writer finds that note wrong, the finding goes to the coordinator, not into a Chronicles note that contradicts it.
- **Two Church sources read a verse differently:** give the newer first; where *Scripture Helps* speaks, it leads.
- **A source quoted inside a Church manual** whose work can't be read (the *Jewish Study Bible*, the *NIV Study Bible*, Alter, Holzapfel in *Scripture Helps*' endnotes; Rasmussen and Clarke in the Student Manual) is cited as the manual; Keil may be read on Bible Hub and cited as himself.
- **The Church's footnote points somewhere the note does not go:** leave it.
- **A slip in a source** (`sc-interpreter.md`: KnoWhy #861 [8], “righteousness is punished and wickedness is rewarded,” reversed; `byu.md`: Davis's note 1 on Uzziah's leprosy in Kings): don't repeat it, and don't cite the page for the sentence that has it.
