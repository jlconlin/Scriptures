Twice in 1 Chronicles David repeats what the LORD told him about the house he wanted to build, and the two sayings give two men two names. Of himself: “Thou shalt not build an house for my name, because thou hast been a man of war, and hast shed blood” ([[1 Chr. 28:3]]). Of the son to come: “Behold, a son shall be born to thee, who shall be a man of rest; and I will give him rest from all his enemies round about” ([[1 Chr. 22:9]]). There is one house. The man who wanted it, dedicated his spoil to it and planned it may not build it, and the man who builds it is given a peace he did not win.

I read the two titles as a pair that divides one work between two men: a man of war whose battles won the rest and furnished the house, but who was barred from building it because of the blood he had shed, and a man of rest who built it in a peace he was given. Israel sang the LORD a man of war, and the rest is His: He gives it and calls it “my rest,” so the division, I argue below, is in the men, not in Him. And I read the two halves as meeting only in Jesus Christ, the Son of David who made peace by blood and builds a temple on it, because the blood was His own. That reading is mine. In the Church’s materials, Scripture Central, the Religious Studies Center and *Interpreter*, as far as I searched them, I found no source that treats “man of war” and “man of rest” as a pair, calls Solomon a type of Christ in this, or ties David’s bar from building to Christ. Two things I do not claim: that David’s wars were sins, for the chapter that bars him credits them with the rest; and that Solomon is the answer, for he held only his half, and did not keep it.

<h2 id="i-will-subdue">“I will subdue all thine enemies”</h2>

Chronicles retells Nathan’s oracle of 2 Samuel 7, and where Samuel speaks of rest, Chronicles does not. Samuel sets the scene “when the king sat in his house, and the LORD had given him rest round about from all his enemies” ([[2 Sam. 7:1]]); Chronicles has David sitting in his house and no more ([[1 Chr. 17:1]]). In the promise Samuel has the LORD say, “and have caused thee to rest from all thine enemies” ([[2 Sam. 7:11]]); Chronicles has, “Moreover I will subdue all thine enemies” ([[1 Chr. 17:10]]). Both of Samuel’s verbs are *nûaḥ*, the verb of giving rest; Chronicles’ is another verb, *hiknaʿtî*, “I will subdue.”[@oshb-wlc] Where Samuel looks back on rest given, Chronicles looks forward to enemies subdued.

Chronicles gives no reason for the change. A probable explanation is the wars that follow: the clause is left out “probably because his next three chapters (18–20) are devoted to wars,”[@cambridge-chronicles] to avoid an apparent contradiction “seeing that the greatest of David’s wars … are narrated only in the succeeding chapter.”[@keil-chronicles] The note at [17:1](/1-chronicles/17/) gives that as probable, and I add no reason to it. What a reader can check is the result. The verb *nûaḥ* occurs three times in 1 Chronicles, and each time the LORD gives rest: to the son to come (22:9), to Israel’s leaders (22:18) and to “his people” (23:25).[@oshb-wlc] It is never said of David. I take that as the result of the book’s wording, not as its purpose: in 1 Chronicles the LORD’s rest is given in David’s days, but never to David.

The wars come next, and the book says where their spoil went. From the cities of Hadarezer David took “very much brass, wherewith Solomon made the brasen sea, and the pillars, and the vessels of brass” ([[1 Chr. 18:8]]). The silver and gold “that he brought from all these nations; from Edom, and from Moab, and from the children of Ammon, and from the Philistines, and from Amalek,” he “dedicated unto the LORD” ([[1 Chr. 18:11]]). The campaigns themselves are in the chapters ([18](/1-chronicles/18/)–[20](/1-chronicles/20/)). What matters here is the order: before a word is said against David’s building, his wars are already furnishing the house.

<h2 id="thou-shalt-not-build">“Thou shalt not build”</h2>

The word against him comes when David charges Solomon with the work: “But the word of the LORD came to me, saying, Thou hast shed blood abundantly, and hast made great wars: thou shalt not build an house unto my name, because thou hast shed much blood upon the earth in my sight” ([[1 Chr. 22:8]]). The bar is on building, and the verse names its reason, blood and wars, and nothing else. The next verse is the promise of the son: “a man of rest; and I will give him rest from all his enemies round about: for his name shall be Solomon, and I will give peace and quietness unto Israel in his days” ([[1 Chr. 22:9]]). The name is *Šĕlōmōh*, and the clause that gives his name goes straight on with *wĕšālôm*, “and peace”;[@oshb-wlc] the name means “Peaceable.”[@bd-solomon]

“A man of rest” is *ʾîš mĕnûḥâ*. The noun *mĕnûḥâ* occurs in 1 Chronicles twice, here and at 28:2,[@oshb-wlc] where David tells the assembly, “I had in mine heart to build an house of rest for the ark of the covenant of the LORD, and for the footstool of our God, and had made ready for the building” ([[1 Chr. 28:2]]). “An house of rest” is *bêt mĕnûḥâ*.[@oshb-wlc] One word names the builder and the building. And the next verse gives David the other title: “because thou hast been a man of war, and hast shed blood” ([[1 Chr. 28:3]]). Read together, the two speeches set the titles against each other. The house of rest belongs to the man of rest; the man of war, who wanted it, may not build it.

If that were all, the wars would seem to undo what they had done. The same chapter forbids that reading. Ten verses after the bar, David says to the princes of Israel: “Is not the LORD your God with you? and hath he not given you rest on every side? for he hath given the inhabitants of the land into mine hand; and the land is subdued before the LORD, and before his people” ([[1 Chr. 22:18]]). The rest is already given, in David’s lifetime, and it came by the land given into his hand. He says it again: “The LORD God of Israel hath given rest unto his people” ([[1 Chr. 23:25]]). And he does everything for the house but build it. “In my trouble I have prepared for the house of the LORD,” he tells Solomon, gold, silver, brass, iron, timber and stone ([[1 Chr. 22:14]]), and he hands over its plan: “the LORD made me understand in writing by his hand upon me, even all the works of this pattern” ([[1 Chr. 28:19]]). Its materials “had been collected by David,” and he “had also planned the house and its furniture to its details.”[@bd-temple-of-solomon] So the text holds both things at once: the victories were the LORD’s gift and won the rest, and the man who fought them may not build its house.

Why blood should bar a man whose victories the LORD gave is read more than one way, and the verse gives only the fact. One reading fits 22:18 well: wars and bloodshed “are unavoidable and necessary in this earth for the establishment of the kingdom of God in opposition to its enemies, but are not consonant with its nature, as it was to receive a visible embodiment and expression in the temple. For the kingdom of God is in its essence a kingdom of peace.”[@keil-chronicles] On that reading the wars served the kingdom, and the house was to show what the kingdom is. Other readings look to David himself; the notes at [22:8](/1-chronicles/22/) and [28:2](/1-chronicles/28/) give them, each as its author’s reading. The argument here needs only the verse.

Solomon’s half is given, not won. “I will give him rest” (22:9) is the LORD’s act, and what Solomon is charged with is the work: “Arise therefore, and be doing, and the LORD be with thee” ([[1 Chr. 22:16]]). Nor did he keep it. Kings tells the end: “the LORD was angry with Solomon, because his heart was turned from the LORD God of Israel” ([[1 Kgs. 11:9]]), and “the LORD stirred up an adversary unto Solomon” ([[1 Kgs. 11:14]]), then “another adversary” ([[1 Kgs. 11:23]]). His reign “was not wholly peaceful”;[@cambridge-chronicles] the note at [22:9](/1-chronicles/22/) follows it. Neither man is the whole. The one who won the rest may not build; the one who builds did not win the rest, and did not keep it.

<h2 id="the-lord-is-a-man-of-war">“The LORD is a man of war”</h2>

What the book divides between father and son is the LORD’s before it is theirs. At the sea Israel sang, “The LORD is a man of war: the LORD is his name” ([[Ex. 15:3]]), extolling “the Lord as a man of war.”[@lds-scriptures] The Hebrew is *ʾîš milḥāmâ*, the same two nouns as David’s title in 28:3, *ʾîš milḥāmôt*, which has the second in the plural.[@oshb-wlc] I found no source that sets the two verses side by side, and the observation is mine. It means the title that kept David from building is one Israel sang of the LORD first. So I do not read the bar as saying that war is foreign to Him.

The rest is His as well. Every verse of 1 Chronicles that has the verb of giving rest makes the LORD its giver (22:9; 22:18; 23:25). The house of rest was for His ark and “the footstool of our God” (28:2). When the house stands, Solomon ends his prayer, “Now therefore arise, O LORD God, into thy resting place, thou, and the ark of thy strength” ([[2 Chr. 6:41]]). Solomon’s words are those of the psalm, “Arise, O LORD, into thy rest; thou, and the ark of thy strength” ([[Ps. 132:8]]), where the LORD answers, “This is my rest for ever: here will I dwell; for I have desired it” ([[Ps. 132:14]]). Both verses of the psalm have *mĕnûḥâ*, the word of 22:9 and 28:2; the word in 2 Chr. 6:41 is a different one, *nôaḥ*, and so is the “had rest” of 1 Chr. 6:31, *mānôaḥ*,[@oshb-wlc] and I build nothing on their likeness. David wanted to build a house of rest; the psalm calls the rest itself the LORD’s: “my rest.”

In David’s words, the LORD’s rest also changes a burden. David’s words go on: the LORD has given rest to His people, “that they may dwell in Jerusalem for ever: And also unto the Levites; they shall no more carry the tabernacle, nor any vessels of it for the service thereof” ([[1 Chr. 23:25–26]]). The verb rendered “that they may dwell” is singular in the Hebrew, *wayyiškōn*, “and he dwelt,”[@oshb-wlc] and the note at [23:25](/1-chronicles/23/) reads it of the LORD dwelling in Jerusalem. The Levites who had carried are given another office: “to stand every morning to thank and praise the LORD, and likewise at even” ([[1 Chr. 23:30]]), as David had already set singers over the song “after that the ark had rest” ([[1 Chr. 6:31]]).

The division, then, is in the men, not in Him.

<h2 id="the-blood-of-his-cross">“The blood of his cross”</h2>

Scripture itself reads the promise to this son past Solomon. “I will be his father” is said of him three times (17:13; 22:10; 28:6), and Hebrews asks of Christ, “I will be to him a Father, and he shall be to me a Son?” ([[Heb. 1:5]]), in the words of 17:13.[@lds-scriptures] 1 Chr. 28:6 is among the verses in which “God declares the king of Israel to be his son”;[@sc-knowhy-865] the note at [28:6](/1-chronicles/28/) follows it to Hebrews. Jesus said of Himself, “a greater than Solomon is here” ([[Matt. 12:42]]), and “in this place is one greater than the temple” ([[Matt. 12:6]]). Isaiah gives the son on David’s throne Solomon’s word: “his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace. Of the increase of his government and peace there shall be no end, upon the throne of David” ([[Isa. 9:6–7]]). “Prince of Peace” is *śar šālôm*, with the *šālôm* of the clause that gives Solomon’s name at 22:9,[@oshb-wlc] and “the throne of David” there points to Jesus Christ’s descent from David.[@lds-scriptures] Zechariah’s Branch is a builder: “he shall build the temple of the LORD” ([[Zech. 6:12]]), and the Church reads Joshua the high priest there as crowned “in similitude of Christ, the Branch.”[@lds-scriptures]

Then Paul, in whose words every term of 22:8–9 stands together: blood, a slaying, peace and a temple. Christ “made peace through the blood of his cross” ([[Col. 1:20]]). To the Ephesians: “ye who sometimes were far off are made nigh by the blood of Christ. For he is our peace” ([[Eph. 2:13–14]]); He reconciled both “unto God in one body by the cross, having slain the enmity thereby: And came and preached peace” ([[Eph. 2:16–17]]); and in Him “all the building fitly framed together groweth unto an holy temple in the Lord” ([[Eph. 2:21]]).

Read beside 1 Chronicles 22, the passage holds together what the book divided. Here, as I read it, is a man of war: something is slain. Here is blood, and it does not bar Him from building, as David’s barred David; the temple is built on it. The difference, as I read the verses, is whose blood it is and what was killed. David shed the blood of his enemies “upon the earth in my sight” (22:8). Christ’s enemy was the enmity, and the blood was His own: “by his own blood he entered in once into the holy place” ([[Heb. 9:12]]). So the peace is won and the house is built by one Son of David, and neither half is borrowed from another man. The claim is no wider than that: what Chronicles divides between two men, the New Testament says of one. I do not say that Christ needed David and Solomon, or that they are halves of Him.

<h2 id="arise-and-build">“Arise therefore, and build”</h2>

The rest Christ offers is given, as Solomon’s was, and won by another’s battle, as Israel’s was: “Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me” ([[Matt. 11:28–29]]); “my peace I give unto you: not as the world giveth, give I unto you” ([[John 14:27]]). It is not idleness. In Chronicles rest is what the house is built in: straight after “hath he not given you rest on every side?” David says, “arise therefore, and build ye the sanctuary of the LORD God” ([[1 Chr. 22:19]]). And His rest comes with a yoke. The Levites set the tabernacle down and were not released from service; their charge was still “the service of the house of the LORD” ([[1 Chr. 23:32]]). I read their change as a likeness of His easy yoke and light burden: the carrying ends, and the service includes praise, morning and evening.

The house now is His people: “ye are the temple of God” ([[1 Cor. 3:16]]); “Ye also, as lively stones, are built up a spiritual house” ([[1 Pet. 2:5]]); “In whom ye also are builded together for an habitation of God through the Spirit” ([[Eph. 2:22]]). There is “the direct connection between making a covenant with God in the temple and the Savior’s stunning promise” of rest, and “covenant keepers are entitled to a special kind of rest that comes to them through their covenantal relationship with God.”[@nelson-overcome-world] A disciple does not win the peace he builds in. The rest that still remains is the subject of [Rest Round About](/joshua/themes/rest-round-about/).
