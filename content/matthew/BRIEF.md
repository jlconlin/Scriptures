## What a chapter is for here

The author's purpose for the Gospels: “the purpose is to anticipate questions a reader might have, and answer those questions” (`GOSPELS.md`). Read the chapter, write down what a careful reader would stop and ask, and answer that from sources read in the session (`STANDARDS.md` §3). A note exists because a reader would ask; a source's having something to say is not a reason for a note. A question no source answers goes in the writer's report and then `UNANSWERED.md`.

**Begin with the chapter's stories.** Before writing your questions, run `node scripts/stories.mjs matthew <n>` and, for each story told in another Gospel, `node scripts/stories.mjs <id> --text`, and follow `GOSPELS.md`, “How a story's research is shared”: read the story's research file if there is one, write it if there is not, answer your own reader in full, and fill the chapter's `parallels` from the lines the script prints. Matthew is written first, so for most stories you are the writer who makes the file: put in it what a writer of Mark's or Luke's account will need, and under `open` the questions their readers will ask that yours does not.

Questions a reader of Matthew asks again and again (a start, not a limit):

- *Which prophecy is “fulfilled” here, what did it mean when it was spoken, and how does Matthew read it?* See “The Old Testament in Matthew.”
- *What does this word mean?* The Gospel Library's footnotes already gloss many words from the Greek (“GR”) and explain others (“IE”): at 5:48 “perfect” is “complete, finished, fully developed”; at 16:15 the plural “ye”; at 26:28 “testament” is “covenant.” A note does not repeat a footnote for its own sake (`STANDARDS.md` §1); it goes further only where the reader's question needs more than the gloss.
- *What did Joseph Smith change here, and why?* See “The Joseph Smith Translation in Matthew.”
- *How is this different in the Book of Mormon?* Above all the Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi 12–14).
- *Why does Matthew tell it this way?* What Matthew adds, leaves out or places differently from Mark and Luke (`GOSPELS.md`, “How a story's research is shared”).

**Where answers come from** is `STANDARDS.md` §1's order, with Talmage after the Latter-day Saint studies and Ellicott, the *Expositor's Greek Testament*, Meyer, the *Cambridge Bible* and Edersheim last (`GOSPELS.md`). Matthew is well served by Latter-day Saint studies; search them first. **Don't always go to the same source first** (`GOSPELS.md`): the works that cover every verse (*Jesus the Christ*, the Bible Dictionary, Ellicott) are cited when they answer the question, and are not where the search begins. The entries quoted in this brief are leads for the questions they are quoted under.

## Divisions

Matthew's own shape. Many scholars see the body of the Gospel as five sections, each closing with a long discourse that ends “when Jesus had ended these sayings” or the like ([[Matt. 7:28]]; [[Matt. 11:1]]; [[Matt. 13:53]]; [[Matt. 19:1]]; [[Matt. 26:1]]), framed by the birth at the start and the death and resurrection at the end (`griffin-matthew-portrayal` [25]; `strathearn-matthew-editor` [22], who names the discourses the Sermon on the Mount, the Apostolic Commission, the Kingdom of God discourse, the Church Administration discourse and the Eschatological discourse). Some read the five as an allusion to the five books of Moses (Griffin [25]); that is a reading, and the divisions do not depend on it. The Bible Dictionary gives no outline of Matthew. **Proposed divisions** (in `book-draft.yaml`):

| Chapters | Division | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | The Birth of the King | the infancy narrative |
| 3–7 | The Kingdom at Hand, and the Sermon on the Mount | first discourse, ends 7:28 |
| 8–10 | Miracles, and the Twelve Sent Out | second discourse, ends 11:1 |
| 11–13 | Opposition, and the Parables of the Kingdom | third discourse, ends 13:53 |
| 14–18 | Peter's Witness, the Mount, and the Church | fourth discourse, ends 19:1 |
| 19–25 | To Jerusalem, and the Last Teachings | fifth discourse (24–25), ends 26:1 |
| 26–28 | The Passion and the Resurrection | the end |

A chapter's sections follow its stories (`node scripts/stories.mjs matthew <n>`).

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks were copied from the page named. The rest is the drafter's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **The Old Testament in Matthew.** Matthew quotes the Old Testament more than any other Gospel: “over sixty quotations,” “at least thirteen specific instances” where an act or saying “expressly fulfilled a prophecy” (`griffin-matthew-portrayal` [9]); “approximately 124 quotations and allusions” (`frederick-use-of-ot` [10]); the formula “that it might be fulfilled” used “as many as fourteen times,” eight of them Isaiah (`millet-js-matthew` [129]). All those Old Testament chapters are on the site. **Proposed:** a fulfillment note (1) says what the passage meant where it was first spoken, in a sentence, linking the site's chapter (`[[Isa. 7:14]]`, `[[Hos. 11:1]]`, `[[Jer. 31:15]]`, `[[Mic. 5:2]]`, `[[Zech. 9:9]]`), and does not re-explain what that chapter's note already explains; (2) says how Matthew reads it of Jesus; (3) is honest that “fulfilled” in Matthew is not always prediction. Hosea 11:1 speaks of Israel's past, which “does not preclude” its foreshadowing Jesus or Matthew's using it of Him (`frederick-use-of-ot` [25]). Read the linked note first (`occurrences.mjs all "<phrase>" --site`) so the two agree. Matthew 27:9 names “Jeremy” for words close to [[Zech. 11:12–13]]; the Jeremiah 19 chapter already has a sourced note on it: link it.
- **Old Testament names in their Greek forms.** The King James New Testament spells them as Greek does: Esaias (Isaiah), Elias (Elijah, [[Matt. 16:14]] footnote; Bible Dictionary “Elias,” use 1), Noe (Noah), Jeremy and Jeremias (Jeremiah), Jonas (Jonah). A `phrase` keeps the spelling of its verse (`data/kjv/matthew.json`); the note says who is meant the first time in a chapter. “Elias” has more than one use in Restoration scripture (`bd-elias`): at the Transfiguration and in [[Matt. 17:10–13]] say which use the verse and the Joseph Smith Translation require, from the Bible Dictionary.
- **The Greek.** `node scripts/greek.mjs matthew <c>:<v>` prints the Textus Receptus (the text behind the King James Version) word by word with Strong's numbers; the lexicon is Thayer on Bible Hub (`biblehub.com/greek/<strongs>.htm`, section “Thayer's Greek Lexicon”; key `thayer`, or `strongs-greek` as earlier chapters used). Where a reader's question turns on a difference between the King James text and the modern critical text, the SBL Greek New Testament is read in Bible Hub's interlinear (`greek-nt`). The test for a Language note is the Hebrew one in `STANDARDS.md` §3: the Greek answers a question the English raises.
- **Matthew's own wording is the text.** A note quotes the verse it sits on; Mark's or Luke's wording comes in only where the difference is the question (`GOSPELS.md`).
- **Dates and places.** The author's chronology is the order the site follows (`GOSPELS.md`). A year (Herod's death, the crucifixion) is given only from a source read, as that source's, never as settled where sources differ. The Bible Dictionary places the Transfiguration “in about October, some six months before the death of Jesus” (`bd-transfiguration`).
- **The Pharisees, the scribes and “the Jews.”** See “Contested and sensitive questions.” The convention for every chapter: name the group the verse names (the Pharisees, the chief priests and elders, the multitude), and never let a note's wording pass from a group in a scene to a people in every age.
- **Talmage.** *Jesus the Christ* is cited wherever it answers the reader's question, like any source; it is not the place to start (`GOSPELS.md`, “Anticipate the reader's questions…”). It runs in order through the life (chapter 17 is the Sermon on the Mount; chapter 37 the Resurrection and Ascension); find the chapter for a passage on its contents page.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each. The writer reads the source again before writing the note and quotes from the page.

- **Who wrote it, for whom, and when.** The Bible Dictionary: Matthew, a publican called from Capernaum (“Matthew”); his Gospel “was written for the use of Jewish persons in Palestine,” its “chief object is to show that Jesus is the Messiah of whom the prophets spoke,” and it “was probably written in Aramaic but is known to us by a Greek translation” (`bd-matthew`); the Guide to the Scriptures says it “was probably written initially for the use of Jews in Palestine” (`gs-matthew`). The Joseph Smith Translation titles it “The Testimony of St. Matthew” (the Gospel Library's footnote on the title). Latter-day Saint scholars affirm the apostle's authorship (`millet-js-matthew` [12]); “ancient Christian writings are unanimous” in it, though modern scholars debate it (`strathearn-matthew-editor` [6]); many scholars think Mark was written first and Matthew used it (`strathearn-matthew-editor` [16]; `griffin-matthew-portrayal` [6]). Both Griffin [11] and Strathearn [24–28] read the gentile thread (the wise men, the centurion, “all nations” in [[Matt. 28:19]]) as showing a wider audience than Jews alone. Matthew is “typically dated” to the AD 80s, “although some date it earlier” (`lavender-torah-matthew` [11]). **Proposed:** the setting of chapter 1 says once what the Bible Dictionary says, adds that Latter-day Saint scholars accept the apostle as author, and gives the scholarly views (Mark first; a date after AD 70) as views, attributed in the sources, without arguing them. No chapter builds on a hypothetical source document (“Q”). Where a note needs Matthew's editorial choices (what he adds or arranges), Strathearn's essay is the guide; it compares him with Mormon as an editor (`strathearn-matthew-editor` [7]).
- **The genealogy and Luke's ([[Matt. 1:1–17]]; [[Luke 3:23–38]]).** The Bible Dictionary: Matthew's descends from Abraham and Luke's ascends to Adam; the names largely differ; “the probable explanation is that the descent may be traced through two different lines. Matthew gives a legal descent and includes several adopted children, … while Luke gives a natural descent” (`bd-genealogy`). The Joseph Smith Translation changes 1:16 (“as the prophets have written, who is called Christ”, JST Matt. 1:4 in the footnote). Matthew follows Joseph because royal succession was through the father, and the angel calls him “thou son of David” (`griffin-matthew-portrayal` [18]); the four women before Mary include Gentiles (`strathearn-matthew-editor` [25]); the opening words *biblos geneseōs* echo Genesis (`griffin-matthew-portrayal` [14]). **Proposed:** follow the Bible Dictionary's explanation as “probable,” as it says; the Old Testament names link to the site's chapters (Ruth 4, 1 Chronicles 2–3, Ezra, Haggai, the footnotes give them). Do not count or reconcile the three fourteens beyond what a source read says.
- **The virgin birth and Isaiah 7:14.** The site's Isaiah 7 already has three notes on 7:14 (the prophecy fulfilled in Christ, the Hebrew *ʿalmah* and the Greek *parthenos*, and who the child of Ahaz's day was). Restoration scripture is plain: “by the power of the Holy Ghost” ([[Alma 7:10]]; `griffin-matthew-portrayal` [15]); [[1 Ne. 11:13–21]]. Combs reads the prophecy as fulfilled in Isaiah's day and used by Matthew to teach Jesus's mission (`combs-ahaz-sign` [8]); Frederick calls the immediate fulfillment “a riddle” (`frederick-use-of-ot` [18]). **Proposed:** Matthew 1's note states the virgin birth as scripture and the Church teach it, links the Isaiah notes for the Hebrew and the near fulfillment rather than repeating them, and says what Matthew's “God with us” means in his Gospel (it returns as “I am with you alway,” [[Matt. 28:20]], which `GOSPELS.md` gives as an example of an arc).
- **The Sermon on the Mount beside 3 Nephi 12–14 and the Joseph Smith Translation.** Three texts: Matthew 5–7, the Sermon at the Temple, and the Joseph Smith Translation (appendix entries for Matthew 5, 6 and 7). The Book of Mormon's additions are covenantal: two added beatitudes, “who come unto me” (3 Ne. 12:3), “filled with the Holy Ghost” (12:6), the sacrifice of a broken heart (12:19–20), and part of the sermon addressed to the Twelve (13:25) (KnoWhy #755, [6]–[9]; Cloward, “The Sermon on the Mount in the JST and the Book of Mormon” [50]). The footnotes already give several of these readings beside the verse (5:3 “who come unto me”; 5:22 “without a cause” omitted by both). **Proposed:** a note brings in 3 Nephi or the Joseph Smith Translation where the reading answers the reader's question about Matthew's verse (Why “without a cause”? What does “perfect” mean, beside [[3 Ne. 12:48]]?), and says what the difference shows; no note lists the differences, and the footnote's own reading is not restated for its own sake. The RSC volume *The Sermon on the Mount in Latter-day Scripture* (2010) is the first place to look for each passage.
- **“Fulfil” the law ([[Matt. 5:17–20]]).** The Book of Mormon gives the Lord's own reading: “the law is fulfilled that was given unto Moses … therefore it hath an end” ([[3 Ne. 15:4–5]]; 15:8). A recent BYU Studies article argues that Matthew meant “observe” and that his community kept the Torah (`lavender-torah-matthew` [10]). **Proposed:** the note follows 3 Nephi 15; the article may inform first-century context (how Jesus's teaching stood among Jewish debates of the time) but is not set beside 3 Nephi as an equal reading (`STANDARDS.md` §1).
- **“The kingdom of heaven.”** Matthew's usual phrase. The Bible Dictionary: “kingdom of heaven is sometimes used in scripture to mean the Church (as in Matt. 3:2; 4:17; 13; and 25:1–13)” (`bd-kingdom-of-heaven`); the Guide to the Scriptures: “the Church is the kingdom of heaven on earth” (`gs-kingdom-of-god`). The Joseph Smith Translation of Matthew 13 adds that “the end of the world” is “the destruction of the wicked” (JST appendix, Matt. 13:39–40). **Proposed:** say once, in the first chapter where the phrase is a reader's question (3:2), what it means, from these two entries; a later chapter links that note. Any claim about *why* Matthew prefers it needs a source read.
- **Peter, the rock and the keys ([[Matt. 16:13–20]]).** Matthew alone has “upon this rock I will build my church” and the keys; only Matthew among the Gospels uses “church” (16:18; 18:17) (`griffin-matthew-portrayal` [6]; `millet-js-matthew` [16]). The Church's sources: “it is the Christ, the Son of God, which Peter had just confessed, who is the true cornerstone of the Church” (`bd-peter` [1]); Joseph Smith in 1843: “upon this rock I will build my Church … what rock, Revelation” (`jsp-discourse-1843-01-22`, pp. [6]–[7]). Latter-day Saint scholars read the wordplay variously: Bowen, that *Petros*/*petra* does not make Peter the rock but identifies him with it, and that the rock is revelation, the gospel and its ordinances (`bowen-founded-rock`, abstract and [31]–[32]); “Upon this Rock” (RSC, *Peter and the Popes*), that the Aramaic suggests Jesus said “thou art a rock and upon this rock” and that Latter-day Saints hold the Church is built “upon the rock of revelation—not on Peter” ([8], [12]); Brown, on the Semitic wording and the keys (`brown-peters-keys` [10], [13]). **Proposed:** the note gives Joseph Smith's teaching and the Bible Dictionary's as the Church's reading; the Greek wordplay is explained (Thayer), and the scholars' readings come in only as far as the reader's question needs. The note argues against no other church. The keys promised here are given on the mount (next item) and named in 18:18.
- **The Transfiguration and the keys ([[Matt. 17:1–13]]).** The Bible Dictionary: “the Savior, Moses, and Elias (Elijah) gave the promised keys of the priesthood to Peter, James, and John,” citing Joseph Smith; they also saw the earth in its glory (D&C 63:20–21); the event is like D&C 110 (`bd-transfiguration`). Joseph Smith, 1841: “The Saviour, Moses, & Elias—gave the Keys to Peter, James & John on the Mount” (quoted by Harper, `harper-peter-priesthood` [24]; read the Joseph Smith Papers original before citing it as his). A revelation of 1831 told Joseph Smith that he had not yet received the full account (D&C 63:20–23, as Harper [24] reads it). **Proposed:** the note rests on the Bible Dictionary, the Joseph Smith Papers and D&C 63 and 110; what else was received on the mount (an endowment, the more sure word of prophecy) is given only as the teaching of the one who taught it, from a page read, and marked as such (Harper [26] reports Joseph Fielding Smith's reading).
- **The hard sayings on divorce ([[Matt. 5:31–32]]; [[Matt. 19:3–12]]).** The setting is the first-century debate between the schools of Shammai and Hillel over Deuteronomy 24:1 (`frederick-divorce` [33], [35]; link [[Deut. 24:1]]). Draper explains *porneia* and *moicheia* and the Joseph Smith Translation of 5:31–32 (`draper-divorce` [8], [18]). The Church's position is Elder Oaks's: the kind of marriage required for exaltation “does not contemplate divorce,” but because “of the hardness of [our] hearts,” the Lord “does not currently enforce the consequences of the celestial standard” (`oaks-divorce` [14]). **Proposed:** the notes explain what Jesus said in its setting, then give Elder Oaks's teaching as the Church's present position, quoted briefly; they do not apply the verse to a reader's own circumstances, and they speak with care, since many readers have been divorced.
- **The woes of chapter 23.** The chapter is Jesus's rebuke of “the scribes and Pharisees,” and Matthew is harsher toward the Jewish leaders than any other Gospel (`griffin-matthew-portrayal` [10]); Strathearn reads “their synagogues” and the like as signs of tension between Matthew's community and the synagogue (`strathearn-matthew-editor` [11]). The Joseph Smith Translation adds to 23 (appendix: “one is your creator and heavenly Father”). **Proposed:** each woe is explained as a rebuke of a practice the verse names (tithing mint while leaving “judgment, mercy, and faith,” [[Matt. 23:23]]); the Pharisees are described from a source as a group of their day, not as a type of Judaism; and the `christ` section turns the rebuke on the disciple, where the verse does (23:8–12, 26). Millet's 1985 essay (`millet-js-matthew` [86]–[89]) is useful for its Joseph Smith Translation readings; its heading, “Jesus' Denunciation of Judaism,” is not the site's framing.
- **“His blood be on us, and on our children” ([[Matt. 27:25]]) and how the notes speak of the Jews.** Huntsman and Hatch: the line has been used “from late antiquity to the mid-twentieth century to accuse Jews of being ‘Christ killers’”; the crowd before Pilate was probably small and largely priestly; and the law of Moses itself rejects punishing children for their parents' sins (Deut. 24:16; Ezek. 18:20) (`huntsman-good-friday` [69], [71]–[72]). They also suggest the line “may well have been an embellishment” ([72]); that is their view. The Book of Mormon rebukes the Gentiles who “have cursed” the Jews, “mine ancient covenant people,” and “have not sought to recover them” ([[2 Ne. 29:4–5]]). **Proposed:** the note on 27:25 says who cried out (the crowd at that hour, as the verse says), what the cry meant in that scene, how it has been misused, and what the law and the Book of Mormon say against inherited guilt and against contempt for the Jews; it does not say or imply that the Jewish people bear the guilt of the crucifixion, and it does not call the verse an embellishment in the site's own voice. Where Matthew's “the Jews” or “the people” is a reader's question elsewhere, the same rule holds. This is a sensitive question the standard lets the drafter settle; the author may wish to see the note.
- **Matthew 24 and Joseph Smith—Matthew.** Joseph Smith—Matthew is Matthew 23:39–24:51 as Joseph Smith revised it, and is live with its own chapter. **Proposed:** Matthew 24's `setting` says in two or three sentences that the Lord gave a fuller and reordered text through Joseph Smith, and links it; each note on a verse answers the reader of the King James verse briefly (the abomination of desolation, “this generation,” the eagles and the carcase) and links the Joseph Smith—Matthew note that answers it more fully (`[[JS—M 1:12]]` and so on), rather than repeating it. Where the King James order itself raises the question (which signs belong to the fall of Jerusalem and which to the end), the answer is the translation's order, from Joseph Smith—Matthew's brief and Jackson. Matthew 23:39 links the same way. Matthew 25 has no counterpart there and is written in full.
- **The resurrection accounts ([[Matt. 28]]).** The Gospels differ in who came first to the tomb and whom the risen Lord appeared to (Julie M. Smith, “The Resurrection,” RSC, [12]). Only Matthew has the guard at the tomb and the bribe (27:62–66; 28:11–15), and the commission to “all nations” (28:19–20) closes the Gospel that began with “God with us.” **Proposed:** each account is explained as Matthew tells it; a difference from another Gospel is stated from the verses, and what it means only from a source read (`GOSPELS.md`). Talmage chapter 37 is read for his account and his testimony, after the newer work. The site gives no harmony of the appearances in a note; that belongs to the harmony guide.

## The Joseph Smith Translation in Matthew

- **How heavy it is.** Joseph Smith changed 483 verses of Matthew, more than in any book but Genesis (662) and Luke (563) (`millet-js-matthew` [11]). For Matthew 1 through John 5 he dictated the whole text, so these books have many more small, clarifying changes than the rest of the New Testament (`jackson-jst-new-testament` [11]–[12]). He named Matthew's and John's Gospels “testimonies” (Jackson [14]).
- **When.** D&C 45:60–61 (7 March 1831) turned him to the New Testament; the manuscript of Matthew 1 is dated 8 March 1831; he worked on Matthew 1:1–9:2 and Genesis together until 5 April, on Matthew alone from 7 April to 19 June, and finished the Gospel in September (`millet-js-matthew` [11]). Matthew 24 was revised “sometime between mid-April and mid-June 1831” (Jackson [31]).
- **What the reader already has.** The footnotes give many readings (in the chapters sampled: 1:16 and 1:18; 5:22, 30, 48; 16:8, 24; 26:26, 29, 66). The appendix has entries for chapters 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 11, 12, 13, 16, 17, 18, 19, 21, 23, 26 and 27 (`jst-matt-<n>`, address `…/scriptures/jst/jst-matt/<n>`). Among them: the Spirit, not Satan, leads Jesus into the wilderness (4:1); “suffer us not to be led into temptation” (6:14); Jesus rejects the Pharisees' baptism (9:18–22; `millet-js-matthew` [122]–[126]); two Eliases (17); little children (18, 19); the sacrament (26:22); Judas's death (27).
- **What is only in the manuscripts.** Most changes are small clarifications that the Church's edition does not print, since it chose the readings “of doctrinal or historical importance” (`jackson-jst-new-testament` [29]). The manuscripts are New Testament Revision 1 and 2 in the Joseph Smith Papers (`…/paper-summary/new-testament-revision-1/<page>`, `…-2/<page>`; both load). A writer who needs an unprinted reading quotes the manuscript page or Jackson's or Millet's transcription, never memory of *A Plainer Translation*.
- **How to use it.** Judiciously, where the change answers the reader's question (`STANDARDS.md` §1). It is “not the official Bible” of the Church but “an invaluable aid to biblical interpretation” (`bd-jst`). Some changes may draw on the textual scholarship of Joseph Smith's day (Wayment, reported by Jackson [25]); say that only from a page read, and never argue from the Greek that a change is or is not what Matthew wrote (the rule of Joseph Smith—Matthew's brief).
