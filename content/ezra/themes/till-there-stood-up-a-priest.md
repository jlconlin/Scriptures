The book of Ezra contains two long lists of names, and most readers skip both. The first, in chapter 2, counts the people who came home from Babylon. The second, in chapter 10, names the men found guilty in the book's last crisis. They stand at either end of the book, decades apart.

I read them together. Each list has priests in it. In each, something about those priests is “found” or “not found.” And between the two lists the book's leading priest prays a prayer whose last sentence uses the Hebrew verb with which the first list left a question waiting.

This essay follows that question. In chapter 2 it is asked about a few families: who is fit to serve as a priest before God? By chapter 9 it has become a question about everyone. My argument is that the book of Ezra leaves a need exposed, and that the Epistle to the Hebrews describes the priest who meets it.

## Not found

Near the end of the first list are some families who had a problem. They said they were priests, but they could not prove it:

> These sought their register among those that were reckoned by genealogy, but they were not found: therefore were they, as polluted, put from the priesthood. ([[Ezra 2:62]])

This was not harshness. Under the law of Moses the priesthood at the altar belonged to the sons of Aaron and to no one else.[@bd-priests] A priest was a priest because of who his father was, and the register was the proof. Without it, there was no way for anyone to know.

What the governor did next is the interesting part. He did not rule that these men were impostors, and he did not let them serve. He set the case aside to wait:

> And the Tirshatha said unto them, that they should not eat of the most holy things, till there stood up a priest with Urim and with Thummim. ([[Ezra 2:63]])

The Urim and Thummim was the means “prepared of God to assist man in obtaining revelation from the Lord.”[@bd-urim-and-thummim] It was kept in the high priest's breastplate, so that Aaron might “bear the judgment of the children of Israel upon his heart before the LORD continually” ([[Ex. 28:30]]). A priest who had it could go in before the Lord and bring back the Lord's own answer.

So the governor's ruling admits something. The register can settle most cases. But where the register fails, the only thing that can decide whether a man may serve at the altar is a priest who can go before God and ask.

The record itself is not being slighted here. Latter-day revelation takes it with full seriousness, and cites these very priests as a warning to any whose names “are not found written in the book of the law” ([[D&C 85:11–12]]). A name in the Lord's record matters. The governor's ruling only shows where a record reaches its limit.

The book of Ezra never says that the priest he waited for came, and it does not mention the matter again. The older commentary says plainly that the governor's “expectation, however, was unfulfilled,”[@keil-ezra] and the Bible Dictionary reports that Jewish tradition counted the Urim and Thummim among the things the second temple lacked.[@bd-temple-of-zerubbabel]

## The fullest register in the book

When Ezra himself appears, five chapters later, the book introduces him in a way it introduces no one else. It gives his register.

It runs for five verses: Ezra the son of Seraiah, the son of Azariah, the son of Hilkiah, and on through sixteen generations until it reaches “Aaron the chief priest” ([[Ezra 7:5]]). Nothing could be further from the families of chapter 2. They looked for their names and could not find them. Ezra's line is written into scripture back to the first high priest.

His character matches his record. He is “a ready scribe in the law of Moses” ([[Ezra 7:6]]), and he “had prepared his heart to seek the law of the LORD, and to do it, and to teach in Israel statutes and judgments” ([[Ezra 7:10]]). The king of Persia trusts him. The hand of God is on him at every step of the road.

If the test of chapter 2 is the whole test, here is a man who passes it. A reader who remembers the unfinished business of chapter 2 might well wonder whether this is the priest who will settle things.

## Before thee in our trespasses

Then Ezra is told that the people, and their priests and leaders first of all, have been unfaithful to the covenant. What he does in answer is the turning point of the book.

He does not stand to pray. He rises from his fasting and goes down again: “I fell upon my knees, and spread out my hands unto the LORD my God” ([[Ezra 9:5]]).

He does not separate himself from the guilty either. Nothing in the report names Ezra among them; he has only lately arrived. But from his first sentence he speaks as one of them: “O my God, I am ashamed and blush to lift up my face to thee, my God: for our iniquities are increased over our head” ([[Ezra 9:6]]). It is “our” all the way through.

And he asks for nothing. The prayer is a confession from beginning to end. The older commentary noticed this, and explains it by Ezra's purpose: “Ezra does not pray for the pardon of their sin,” because he wants the congregation to see how great the sin is and to act.[@keil-ezra] That may well be right. I notice also, as my own reading, how the prayer ends, because its last sentence gives a reason of its own:

> O LORD God of Israel, thou art righteous: for we remain yet escaped, as it is this day: behold, we are before thee in our trespasses: for we cannot stand before thee because of this. ([[Ezra 9:15]])

The verb “stand” there is the verb of chapter 2. “Till there *stood up* a priest” and “we cannot *stand* before thee” are the same Hebrew word.[@oshb-wlc]

I do not want to make more of that than it will bear. The verb is a common one. The book uses it for setting up the house and for appointing Levites to their posts, and a few verses after the prayer it uses it of people who cannot stand outdoors in the rain ([[Ezra 10:13]]). In chapter 2 it has the sense of someone arising or appearing.[@bdb] The writer may not have planned an echo. What joins the two verses for me is their subject. Chapter 2 waits for a priest who can go before God on behalf of men whose priesthood is in doubt. Chapter 9 hears a priest whose priesthood is in no doubt at all say that before God he and his people cannot stand.

The second list then closes the circle. It begins: “And among the sons of the priests there were found” those who were guilty, “namely, of the sons of Jeshua the son of Jozadak, and his brethren” ([[Ezra 10:18]]). “Found” is the verb of chapter 2 again.[@oshb-wlc] There, certain priests were *not found* in the register and were put out. Here, priests are *found* in the list of the guilty, and the family named first is the high priest's own, the house of the Jeshua who built the altar in chapter 3.

The book does answer Ezra's prayer in its own way, and that should be said. The people weep; one of them says “there is hope in Israel concerning this thing”; they make a covenant; and the priests named in the list “offered a ram of the flock for their trespass” ([[Ezra 10:2]]; [[Ezra 10:19]]). The law provided for repentance, and the people used what it provided.

Here is the reading I am offering beyond that, and it is mine. The book has shown its reader what a register can do and what it cannot. It can tell who is a son of Aaron. It could not make a son of Aaron able to stand before God. The families of chapter 2 could not show the right descent, and the family of chapter 10 was the high priest's own, and at the end all of them need the same thing: a priest who can go before God for them. The ram is offered by guilty priests for themselves.

None of this is a mark against Ezra. He is the nearest thing to a mediator the book has. He takes the people's guilt on his own lips, though nothing charged him with it, at the hour of the evening sacrifice. It is because he is so good a priest that his last sentence weighs so much.

## The high priest in filthy garments

The book of Ezra says no more, but two of the prophets do, and they use the same word.

Zechariah prophesied in Jerusalem while the temple was being rebuilt; Ezra names him ([[Ezra 5:1]]). In one of his visions he sees the high priest of those years. Ezra calls him “Jeshua the son of Jozadak” ([[Ezra 3:2]]); Haggai and Zechariah call him “Joshua the son of Josedech, the high priest” ([[Hag. 1:1]]).

> And he shewed me Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the LORD, and Satan standing at his right hand to resist him… Now Joshua was clothed with filthy garments, and stood before the angel. ([[Zech. 3:1]]; [[Zech. 3:3]])

“Standing” is the verb of Ezra 2:63 and 9:15.[@oshb-wlc] Here is a priest who is before the Lord, and he has an accuser beside him and filthy clothes on his back. What happens next is not his doing. “Take away the filthy garments from him,” the angel says, and then to Joshua, “Behold, I have caused thine iniquity to pass from thee” ([[Zech. 3:4]]). Only then is he promised a lasting place: “I will give thee places to walk among these that stand by” ([[Zech. 3:7]]). The high priest is cleansed by Another, and so given his place among those who stand in the Lord's presence.

The vision goes on to say how the same will be done for the land. “I will bring forth my servant the BRANCH,” the Lord says, and “I will remove the iniquity of that land in one day” ([[Zech. 3:8–9]]). The Church's heading to the chapter reads this of the Messiah.[@lds-scriptures]

Malachi asks Ezra's question outright. The Lord is coming to His temple: “But who may abide the day of his coming? and who shall stand when he appeareth?” ([[Mal. 3:2]]). It is the same verb once more.[@oshb-wlc] And his answer concerns the priests in particular: the Lord Himself “shall purify the sons of Levi” ([[Mal. 3:3]]).

As I read them, these prophets agree with Ezra's prayer and go a step beyond it. A priest may be before the Lord, as Joshua was, and still be unfit to remain there. His lasting place among those who stand is given when the Lord does for him what he cannot do.

## Another priest

The Epistle to the Hebrews takes up the question in the form Ezra 2 gave it, as a matter of descent.

Its writer asks why, if the priesthood of Aaron's house could bring people all the way to God, there should ever have been “further need… that another priest should rise after the order of Melchisedec, and not be called after the order of Aaron” ([[Heb. 7:11]]). Then he says the thing that would have ended the discussion in Zerubbabel's Jerusalem. Our Lord belonged to a tribe “of which no man gave attendance at the altar”: “it is evident that our Lord sprang out of Juda; of which tribe Moses spake nothing concerning priesthood” ([[Heb. 7:13–14]]).

By the test of Ezra 2, then, the name of Jesus of Nazareth would not have been found among the sons of Aaron. Hebrews does not hide this. It makes it the point. He is a priest “not after the law of a carnal commandment, but after the power of an endless life” ([[Heb. 7:16]]).

The Joseph Smith Translation of this chapter says that the order of priesthood to which Melchizedek was ordained, “the order of the Son of God,” was “without father, without mother, without descent” ([[JST, Heb. 7:3]]).[@jst-heb-7] The Church's manual draws the contrast with the Aaronic order, which came by lineage, and says that the Savior's right to the priesthood rests “not on ancestry but on ‘the power of an endless life.’”[@nt-student-manual-49] Because of this, “Jesus Christ could act as the Great High Priest without being either a Levite or a descendent of Aaron.”[@sc-knowhy-643]

No source I have found joins Hebrews 7 to the unanswered ruling of Ezra 2:63, and I do not say that the governor's words were a prophecy of Christ. He was waiting for a high priest of Aaron's line with the breastplate. What I do say is that the book of Ezra leaves a need exposed, and that Hebrews describes the priest who meets it. The governor waited for a priest who could go before God when the register had nothing more to say. Hebrews presents one whose priesthood never rested on the register.

Hebrews also says what such a priest can do, and each thing is something Ezra could not.

Ezra had to say “our iniquities.” Of Christ it says that He is “holy, harmless, undefiled, separate from sinners,” one who “needeth not daily, as those high priests, to offer up sacrifice, first for his own sins, and then for the people's” ([[Heb. 7:26–27]]). Isaiah had said both halves in a line: “he was numbered with the transgressors… and made intercession for the transgressors” ([[Isa. 53:12]]). Ezra numbered himself with the transgressors because he belonged to the people. Christ was numbered with them though He did no sin.

Ezra's prayer asked for nothing. Christ pleads, and the Lord has told us in what words: “Father, behold the sufferings and death of him who did no sin… Wherefore, Father, spare these my brethren that believe on my name” ([[D&C 45:4–5]]). He has something to offer that no son of Aaron had. The site's page on Ezekiel, [A Man to Stand in the Gap](/ezekiel/themes/stand-in-the-gap/), follows that pleading from the Lord's side.

And every priest's work ended, because the priests “were not suffered to continue by reason of death” ([[Heb. 7:23]]). Of Christ, Hebrews says: “Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them” ([[Heb. 7:25]]).

## Through the merits of the Holy Messiah

Ezra's last word in his prayer is “we cannot stand before thee.” Restoration scripture says the same thing as doctrine, and says the rest. Lehi taught “that there is no flesh that can dwell in the presence of God, save it be through the merits, and mercy, and grace of the Holy Messiah” ([[2 Ne. 2:8]]).

Ezra, with a descent written back to Aaron and a lifetime in the scriptures, put none of it forward when he prayed. I read his silence in the light of that doctrine. A good record and a true priesthood are gifts of God and are required of His people. They are not what a person pleads. The members of Christ's church in Moroni's day were numbered and their names taken, and still they are described as “relying alone upon the merits of Christ” ([[Moro. 6:4]]).

The same letter that describes the other priest draws the conclusion, and it is the opposite of Ezra's last sentence, for a reason Ezra did not yet have: “Seeing then that we have a great high priest, that is passed into the heavens, Jesus the Son of God… Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need” ([[Heb. 4:14]]; [[Heb. 4:16]]).

The men of chapter 2 were told to wait until a priest stood up. In that one respect they are a picture of everyone, for everyone comes before God needing a priest. He has stood up, and He ever lives to make intercession.
