The book of Ezra is about going home. It opens with a king's decree that ends the exile, follows the people back to Jerusalem, and stays with them until the house of the Lord stands again. If a reader stopped at the end of chapter 6, it would be a story in which everything lost has been given back.

The book does not stop there. It goes on calling the people who came home by the name of the place they left. Then, in chapter 9, the man the book admires most kneels before the rebuilt temple and says that he and his people are bondmen.

I want to take that sentence seriously. My argument, and it is my own reading, is that Ezra's return makes a picture of the difference between being brought back and being changed. The return was real, and it was God's doing. But coming home is one gift and a new heart is another, and scripture says that the second is what Jesus Christ came to give.

## A captivity ended

The return has to be given its full weight first, because the book gives it.

Ezra's first sentence says that the Lord stirred up the spirit of Cyrus “that the word of the LORD by the mouth of Jeremiah might be fulfilled” ([[Ezra 1:1]]). The people who go up are those “whose spirit God had raised” ([[Ezra 1:5]]). Nothing in this is grudging. God promised, and God did it.

What the people do when they arrive is also right. They build the altar and keep the feast “as it is written” ([[Ezra 3:4]]). When the foundation is laid they sing that the Lord “is good, for his mercy endureth for ever toward Israel” ([[Ezra 3:11]]). After long opposition the house is finished, and they keep its dedication “with joy” ([[Ezra 6:16]]); the priests and Levites are set in their places “as it is written in the book of Moses” ([[Ezra 6:18]]). The chapter ends by saying where the joy came from: “the LORD had made them joyful” ([[Ezra 6:22]]).

So this is not a story of a people going through empty forms. Their worship is correct, and the book says it is glad. The Bible Dictionary can say without qualification that the captivity “was brought to a close by the decree of Cyrus.”[@bd-captivities]

## The children of the captivity

And yet the book keeps a name for these people that a reader might not expect once the captivity is over.

In Hebrew they are *haggôlâ*, “the exile,” or *bĕnê haggôlâ*, the children of the exile. The King James Version gives it as “the children of the captivity” or “those that had been carried away.” The lexicon lists the places where Ezra uses the phrase, and they run to the end of the book.[@bdb]

Where the name appears is what interests me. It is not used only on the road. It is used of the people as they build: “the children of the captivity builded the temple” ([[Ezra 4:1]]). It is used when the house is finished: “the children of the captivity kept the passover” ([[Ezra 6:19]]). It is still their name on the last page, long afterward, when a proclamation goes out “unto all the children of the captivity” ([[Ezra 10:7]]), summoning them to Jerusalem from the towns of Judah.

The book never explains this, and no source I have read explains it either. A name can simply stick. But it is worth keeping in mind when Ezra begins to pray.

## Bondmen, and not forsaken

Ezra arrives in chapter 7 and learns in chapter 9 that the people of his own day, their leaders first, have been unfaithful to the covenant. He tears his clothes, sits appalled until evening, and then falls on his knees before the house of God and prays.

His prayer begins with the history. It was “for our iniquities,” he says, that Israel was handed over “to the sword, to captivity, and to a spoil, and to confusion of face, as it is this day” ([[Ezra 9:7]]). Then he describes the return itself:

> And now for a little space grace hath been shewed from the LORD our God, to leave us a remnant to escape, and to give us a nail in his holy place, that our God may lighten our eyes, and give us a little reviving in our bondage. For we were bondmen; yet our God hath not forsaken us in our bondage. ([[Ezra 9:8–9]])

This is praise, and it should be read as praise. The grace has been shown “for a little space” because the return is still recent when set against the long years of ruin. The nail is an image of something firm; the Church's footnote sends a reader to Isaiah's “nail in a sure place” ([[Isa. 22:23]]).[@lds-scriptures] God has given this people a secure hold in His own house, and Ezra knows it.

It is all the more striking, then, what he calls the people who hold it. The King James Version puts the clause in the past, “we were bondmen,” but the Hebrew has no verb there, only “bondmen we,”[@oshb-wlc] and modern translations give it in the present: “For we are slaves.”[@esv] Ezra is not remembering Babylon. He is describing the people around him in Jerusalem.

What he means in the first place is plain enough. Judah is a small province of the Persian empire, and its people are the king's subjects. That is how the older commentary reads the verse: they are “still bondmen, for they are yet under the Persian yoke.”[@keil-ezra] The Church's Student Manual likewise calls the Israel of these books “a tiny nation in bondage.”[@ot-manual-29]

But Ezra does not leave it as a fact of politics. He has already said where the bondage came from: “for our iniquities.” And his next words are about the present: “And now, O our God, what shall we say after this? for we have forsaken thy commandments” ([[Ezra 9:10]]). Of the people of Ezra's day the Student Manual says that “even the horrors of defeat and exile had not taught the people their lesson.”[@ot-manual-29]

Here is what I draw from it, as my own reading. Ezra kneels before a finished temple, among a people who have been home for generations, and he does not speak of them as a finished work. They have been brought back, they have been given a sure place, God has not forsaken them, and they are bondmen who have forsaken His commandments. Arriving and being changed are different things, and Ezra's prayer holds them apart.

## A gathering and a new heart

Scripture has a pattern that helps here. When the prophets speak of God gathering Israel, they do not speak of a journey alone. They join it to something done inside the people.

Moses said that the Lord “will turn thy captivity,” and then, three verses on, that “the LORD thy God will circumcise thine heart, and the heart of thy seed, to love the LORD thy God with all thine heart” ([[Deut. 30:3]]; [[Deut. 30:6]]). Ezekiel, prophesying among the exiles, put the two in the same order: “I will take you from among the heathen, and gather you out of all countries, and will bring you into your own land,” and then, “A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you” ([[Ezek. 36:24]]; [[Ezek. 36:26]]). Jeremiah promised a covenant in which the Lord would “put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts” ([[Jer. 31:33]]).

I should be careful about what these promises refer to. The Church's edition of the scriptures places Ezekiel's gathering and new heart in the last days,[@lds-scriptures] and its study helps read Jeremiah's new covenant as the gospel “revealed anew in the last days,” the law written in the heart “symbolizing” a people's “true conversion.”[@scripture-helps-jer-31-33] They were not promises that fell due when Cyrus issued his decree, and Ezra does not say they were.

Nor do the Church's sources speak with one voice about the hearts of the people who returned. The Bible Dictionary calls the captivity “a time of great spiritual revival,” citing Ezekiel's very passage, and says it “put a stop to the old sin of idolatry.”[@bd-captivities] The Student Manual, as we saw, says of Ezra's generation that the lesson had not been learned.[@ot-manual-29] I do not try to settle that.

What I can say is what Ezra's own text shows. It records a gathering and calls it “a little reviving.” When it speaks of hearts and spirits, it is almost always God who acts: He stirs the spirit of Cyrus, raises the spirit of those who go up, and turns the hearts of kings ([[Ezra 1:1]]; [[Ezra 1:5]]; [[Ezra 6:22]]; [[Ezra 7:27]]). Of one man only does it say that he worked on his own heart. Ezra “had prepared his heart to seek the law of the LORD, and to do it” ([[Ezra 7:10]]).

And the book ends with the beginning of the thing the pattern looks for. As Ezra prays, “the people wept very sore,” and one of them says, “yet now there is hope in Israel concerning this thing” ([[Ezra 10:1–2]]). It is the only time the word “hope” appears in the book. He proposes what to do with it: “let us make a covenant with our God” ([[Ezra 10:3]]). And when Ezra puts it to them, the whole congregation answers, “As thou hast said, so must we do” ([[Ezra 10:12]]). The Church's footnote on that verse sends a reader to King Benjamin's people, who said, “we are willing to enter into a covenant with our God to do his will” ([[Mosiah 5:5]]).[@lds-scriptures]

The pattern, then, is a gathering that is meant to issue in a changed heart. Ezra shows the gathering in full. Of the change it shows, as I read it, only a beginning: sorrow for sin, and a people binding themselves to do what God has said.

## The servant and the Son

Centuries after Ezra's prayer, Jesus stood in the temple in Jerusalem and told a group of people who believed in Him that the truth would make them free. They objected. “We be Abraham's seed,” they said, “and were never in bondage to any man: how sayest thou, Ye shall be made free?” ([[John 8:33]]).

Ezra had not spoken so. He was Abraham's seed too, and a priest with his descent written out to Aaron, and kneeling before an earlier temple in the same city he had called himself and his people bondmen.

Jesus names the bondage that matters. “Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin” ([[John 8:34]]). Then He says what Ezra could not:

> And the servant abideth not in the house for ever: but the Son abideth ever. If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed. ([[John 8:35–36]])

Setting these two passages side by side is my own connection; I have not found it made elsewhere. What joins them is in the words. Ezra calls his people bondmen and gives the cause: “for our iniquities.” Jesus says that sin is what makes a person a servant. Ezra can confess the condition, and he does it with great honesty. Jesus says that the Son can end it.

He ends it by more than a release. The Church's manual on this passage observes that a son “has a rightful place” in the house, and that through His Atonement Christ “extends that rightful place to each of His followers.”[@nt-student-manual-24] The freedom He gives is a son's or a daughter's place.

I hear the same thing in a story He told, and joining it here is again my own doing. The prodigal son, coming to himself in a far country, plans his return carefully. He will go home and say, “make me as one of thy hired servants” ([[Luke 15:19]]). He means to come back to his father's house and live in it as a servant. The father will not hear of it. The robe, the ring and the shoes all declare that he is received “as his son, not as a servant.”[@nt-student-manual-18] The son had set his hopes on getting home. The father's purpose was to have a son.

The Book of Mormon states the doctrine without a figure. To be redeemed is to be freed from bondage by a price,[@gs-redeem] and Alma, newly delivered from his own rebellion, reports what the Lord told him that redemption consists of: all people must be “changed from their carnal and fallen state, to a state of righteousness, being redeemed of God, becoming his sons and daughters” ([[Mosiah 27:25]]). Redemption is described there as a change in what a person is. King Benjamin's people say the same of themselves, that the Spirit “has wrought a mighty change in us, or in our hearts” ([[Mosiah 5:2]]), and they are told at once what they have become: “the children of Christ, his sons, and his daughters” ([[Mosiah 5:7]]).

## Returning and becoming

I have been reading Ezra's return as a picture, and I should say plainly that the picture is mine and not the book's. Ezra tells of one people at one time. But a journey back to the Lord's house is a natural image of a disciple's hope to return to the presence of God, and Ezra's prayer, said at the end of that journey, is a reminder that arriving is not all that God intends.

President Dallin H. Oaks taught that the gospel is aimed at what we become and not only at what we do. The Final Judgment, he said, “is not just an evaluation of a sum total of good and evil acts—what we have done. It is an acknowledgment of the final effect of our acts and thoughts—what we have become.” The commandments, ordinances and covenants “are not a list of deposits required to be made in some heavenly account,” but “a plan that shows us how to become what our Heavenly Father desires us to become.”[@oaks-challenge-become]

What He desires us to become is stated by the Savior Himself. “I would that ye should be perfect even as I, or your Father who is in heaven is perfect” ([[3 Ne. 12:48]]). “What manner of men ought ye to be? Verily I say unto you, even as I am” ([[3 Ne. 27:27]]). And the power by which it happens is His. Elder D. Todd Christofferson promised that “through Christ you will become holy, as He and our Father are holy.”[@christofferson-born-again]

So the hope of a disciple is not only to get back. It is to be made, by the Atonement of Jesus Christ, into a son or daughter who is like the Father he or she is returning to.

Nor does the change usually come all at once. Ezra's people were given “a little reviving,” and it was a true gift. Elder Christofferson taught that for most of us being born again “is more a process than an event,” and that “engaging in that process is the central purpose of mortality.”[@christofferson-born-again] A little reviving can be a beginning. It is a mercy to be home, to have a nail in the holy place and light for the eyes, and not to be forsaken. The Son, who abides in the house for ever, is the one who makes a bondman free.
