## What the author has decided

Nothing for Ezra in particular beyond choosing it. The author's standing rules are in `STANDARDS.md` §1 and `AUTHORING.md` and are not repeated here. How four of them land on this book:

- **Newest trumps oldest.** The newest Church help is *Scripture Helps: Old Testament* (2025), chapter “Ezra 1; 3–7; Nehemiah 2; 4–6; 8” (`scripture-helps-ezra`, shared with Nehemiah). It has nothing on chapters 2 and 8–10 beyond its overview (paragraph 7). The *Old Testament Student Manual: 1 Kings–Malachi*, chapter 29, “The Exiles Return (Ezra)” (`ot-manual-29`), is older, goes verse by verse through all ten chapters, and prints William Smith, Talmage, Josephus, Adam Clarke and the *Eerdmans' Handbook*; where it and *Scripture Helps* differ (the temple finished in 516 or 515), say both or follow *Scripture Helps*.
- **Quote the Church's material sparingly.** *Scripture Helps* prints President Benson on Cyrus (paragraph 9: “God, the Father of us all, uses the men of the earth, especially good men, to accomplish his purposes”; his own talk, `benson-civic-standards`, paragraph 8): once at most, in chapter 1. Elder Spencer J. Condie, April 1990 conference, on 7:10 (`condie-scriptural-leadership`, paragraph 16): once at most, in chapter 7. The Bible Dictionary's Ezra entry is the book's summary; say it in the site's words and quote at most a phrase per chapter.
- **Don't replicate the Gospel Library.** What the footnotes and *Scripture Helps* already give beside the verse is listed under “What the reader already has.” A note says more than these or says nothing.
- **Apply scripture to the reader, in statements** (`STANDARDS.md` §1). *Come, Follow Me* and the institute manual on Ezra are questions and application (“How do you see the Lord working through men and women around you…?”; “The house of the Lord is a place of joy”); none is copied. A chapter's `christ` section says, in statements, where the chapter's principle reaches a disciple now. **Chapters 9–10 apply nothing to modern marriage** beyond what a Church source states (below).

## What a chapter is for here

Ezra is ten chapters of narrative (280 verses), about two returns from Babylon some eighty years apart, with three long lists, a file of Persian letters in Aramaic, and one man's memoir. A reader asks: *Why would a Persian king send the Jews home and pay for their temple, and what did Isaiah and Jeremiah have to do with it? Who are Sheshbazzar, Zerubbabel and Jeshua? Why list forty-two thousand names and numbers, and why set aside priests who could not find their family record? What is a Urim and Thummim doing in a book with no prophet using one? Why build the altar before the house? Why did the old men weep when the foundation was laid? Who were “the adversaries of Judah and Benjamin,” and why refuse their help? Why do Ahasuerus and Artaxerxes appear before Darius, and why does the letter talk about walls? Why is half the book in another language? Who was Ezra, what is a scribe, and what did it mean that “the hand of the LORD his God” was upon him? Why fast instead of asking for soldiers? Why were the foreign marriages a crisis, what law forbade them, and what happened to the wives and children? What does any of this mean for a Church that builds temples and keeps records?* A chapter answers those from the text first.

**The book's two threads.** Every source read divides it in the same place and reads it the same way. *Scripture Helps* (paragraphs 5–7) and the Bible Dictionary (Ezra, paragraphs 1 and 3): chapters 1–6, the first return and the house rebuilt; chapters 7–10, Ezra and the law. Running through both: **the Lord moves kings and people to restore what was lost** (1:1, 5; 5:5; 6:22; 7:6, 27–28; 8:18, 22, 31); the Bible Dictionary's first “religious value” is that “the promises of the Lord through His prophets shall all be fulfilled (Ezra 1:1…).” And **a restored community has to decide who belongs to it and on what terms**: the register of chapter 2, the refusal of 4:1–3, the separation of 6:21, the lists of chapter 8, the marriages of 9–10. Welch (BYU, Maxwell Institute, `welch-religious-authority`, paragraphs 9–11) reads the book as rebuilding “religious authority” by showing “God's protective hand” behind events and by showing worship resumed “in continuity with the pre-exilic past”; that is one Latter-day Saint reading, and it fits both threads. A chapter's `thread` says which of the two its chapter carries and how; it does not restate the whole book.

**The lists are not padding, and not explained name by name.** Chapter 2 (the returnees by family and town, then priests, Levites, singers, porters, Nethinims, Solomon's servants, the unregistered, the totals and the gifts), chapter 8:1–20 (the heads of families who came with Ezra, and the Levites he sent for), and chapter 10:18–44 (the men who had married foreign wives) each say something by being there. **Proposed:** each list gets at most two or three notes, on what the list *does*: chapter 2 shows the restored people by its old families and towns and its temple orders, keeps lineage as the test for priesthood (2:59–63), and counts 42,360 (2:64; Isaiah 49:20 and 60:22 on the site already use the figure: link); chapter 8 shows Ezra's company and his care to bring Levites (8:15–20); chapter 10 ends the book on the names of the guilty, priests first (10:18), which Ludlow reads as the bridge to Nehemiah's same problem (`ludlow-ezra-minute`, chapter 10). A name gets a note only where another book on the site already explains it (Anathoth, 2:23: `[[Jer. 11:23]]`; Immer, 2:37: `[[Jer. 20:1]]`; Asaph's sons, 2:41: `[[Ps. 74:0]]`) or a reader would stop (the Nethinims; Barzillai, 2:61).

### When: one chronology for all ten chapters

The guide [Persia and the Return](/old-testament/guides/persia-and-the-return/#rulers) carries the dates with their sources (its sections are `#map`, `#rulers`, `#nations`, `#books`). A chapter cites the verse and links `#rulers`; it does not retell the history.

- **Cyrus's decree** (1:1, “the first year of Cyrus”). *Scripture Helps* 1: Babylon fell in 539 and “About a year later” came the decree; the Bible Dictionary (Ezra) and the Bible Chronology (`bible-chron-ot`, 67) say 537; the Bible Dictionary's Captivities, 536. **Proposed:** “about 538 B.C.” (*Scripture Helps*, as Haggai's brief counts), and the guide for the rest.
- **Jeremiah's seventy years (1:1).** The site already counts them at `[[Jer. 25:11]]` and `[[Jer. 29:10]]` (from the fall of Jerusalem to the decree “is about fifty years”). The Church's manuals count from destruction to rebuilding: the Student Manual (29-22), the temple finished “exactly seventy years after the temple was destroyed by Nebuchadnezzar in 586 B.C.”; the institute manual (paragraph 21) the same. **Proposed:** chapter 1 links `[[Jer. 29:10]]` and does no arithmetic; chapter 6 may give the manuals' count at 6:15 in a sentence, as theirs.
- **Sheshbazzar and Zerubbabel.** Keep the guide's line: Sheshbazzar may be Zerubbabel's Persian name (Bible Dictionary, Zerubbabel: “also known by the Persian name Sheshbazzar”) or, likelier, a second person (Student Manual 29-6: “The evidence seems to favor the idea of two separate persons. Both were of the royal line of Judah”). Ludlow agrees with the manual (“the text does not seem to support this idea”); Van Dyke and Ogden (107, 109) make Sheshbazzar Zerubbabel's uncle. There is no Bible Dictionary entry for Sheshbazzar. **Proposed:** one note at 1:8 with both readings, weighted as the guide weights them; 5:14–16 points back. Zerubbabel himself is explained at `[[Hag. 1:1]]`, `[[Hag. 2:23]]` and `[[Jer. 22:30]]`: link at 2:2 or 3:2. Jeshua is Haggai's Joshua (Student Manual 29-8; `[[Zech. 3]]`).
- **Altar, foundation, stop, 520.** The altar in the seventh month (3:1, 6), the foundation in the second year (3:8–10); opposition “all the days of Cyrus” (4:5); “So it ceased unto the second year of the reign of Darius” (4:24), 520 B.C. (Bible Dictionary, Temple of Zerubbabel). Why: *Scripture Helps* 1, “conflict with the Samaritans delayed the work,” and endnote 10, some “lost interest” (Haggai 1:2–6). How long: give a count only as one source's (Student Manual 29-19: “fifteen to seventeen years”). Don't convert months to modern dates without a source.
- **The temple finished** “in the sixth year of the reign of Darius” (6:15): 516 (Bible Dictionary; Student Manual; Garner) or 515 (*Scripture Helps* 1, 12; Van Dyke and Ogden; Matson). Say “516 or 515 B.C.” as the guide does.
- **The gap between 6 and 7.** Bible Dictionary (Ezra): chapters 1–6 describe events “from 60 to 80 years before the arrival of Ezra,” and “There is no record in the book of any events between this date and the mission of Ezra.” Student Manual 29-24 (printing the *Eerdmans' Handbook*): “Nearly 60 years separate 7:1 from 6:22,” the years of Esther. **Proposed:** chapter 7's `setting` says so in two sentences and points to Esther.
- **“In the seventh year of Artaxerxes” (7:7).** Artaxerxes I, “Longimanus… king of Persia, 465–425 B.C.” (Bible Dictionary); Ezra's commission 458 (Bible Dictionary, Ezra; Bible Chronology, 78; the guide). Enrichment J (J-5, 35): “The date of Ezra's mission is disputed. Some scholars date Ezra's journey before that of Nehemiah, some after. The scriptures seem to indicate that Ezra's group came to Jerusalem before Nehemiah.” No Church or Latter-day Saint source read names Artaxerxes II. **Proposed:** 458 B.C. and Artaxerxes I throughout; chapter 7's setting says once, in a clause, that the date is disputed; nothing more on the order of Ezra and Nehemiah.
- **Ezra 4:6–23 stands out of order.** Cyrus (4:1–5), then Ahasuerus (4:6) and Artaxerxes (4:7–23), then Darius (4:24). The letters concern the city: “building the rebellious and the bad city, and have set up the walls thereof” (4:12); “that this city be not builded” (4:21). The Bible Dictionary takes the kings as the later ones: Ahasuerus of Ezra 4:6 is “probably the same as” Esther's, who is “certainly to be identified with Xerxes”; Artaxerxes Longimanus “hinders Jews from building (Ezra 4:7–23).” Keil (last): 4:24 “returns to the notice in Ezra 4:5,” and what lies between is “an illustrative episode” about the walls (`keil-ezra`, chapter 4, 78–80). The Bible Chronology's table pairs the names instead with Cambyses and Pseudo-Smerdis (69, 71), the view Keil assigns to “older expositors.” **Proposed:** follow the Bible Dictionary. Chapter 4's `setting` says verses 6–23 are later letters about the wall, set here because they show the same adversaries, and that verse 24 picks up verse 5; one History note at 4:6–7 gives the identifications and the text's evidence, and says in a clause that the Bible Chronology pairs the names differently. No note treats 4:23 as what stopped the temple.
- **Proposed `when`, by chapter:**
  - 1: `'The first year of Cyrus king of Persia (verse 1), about 538 B.C.'`
  - 2: `'The first return under Cyrus, about 538 B.C. The same list is given again in Nehemiah 7.'`
  - 3: `'The first and second years after the return under Cyrus (verses 1, 8).'`
  - 4: `'Verses 1–5 and 24 run from Cyrus to the second year of Darius I, 520 B.C. Verses 6–23 are letters from the later reigns of Ahasuerus (Xerxes) and Artaxerxes I, set here out of order.'`
  - 5: `'The second year of Darius I, 520 B.C. (Ezra 4:24; Haggai 1:1).'`
  - 6: `'From the second year of Darius I to the sixth, when the temple was finished (verse 15), 516 or 515 B.C.'`
  - 7: `'The seventh year of Artaxerxes I (verse 7), 458 B.C., about sixty years after chapter 6.'`
  - 8: `'The journey of 458 B.C., from the first month to the fifth (7:9; 8:31).'`
  - 9: `'Jerusalem, 458 B.C., between Ezra's arrival in the fifth month (7:9) and the assembly of the ninth (10:9).'`
  - 10: `'The ninth month of 458 B.C. to the first month of the next year (verses 9, 16–17).'`

### Languages

- **Aramaic:** 4:8–6:18 and 7:12–26 (Bible Dictionary, Aramaic: “An official language of the Persian Empire”); the footnote at 4:7 already says “Syrian” is “HEB Aramaic.” **Proposed:** chapter 4's setting says in a sentence where the Aramaic begins and why (the letters; `[[Dan. 2:4]]` has the same switch: link); chapters 6 and 7 say where it ends and that the king's letter is Aramaic. No note is about the language unless a word needs it.
- **Looking up an Aramaic word.** Tested: `hebrew.mjs ezra 4:8` and `5:1` work. In an Aramaic verse every morphology code begins with **A** (`ANcmsa`, `AVqp3mp`), in a Hebrew one with **H** (6:19: `HC/Vqw3mp`); the Strong's numbers are the Aramaic ones (4:8 “chancellor” H2942 *ṭĕʿēm*; 5:1 “God” H426 *ʾĕlāh*), and ketiv and qere are marked. The Bible Hub page (`biblehub.com/hebrew/<n>.htm`) has a Brown-Driver-Briggs section for the Aramaic word, but **its page title can be wrong** (2942 is headed “End, completion, perfection”; the entry says “(Aramaic)… judgment… decree”): quote the BDB paragraphs, never the title. The Persian loanword *dāt*, “law” (7:12–26), is already explained at `[[Deut. 33:2]]`: link.

### The book's make-up (only what a Latter-day Saint or Church source says)

- **One work with Nehemiah**, perhaps a sequel to Chronicles: Bible Dictionary, Chronicles (“originally forming one book in the Hebrew canon”); *Scripture Helps* 4. **2 Chronicles 36:22–23:** “Ezra 1:1–3 and 2 Chronicles 36:22–23 are almost identical” (Student Manual 29-2). Chapter 1's setting, once each.
- **Ezra's memoir.** Welch (4): the books “incorporate first-person memoir into anonymous third-person historiographical reports, royal correspondence, and official documents in Aramaic and Hebrew. (Look for Ezra's memoir in Ezra 7–10…).” The Student Manual (29-24): “Ezra's own memoirs, written in Hebrew, begin at verse 27.” The text is first person from 7:27–28 to 9:15, third person from 10:1 (Keil, chapter 10, 3, notes the change). **Proposed:** chapter 7 says where the “I” begins, chapter 10 that it ends; who wrote the frame is not known from any source read.
- **Ezra 2 and Nehemiah 7** (the footnote at 2:1). The Joseph Smith Translation changes Nehemiah 7, not Ezra 2: Nyman (`nyman-jst-historical`, 69), seventeen verses “concern the numbers of the various families who returned from Babylon. They are changed to conform to the parallel account given in Ezra.” One note in chapter 2.
- **1 Esdras** has “another account” of the return (Bible Dictionary, Apocrypha); not used, except as the Student Manual's evidence at 1:8 (29-6).
- **The Persian documents** (1:2–4; 4:11–22; 5:7–17; 6:3–12; 7:12–26). No Latter-day Saint source read argues their authenticity. **Proposed:** present them as the book does (“the copy of the letter,” 4:11; 5:6; 7:11); link `[[Dan. 4:1]]` for the form of a Persian edict (it cites Ezra 4:17 and 7:12) and `[[Isa. 45:1]]` and the guide's figure for the Cyrus Cylinder.

### Other conventions

- **“The hand of the LORD his God upon him”** (7:6, 9, 28; 8:18, 22, 31): Welch (10), “the benevolent hand of God working behind the scenes.” One note at 7:6; chapters 7–8 point back.
- **“Stirred up the spirit”** (1:1, 5): already `[[Hag. 1:14]]`. Link.
- **Nethinims.** The footnote at 2:43 already says “HEB servants of the temple (who attended the Levites in their sacred service)”; the Student Manual (29-9) adds “given” or “appointed”; 8:20 says David appointed them; Ludlow calls “Solomon's servants” (2:55) “probably another group of temple laborers descended from the Canaanites mentioned in 1 Kings 9:21.” One note at 2:43 if it says more than the footnote; no Bible Dictionary entry.
- **Tirshatha** (2:63): “The Persian title for a local or provincial governor; used of Zerubbabel (Ezra 2:63…)” (Bible Dictionary). `[[Mal. 1:8]]` has *peḥah*.
- **“Beyond the river”**: the footnotes gloss it each time. No note.
- **The refrain of 3:11** is already explained at `[[Ps. 136:1]]` and `[[Ps. 107:2]]`, both citing Ezra 3:11. Link one.
- **Theme candidates** (leads only; the coordinator keeps `THEME-CANDIDATES.md`, not yet created): the hand of the Lord on kings and on Ezra; belonging and the record (2:59–63; 6:21; 9–10; D&C 85); weeping and joy at the house of God (3:12–13; 6:22; 10:1). Several may belong more to Ezra–Nehemiah together.

**Habits to avoid** are listed in Amos's brief and hold here. In Ezra especially: **don't retell the return or the Persian kings** (the guide has them); **don't re-explain Cyrus, the vessels, Zerubbabel or the signet** (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Daniel and Haggai have them); **don't make every chapter a sermon on temples or on opposition**; and **don't read chapters 9–10 as a lesson on modern marriage or on descent**.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that ten chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-11-12.

- **Cyrus as the Lord's instrument (1:1–4).** The text: “the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus” (1:1), and Cyrus says the LORD “hath charged me to build him an house at Jerusalem” (1:2). *Scripture Helps* 8–9: his actions “fulfilled prophecies that Isaiah and Jeremiah made,” and “Though Cyrus was not a follower of Jehovah, the opening verse of Ezra points out that his actions were inspired.” The Bible Dictionary (Cyrus) gives a motive beside it: “possibly as a token of gratitude for assistance given to him in his conquest.” Josephus says Cyrus knew Isaiah's prophecy “by his reading the book which Isaiah left behind him” (*Antiquities* 11.1.2, `josephus-antiquities`, read at `penelope.uchicago.edu/josephus/ant-11.html`, paragraph 5; Enrichment J prints it). The site already has Cyrus named in advance (`[[Isa. 44:28]]`), the Cylinder and the “pagan messiah” (`[[Isa. 45:1]]`), “though thou hast not known me” (`[[Isa. 45:4]]`), and Ezra 1:2 beside the Cylinder (`[[Isa. 41:25]]`). **Proposed:** one note at 1:1–2: what Ezra claims (the Lord moved Cyrus, as Jeremiah and Isaiah said), Josephus's account once as a tradition, the Bible Dictionary's other motive in a clause, links for the rest; Benson's sentence at most once.
- **The weeping and the shouting (3:12–13).** *Scripture Helps* 12: the older generation wept, perhaps “because the new temple would not be as grand as the first.” The site already has the scene (`[[Hag. 2:3]]`, `[[Ps. 126:5]]`, the guide [Temple and Worship](/old-testament/guides/temple-and-worship/)). Ezra's own detail is 3:13: “the people could not discern the noise of the shout of joy from the noise of the weeping.” **Proposed:** one note at 3:12–13 on the two sounds, with *Scripture Helps*' reason in a clause and `[[Hag. 2:3]]` for what the Lord promised that house; don't retell Haggai 2.
- **The refusal of “the adversaries of Judah and Benjamin” (4:1–5).** The text calls them adversaries before they speak (4:1); they claim to have sacrificed to Israel's God “since the days of Esar-haddon king of Assur, which brought us up hither” (4:2); Zerubbabel's stated ground is the commission, “as king Cyrus the king of Persia hath commanded us” (4:3). *Scripture Helps* 11 names them Samaritans, “a mix of Israelites and foreign settlers,” whom the Jews “did not consider… to be pure Israelites or true worshippers of Jehovah. These factors likely influenced Zerubbabel”; its endnote 12: “The Savior took many opportunities during His ministry to break down cultural barriers between Jews and Samaritans.” Bible Dictionary, Samaritans: “partly Israelite and partly gentile. Their religion was also of a mixed character (see 2 Kgs. 17:24–41).” Matson (18): they “may have had a connection with the Samaritans.” `[[2 Kgs. 17]]`'s `explore` already points here. **Proposed:** one History note at 4:2–3: their claim (with `[[2 Kgs. 17:24]]`), the reason the Church's sources give (mixed worship), and Zerubbabel's own ground (Cyrus's command); it names them Samaritans as *Scripture Helps* does and says the text itself says “adversaries.” **Describe their worship as mixed, as the Bible Dictionary does; never call them a race** (the Student Manual's “a mixed race of Samaritans,” 29-15, is not used). A `christ` section may give endnote 12 in a sentence.
- **The priests without a register, the Urim and Thummim, and D&C 85 (2:59–63).** The text: priests who “sought their register… but they were not found: therefore were they, as polluted, put from the priesthood” (2:62), to wait “till there stood up a priest with Urim and with Thummim” (2:63). The footnotes already gloss the words and point to **`[[D&C 85:12]]`**: “it shall be done unto them as unto the children of the priest, as will be found recorded in the second chapter and sixty-first and second verses of Ezra” (Kirtland, November 27, 1832, “an extract from a letter of the Prophet to William W. Phelps”; the headnote). The letter in the Joseph Smith Papers (`jsp-letter-phelps-1832`, page 3) has the same words. The setting is Saints in Zion who had not received inheritances by consecration, whose names would not be “in the book of the law of God” (85:1–5, 7, 11). `[[Ex. 28:30]]` already explains the Urim and Thummim and quotes 2:63; the Bible Dictionary (Temple of Zerubbabel) lists it among five things the second temple lacked; `[[Ezek. 13:9]]` and `[[Isa. 66:21]]` cite 2:62. Ludlow (chapter 2): lineage then qualified a priest, but today “it is not required that someone be descended from a specific lineage to hold the priesthood… their authority is traced not through genealogy but through lines of authority.” **Proposed:** chapter 2 has a History note at 2:62–63 (the register; why the case waited for revelation; link `[[Ex. 28:30]]`; Ludlow's sentence) and a Restoration note on D&C 85:11–12 (the Prophet's own use of the verses, from the headnote and the Papers). **Not used:** the Student Manual's Joseph Fielding Smith quotation (29-10) that the passage concerns those who “had intermarried among peoples who were not entitled to the blessings of the priesthood.” The text is about a missing record (Barzillai's marriage in 2:61 is to an Israelite, a Gileadite), and the phrase reads as the kind of explanation the Church has disavowed (`topics-race`). A writer who thinks the chapter needs it reports it and does not write it.
- **Ezra himself (7:1–11).** *Scripture Helps* 15: “Ezra was both a scribe and a priest… From Ezra's day onward, scribes were also often teachers of the law.” Bible Dictionary, Scribe: “After the captivity we find the title given to Ezra (7:6, 21) and to others who acted as teachers of the law” (the guide [How It Came to Us](/old-testament/guides/how-it-came-to-us/) already says so). Garner (9): the word means “one who was eminently skilful in expounding the law.” His descent: `[[2 Kgs. 25:18]]` and `[[Num. 25:13]]` already read 7:1–5; link. 7:10 (the `heroQuote`): Elder Condie (1990), “one of the great teachers and prophet-leaders in Israel.” The traditions: Bible Dictionary, Ezra: “He is said to have formed the canon of Hebrew scripture and to have established… the Great Synagogue… But for none of these traditions is there trustworthy evidence”; Synagogue, the Great: “it is doubtful whether there ever existed such a body.” **Proposed:** notes at 7:6 (scribe and priest; the hand of the Lord) and 7:10 (seek, do, teach, in that order; Condie at most once); the traditions one sentence in a setting if needed, never a note. The book never calls Ezra a prophet: the site doesn't either in its own voice.
- **The fast at Ahava (8:21–23).** Ezra “was ashamed to require of the king a band of soldiers” because he had said “The hand of our God is upon all them for good that seek him” (8:22). One note from the text, tied to 7:6, 9, 28; the Student Manual (29-27, printing the *Eerdmans' Handbook*) as one voice at most.
- **The “strange wives” (chapters 9–10).** The hardest part of the book.
  - *The text.* The princes report that “the people of Israel, and the priests, and the Levites, have not separated themselves from the people of the lands, doing according to their abominations” (9:1), “so that the holy seed have mingled themselves with the people of those lands: yea, the hand of the princes and rulers hath been chief in this trespass” (9:2). Ezra's prayer (9:6–15) confesses in the first person plural (“our iniquities are increased over our head”). Shechaniah proposes “a covenant with our God to put away all the wives, and such as are born of them” (10:3). The people sit “trembling because of this matter, and for the great rain” (10:9) and ask for time (10:13); two men stand against it (10:15; the footnote: “HEB (probably) stood up against this”); a court sits three months (10:16–17); the book ends on the list and “some of them had wives by whom they had children” (10:44).
  - *The law.* The list in 9:1 joins the nations of `[[Deut. 7:1]]`–3 (“neither shalt thou make marriages with them”) with the Ammonite, Moabite and Egyptian of `[[Deut. 23:3]]`–8, and 9:12 uses the words of `[[Deut. 23:6]]` (*lead*: confirm with `check-quotes`). The site already explains the law: `[[Deut. 7]]` (setting and thread: Israel “an holy people”), `[[Ex. 34:15]]` (“Idolatry as unfaithfulness in marriage”), `[[Deut. 23:3]]` (set beside Ruth). Link; don't explain again.
  - *Why, in the sources.* *Scripture Helps* 7: “Ezra sorrowed when he learned that some of the Jews in Jerusalem had married out of the covenant. He counseled the people to repent, and they made a covenant to repent of their sins.” Student Manual 29-29: intermarriage “was expressly forbidden by the Lord because it led to idolatry (see Deuteronomy 7:1–5).” KnoWhy #110 (`sc-knowhy-110`, 2, 19), on the same law: Israel was forbidden to intermarry with Canaanites “lest they abandon their covenants with the Lord,” and “To be clear, this is not to say that interracial or intercultural marriage is inherently sinful or dangerous.” The text's own word for the danger is “abominations” (9:1, 11, 14), their worship.
  - *The women and children.* No source read says what became of them, and the Hebrew of 10:44 is difficult (*lead*: `hebrew.mjs ezra 10:44` and the NRSVUE, ESV, NIV, as renderings). Ludlow: the people “covenanted with God to expel the foreign women and their children.” Welch (BYU, Maxwell Institute, 2026; 7): “on one point, Ezra employs a means that is decidedly coercive… It appears that this decree is only partially carried out, but it remains a troubling anomaly in the otherwise effective and inspiring ministry of Ezra.”
  - *Ruth and Malachi.* Ruth 1's `setting` already says the book may come from after the exile, “when some held that Israel should separate itself from every foreign people; beside that belief, a faithful woman of Moab as David's great-grandmother teaches that faithfulness matters more than ethnicity” (with `[[Ruth 1:4]]` and `[[Deut. 23:3]]`). `[[Mal. 2:11]]` reads the same marriages as to women “who worship a foreign god” and cites Ezra 9:1–2; with the Bible Dictionary it separates “marriage outside the covenant” from “divorces from wives within the covenant” (`[[Mal. 2:14]]`, `[[Mal. 2:16]]`). `[[Isa. 56:3]]`: whether Isaiah 56 answers Ezra and Nehemiah “is debated.”
  - **Proposed.** (1) Chapter 9 explains the crisis as the text and the Church's sources do (a covenant people marrying into the worship of the peoples around it, the leaders first) and links the law. Its center is Ezra's prayer: he confesses the sin as his own, traces the exile to it (9:7), and calls the return a “little space” of “grace” and “a nail in his holy place” (9:8; `[[Ps. 13:3]]` already reads “lighten our eyes”; `[[Ps. 141:2]]` and `[[Dan. 9:21]]` have the evening sacrifice). (2) Chapter 10 tells what was done as the text tells it, with what the text shows of the cost (the weeping, the rain and trembling, the delay, the men who stood against it, the children), says plainly that no source tells what became of the women and children, gives the reason from *Scripture Helps* and the Student Manual, and gives **once Welch's judgment** (“decidedly coercive… a troubling anomaly”), worked into the sentence with its citation and labelled as one Latter-day Saint reader's judgment, not the site's verdict and without her name in the text (`STANDARDS.md` §3; the label is required here, and a language pass keeps it). The author is asked about this in `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` (E1) and may take the sentence out. (3) Ruth and Malachi are linked, not argued with: one sentence that scripture also keeps Ruth the Moabite in the line of David and of Christ (`[[Ruth 4:17]]`; `[[Matt. 1:5]]`), and `[[Mal. 2:11]]` for the same problem later; nothing that sets Malachi against Ezra. (4) **Not said:** nothing about race, blood or ethnicity (KnoWhy #110 is used only for the point that the law concerned covenant and worship); no “holy seed” as a racial idea; no application to marriage outside the faith today, since no Church source on these chapters states one (the footnotes' Topical Guide “Marriage, Interfaith” is the reader's to follow); no defense of the expulsion as kind and no condemnation of Ezra in the site's own voice. The `christ` sections of 9 and 10 rest on the confession and the covenant to repent (*Scripture Helps* 7), not on the separation.
- **Restoration connections that are real.**
  - **D&C 85:11–12** cites Ezra 2:61–62 by chapter and verse (above). Chapter 2.
  - **Opposition to the work.** The footnote at 4:21 gives **`[[D&C 124:49]]`**: when enemies hinder a commanded work, “it behooveth me to require that work no more at the hands of those sons of men, but to accept of their offerings.” **Proposed:** at most one Restoration note, at 4:24 or 5:1–2, setting D&C 124:49 beside what happened here (the work stopped, and through Haggai and Zechariah the Lord required it again), from the footnote and the text; read 124:49–51 first. No general lesson about modern opposition (*Come, Follow Me* 10 frames one as a question: not copied).
  - **Kirtland.** Ludlow: 1:4–6 echoes in `[[D&C 109:5]]`, and the Kirtland dedication fell “around Passover time” as 6:16–22 did. Ludlow's; a clause at most.
  - **Joseph Smith Translation:** no change in Ezra (the footnotes; Nyman, 5: “Ruth, Ezra, and Esther have no changes”).
  - **Book of Mormon:** no Ezra passage; **no `bom` note.** Ludlow sets the crowd around the praying Ezra (10:1) beside Nephi on his tower (Helaman 7): an `explore` line at most.
  - **General conference:** Condie (1990) on 7:10; Benson (1972) on Cyrus. No other use found.
- **Temple joy (3:10–13; 6:16–22).** *Come, Follow Me* (8) and the institute manual (25) draw “The house of the Lord is a place of joy” from these verses. Chapter 6's `christ` may say it in a statement from the text (“the LORD had made them joyful,” 6:22).
