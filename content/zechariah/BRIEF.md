## What a chapter is for here

Zechariah is 211 verses in fourteen chapters: dated visions and sermons in prose (1–8), then undated poetry and prophecy (9–14). A reader asks: *Who was Zechariah? Who is the angel explaining the visions, and what do the horns, the flying roll and the woman in the ephah mean? Why is the high priest in filthy garments, with Satan in the Lord's court? Who are the two olive trees, who is crowned, and who is the Branch? Why does the book change at chapter 9, and did Zechariah write it? How did the Gospels find Jesus here? What is the battle of 12–14?* A chapter answers those from the text first.

**Two halves, one hope.** The visions answer a small, discouraged community rebuilding a modest temple (4:10, “the day of small things”); chapters 9–14 look past it to the King, the rejected Shepherd, and the day the Lord comes to Jerusalem. Van Dyke and Ogden name the thread: “God’s feelings for the city of Jerusalem and her inhabitants in Zechariah’s day, in the meridian of time, and in the latter days,” giving hope “in at least three time periods.” A chapter's `setting` says which time its prophecy addresses first; its `christ` section says where the Church reads it fulfilled.

**Read the visions as visions.** Each has an image, the prophet's question (“What are these?”), and the angel's answer. Give the angel's answer first, then what the Church's sources and Latter-day Saint scholars add; don't allegorize every detail beyond what a source says. Where no source explains a detail (the colours of the horses, 1:8; the woman in the ephah, 5:7), say so briefly and record it in `UNANSWERED.md`.

## Divisions

**Proposed: four divisions, 1–6, 7–8, 9–11 and 12–14** (`book.yaml`: `zechvisions`, “Eight Visions in the Night”; `zechfasts`, “Fasts Turned to Feasts”; `zechshepherd`, “The King and the Shepherd”; `zechthatday`, “In That Day”). The Bible Dictionary and *Scripture Helps* both give two halves, 1–8 and 9–14; the Bible Dictionary's outline subdivides each where the book itself turns: the visions with the crowning (1:1–6:15) and the fasts (7–8), then 9–11 and 12–14. Keil's outline is the same four parts: the night visions with the symbolic crowning, the answer about the fast, “a burden … concerning the land of Hadrach” (9–11) and “a burden concerning Israel” (12–14), the two burdens “connected together by the common epithet massȧ'” (9:1; 12:1). The book page uses four because two halves of six and eight chapters hide the turns a reader meets. Chapter 1's setting says that 1:1–6, a call to repent, introduces the whole. The keys repeat no other book's.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest is the coordinator's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **When.** 1:1, “the eighth month, in the second year of Darius” (autumn 520 B.C.); 1:7, the visions, “the four and twentieth day of the eleventh month … in the second year of Darius” (early 519); 7:1, “the fourth year of king Darius … the fourth day of the ninth month, even in Chisleu” (late 518). Bible Dictionary: “prophesied from the second to fourth year of Darius Ⅰ (520–518 B.C.)”; *Scripture Helps*: “from about 520 to 518 BC.” Van Dyke and Ogden: Darius “(son of Hystaspes) took the throne and was securely in power by 520 BC,” and “Zechariah’s revelation came about two months after Haggai’s.” **Proposed `when`:** chapters 1–8, “Zechariah prophesied in Jerusalem under Darius I of Persia, 520–518 B.C.” (ledger row from the Bible Dictionary); chapters 9–14, which carry no date, “Undated; the book sets these prophecies with Zechariah, who prophesied 520–518 B.C.” A month-and-day equivalent (Correa gives modern dates) is used only from a source, and Correa's “February 15, 529 BC” at 1:7 is a misprint for 519: don't copy it. The temple was finished in 515 (Van Dyke and Ogden; `[[Ezra 6:15]]`). Darius is not “Darius the Median” (the site's `[[Dan. 5:31]]`).
- **Who.** “The son of Berechiah, the son of Iddo the prophet” (1:1); Iddo was among the priests who came up with Zerubbabel (`[[Neh. 12:4]]`; Zechariah is head of Iddo's house in `[[Neh. 12:16]]`); Ezra calls him “the son of Iddo” (`[[Ezra 5:1]]`). *Scripture Helps*: “the son of the priest Iddo.” The name: Correa (Scripture Central) and the Interpreter study aid, “Jehovah remembers” / “The Lord remembers.” **Not the Zacharias of Matthew 23:35:** the Student Manual (33-2) says Joseph Smith taught they are different men; Wilson (RSC) reads Matthew's “son of Barachias” as a confusion of the prophet with “Zechariah son of Jehoiada” (`[[2 Chr. 24:20–21]]`). The note at 1:1 says only that the prophet is not the martyr and takes no side on who the martyr was (author, 2026-10-24, ZC1: “B”), since the Student Manual attributes the other identification, John the Baptist's father, to Joseph Smith.
- **Haggai, Zerubbabel and Joshua.** Haggai's chapters introduce Zerubbabel (governor, of David's line) and Joshua (high priest, son of Josedech) and the stalled rebuilding (*Scripture Helps*, paragraph 7, under Haggai 1:1–2; Van Dyke and Ogden on why the work stopped). Zechariah's chapters link `[[Hag. 1:1]]` and `[[Hag. 2:23]]` (the signet) and add only what is Zechariah's: Joshua cleansed (3), Zerubbabel's hands finishing the house “not by might, nor by power” (4:6–10), the crowns (6:9–15). Ezra 3–6 is in the volume guide: link, don't retell.
- **The interpreting angel.** Two figures, kept apart: “the angel that talked with me” (1:9, 13, 14, 19; 2:3; 4:1, 4–5; 5:5, 10; 6:4), who explains, and “the angel of the LORD” (1:11–12; 3:1, 5–6), who intercedes (1:12) and presides at Joshua's trial. Correa: “In apocalyptic fashion, a messenger aided the prophet in understanding the meaning of the visions,” and “interpretation and understanding come with divine help (Moses 1:30; 7:67).” KnoWhy #471 calls Zechariah among the texts “generally considered to be the forerunners of fully developed apocalyptic literature”; #492 (on Nephi's and Lehi's angelic escorts) points to Zechariah 1–6 (leads: read before use). **Proposed:** one note at 1:9 explains the guide once; later chapters say “the interpreting angel” and point back. Daniel's guide Gabriel (`[[Dan. 8:16]]`) is the nearest parallel on the site.
- **The visions, one note each, at the vision's first verse.** The Bible Dictionary's outline gives a reading of each (horsemen reporting on the nations; horns that scattered Israel and craftsmen who destroy them; the city that “shall be immeasurable”; Joshua; the lampstand and oil, “the symbol of the Spirit”; the curse on sin; sin removed “to Shinar”; the chariots, “agencies by which God shall subdue the nations”): it is the Church's reading, and a note may start from it. The Student Manual (33-4 to 33-23) adds older commentaries (Dummelow; Guthrie and Motyer); Correa adds the ancient Near Eastern horned crown (1:18) and the carpenters as possibly “the temple builders.” Mark readings as readings.
- **The BRANCH (3:8; 6:12).** *Scripture Helps* (15–16): Jeremiah's “righteous Branch” is “a reference to the Messiah,” and Zechariah “said that Joshua and his fellow priests were symbolic of the coming Branch”; its endnote 27 says the Hebrew behind “men wondered at” “can also mean ‘sign.’” The Interpreter study aid: “‘the BRANCH’ is Jesus Christ.” Bowen (RSC, “Ominous Onomastics”) reads Zechariah taking up Jeremiah's prophecy “as a discreet way of prophesying of a Davidic” ruler (read the paragraph before use). **The site's `[[Isa. 4:2]]` note has the title's history**: link it; chapter 3 explains the Branch once (at 3:8), chapter 6 points back and adds what 6:12–13 adds, a priest on a throne.
- **Verse numbers.** **The Hebrew chapters differ in chapters 1 and 2:** King James 1:18–21 is Hebrew 2:1–4, and King James 2:1–13 is Hebrew 2:5–17 (`hebrew.mjs` prints both). A note quoting a translation or lexicon that numbers by the Hebrew gives the King James number.
- **The Joseph Smith Translation.** Two changes, both in chapter 8: at 8:7 and 8:13 “save” becomes “gather” (footnotes 7a and 13b; the Interpreter study aid names 8:7). A Language note at 8:7 says what the change does (the promise to bring the people from east and west is a gathering), once; 8:13 points back. No other chapter has a JST footnote.
- **The Hebrew where it pays.** “Satan” at 3:1 is *haśśāṭān*, with the article, and “to resist him” is the verb from the same root (`hebrew.mjs zech 3:1`: H7854, H7853; the footnote: “HEB the Adversary or Accuser”; Correa: “not a name but a title with the article *the* before it”). “Pierced” at 12:10 is *dāqar* (H1856), **the same verb as “thrust him through” at 13:3** (`hebrew.mjs zech 13:3`). **The “wounds” and “wounded” of 13:6 are other words** (*makkôt*, H4347; *nākāh*, H5221): Correa's statement that 13:6 uses “the same root translated as ‘pierce’ in Zechariah 12:10” is mistaken, and is not repeated. “Burden” (9:1; 12:1) is *maśśāʾ*, an oracle (Correa; Keil).
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports what runs across the book; the coordinator adds it to `THEME-CANDIDATES.md` (not yet created). Leads from the sources, not choices: the Lord's return to Jerusalem (1:16; 2:10; 8:3; 14:4), which Van Dyke and Ogden trace through three times; the shepherds (10:2–3; 11; 13:7); cleansing (3:4; 5; 13:1); holiness (2:12; 8:3; 14:20–21, *Come, Follow Me*'s title).

**Habits to avoid** are listed in Amos's brief and hold here. In Zechariah especially: **don't explain the Matthew passages again** where the site has them (above); **don't read every vision as a last-days timetable** (the Student Manual reads chapter 11 as Armageddon: 33-37); and **don't describe the slaughter of 14:12 beyond what the text says** (`STANDARDS.md` §3, graphic passages).

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that fourteen chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-23.

- **Who wrote chapters 9–14? (“Second Zechariah”; chapter 9's setting).** The text changes at 9:1: no date, no prophet's name, a heading “The burden of the word of the LORD,” and poetry. Keil names the difference (“the headings contain neither notices as to the time, nor the prophet's name”) and defends one author: the parts “hang closely together,”, and he blames the objection on “the dogmatic assumption of the rationalistic and naturalistic critics.” Correa (Scripture Central), at 9:13 (“thy sons, O Greece”): “A clear indication that this is either a later text or was updated to reflect a future time is the reference to Greece,” and the passage “may reflect the desires of the people during the Maccabean Revolt.” KnoWhy #218 mentions the view that a later editor may have “modified the books of Haggai and Zechariah” to lead into Malachi. The Church's sources speak of the whole as Zechariah's: the Bible Dictionary (9–14, “prophecies about the Messiah and events preceding His Second Coming”) and *Scripture Helps* (“The Lord revealed through Zechariah many prophecies about the Messiah”). And the Lord Himself takes up 13:6 and 14:4 as prophecy of His coming (`[[D&C 45:48–52]]`). **Proposed** (Isaiah's model, the `setting` of Isaiah 40, and Micah's answer for its own book): chapter 9's setting describes the change (no date or name, a “burden,” poetry, a wider horizon), says in one or two sentences that many modern scholars assign chapters 9–14 to a later prophet or prophets, and that others, Keil among them, hold one author; that the Church's helps read these chapters as Zechariah's prophecies of Christ and the last days, and that for Latter-day Saints the Lord's use of them in Doctrine and Covenants 45 settles that they are prophecy, whoever wrote them down. The scholars' view is stated only from a source read (Correa, as his, if no better). No chapter assigns verses to a later hand. At 9:13 a note explains “Greece” (*yāwān*, Javan); Correa's reading is given as his, once, beside what the text and the site's notes show of Javan before Zechariah (`[[Gen. 10:2]]`, `[[Isa. 66:19]]`, `[[Ezek. 27:13]]`, `[[Joel 3:6]]`). `periods:` stays `persia` for all fourteen chapters: the book places them with Zechariah, and no source read dates them to a different period with any agreement.
- **Joshua, Satan, and the filthy garments (3:1–7).** A court: “the satan” accuses, the angel of the Lord presides, the Lord rebukes the accuser, and Joshua's filthy garments are replaced. The Bible Dictionary: the people “are comforted with the assurance that His favor and forgiveness are with them,” and the ministries among them “are but types of a more perfect one,” the Branch. KnoWhy #832 reads a divine council, the accuser “similar to a prosecuting attorney.” Van Dyke and Ogden: Joshua “sloughs off the filth of the world … dons clean clothing,” a change that can come “in one day.” The Student Manual (33-13): Joshua's name is Jesus' name, and “Joshua … was a type of Jesus Christ, the great High Priest”. **Proposed:** the site's notes on `[[Num. 22:22]]` and `[[Ps. 109:6]]` already discuss *the satan* as a title: chapter 3 links them and says what Zechariah's scene adds (the Lord's rebuke, 3:2; “a brand plucked out of the fire”). The note gives the Hebrew title and does not argue that the accuser is someone other than Satan (the Bible Dictionary: “see also Job 1:6–11”). The clean garments: what the text says (3:4), then the type of Christ's cleansing, as the Bible Dictionary reads it.
- **The candlestick and the two olive trees (4); Revelation 11.** The Bible Dictionary: the oil “is the symbol of the Spirit,” dispensed through “the two anointed ones (‘sons of oil’), representing the priestly and royal rule (Joshua and Zerubbabel).” The Student Manual (33-18) gives the same (citing McConkie) and notes “The same imagery was used by John in Revelation 11:4.” D&C 77:15: Revelation's witnesses are “two prophets that are to be raised up to the Jewish nation in the last days.” **Proposed:** chapter 4 reads the trees as the text's angel does (“the two anointed ones,” 4:14) and as the Bible Dictionary does (Joshua and Zerubbabel), with 4:6 as the chapter's centre; one Related Scriptures note at 4:14 says John borrowed the image for the two witnesses (`[[Rev. 11:3–4]]`; `[[D&C 77:15]]`) and does not make Zechariah's trees the witnesses. 4:6 is the hero quotation and a `beloved` verse; the site's Wilson (`wilson-holy-spirit`) cites it for the Spirit's power: check and link.
- **The crowns (6:9–15).** “Crowns” (plural) of the exiles' silver and gold set on Joshua's head. The Bible Dictionary: a “symbolic action to teach that the Branch (Messiah), who shall truly build the temple of the Lord, shall be a Crowned Priest.” Parry (`parry-symbolic-action`): “replete with Christ-centered symbolism.” Correa notes that “many in Zechariah’s day may have hoped for a branch fulfillment in Joshua.” A reader asks why the priest is crowned and not Zerubbabel, the king's heir. **Proposed:** say what the text does (the crown on the priest; the Branch who will build; “the counsel of peace … between them both,” 6:13), give the Church's reading (priest and king in one, the Messiah), and answer the reader's question only from a source that addresses it (Keil or the Student Manual; if none, record it in `UNANSWERED.md`). A Witness of Christ note at 6:12–13.
- **The fasts (7–8).** Bethel asks whether to keep the fast of the fifth month (7:3); the Lord asks “did ye at all fast unto me?” (7:5) and turns four fasts into feasts (8:19). The Bible Dictionary: “the Lord is indifferent whether men fast or eat; He desires that they execute judgment and show mercy (7:8–10).” The Student Manual (33-26) ties the four to the siege (tenth month), the breach of the city (fourth), the temple burned (fifth) and Gedaliah's murder (seventh), but calls them “feasts” and Gedaliah “the puppet king”: take the months and their scriptures (`[[Jer. 39:1–2]]`; `[[Jer. 52:12–14]]`; `[[2 Kgs. 25:25]]`), not its wording (Gedaliah was governor, `[[2 Kgs. 25:22]]`). **Proposed:** the site's Isaiah 58 setting and `[[Isa. 58:3]]` already use 7:5, and `[[Ezek. 24:2]]` the tenth month: link. Chapter 7 does not say the Lord was indifferent to fasting as such (the Bible Dictionary's wording is strong: give it as the Bible Dictionary's, or say the plainer thing the text says: fasting without justice is not fasting to Him).
- **The Messianic passages and their fulfilment (9:9; 9:11; 11:12–13; 12:10; 13:7).** *Come, Follow Me* lists them with their fulfilments: 9:9–11 with `[[Matt. 21:1–11]]` and `[[1 Pet. 3:18–19]]`; 11:12–13 with `[[Matt. 26:14–16]]` and `[[Matt. 27:1–7]]`; 12:10 with `[[John 19:37]]` and `[[Rev. 1:7]]`; 13:6–7 and 14:1–9 with `[[Matt. 26:31]]` and `[[D&C 45:47–53]]`. *Scripture Helps* endnote 22 adds 13:7 with `[[Mark 14:27]]`. **Proposed:** each gets one Witness of Christ note at its verse, giving the verse in its own setting first and then the fulfilment, in the Church's reading:
  - **9:9.** *Scripture Helps*: Jesus “fulfilled this prophecy with His triumphal entry.” The donkey was royal, not merely humble: Elder Soares, quoting Talmage, an “ancient symbol of Jewish royalty”; Correa, “The donkey was a sign of kingship.” Matthew's two animals: Huntsman and Hatch say Zechariah's parallelism means “only one donkey,” and that Matthew “nonetheless chose to portray two”; Frederick (endnote 21) gives the debate. Say it once, as theirs.
  - **9:11–12, “prisoners of hope.”** *Come, Follow Me* pairs 9:11 with 1 Peter 3:18–19 (Christ preaching to the spirits in prison); the Student Manual (33-32) prints Elder McConkie reading “the pit wherein is no water” of the spirit world, and the Interpreter study aid lists 9:11–12 under “Those in the world of spirits.” A Restoration reading worth a note (`[[D&C 138]]` may be linked if a source does), given after the verse's plain sense (captives freed “by the blood of thy covenant”).
  - **11:4–17, the shepherd and the thirty pieces.** *Scripture Helps*: an allegory in which Zechariah is “a shepherd who is ultimately rejected by his flock”; the broken staves are “the broken covenant” and the breakdown of unity; thirty pieces are “the price of a slave” (`[[Ex. 21:32]]`, already noted), and “goodly price” “is apparently sarcasm” (endnote 34); it “points to the Good Shepherd.” Correa doubts the slave price and gives a Sumerian idiom for little value: as his, if at all. The Matthew–Jeremiah question is Jeremiah 19's: link it.
  - **12:10.** John 19:37 quotes it at the cross; Revelation 1:7 at His coming. The site's Ezekiel 39 `christ` and the *New Heart* theme page carry the outpouring; chapter 12's note adds “the spirit of grace and of supplications” and the mourning (12:10–14), with the Student Manual (33-44) printing President Joseph Fielding Smith on the Jews' mourning when they accept Him: as his, once.
  - **13:7.** Jesus quotes it the night of His arrest (`[[Matt. 26:31]]`; `[[Mark 14:27]]`); Huntsman (RSC) reads His use as making the saying “almost certainly a prediction.” The Student Manual (33-46) prints Keil on the regathering of the disciples after the Resurrection. In context the sword falls on the Lord's own “fellow,” and a refined third remains (13:8–9).
- **Chapters 12–14 and the last days (Armageddon; the Mount of Olives; D&C 45).** *Scripture Helps* (22–26): a final battle “in and around Jerusalem in the last days, just prior to the Second Coming,” “often referred to as Armageddon”; the Mount of Olives splits and the people “flee through the newly formed valley”; the deliverance “will prepare the Jews to recognize and accept Jesus Christ as the Messiah” (D&C 45); “The Savior will stand on the Mount of Olives prior to His appearance to all the world.” The institute manual pairs 10:5–9, 12 with `[[D&C 45:66–71]]`, 12:10 and 13:6 with `[[D&C 45:51–52]]`, and 12:8–9 and 14:4–5 together. **Proposed:** these chapters are read as the Church reads them, prophecy of the last days, with *Scripture Helps* as the frame; Joel's note on Armageddon and Ezekiel's on the earthquake are linked, not repeated; the Bible Dictionary's “Armageddon” and the Student Manual's Enrichment I are older voices, the latter in no more than a note. The order of events (*Scripture Helps*: the appearance at the Mount of Olives before the appearance to all the world, `[[D&C 45:44]]`, `[[D&C 133:19–25]]`) is given once, in chapter 14's setting. 14:16–19, the nations keeping the Feast of Tabernacles: Gardner (RSC) says Zechariah 14 was read at the feast's lamp-lighting, with its light (14:6–7) and “all the nations”; the Student Manual (33-48) on non-Israelites surviving: as its reading.
- **13:6, “What are these wounds in thine hands?” (D&C 45:51–52).** In its context (13:2–6) the verse describes a false prophet, ashamed in that day, who denies his calling and explains his marks: Frederick (RSC): “D&C 45 … expand[s] upon Zechariah 13:6 and 14:4 by maintaining the language but drastically shifting the context so that Zechariah’s words have a new application and meaning”; KnoWhy #790: originally “part of a larger prophecy referring to the end of false prophets,” “the Lord adapts the language to fit a new context,” so that “wounded in the house of my friends” refers to Jesus Christ, “mistakenly identified as a false prophet during His mortal ministry.” *Scripture Helps* (24) reads the verse with 12:10 as the prophecy the Lord expands in D&C 45; the Student Manual and Enrichment I read it as Christ outright. **Proposed** (the one question in this brief that the coordinator may put to the author): chapter 13's note gives the verse's setting in 13:2–6 plainly, then the Lord's own application in D&C 45:51–52 as the reading that matters most for a Latter-day Saint, from KnoWhy #790 and Frederick; the `christ` section rests on the Lord's words. Neither half is left out, and the Lord's use is never called a misreading. The site's `[[Isa. 25:9]]` already quotes D&C 45:51–52: link.
- **14:20–21, “Holiness unto the Lord.”** *Scripture Helps* (27) prints Elder Christofferson (“just how pervasive and constant our focus on holiness needs to be”), and the lesson takes its title from the verse. The site's `[[Isa. 23:18]]` already explains the inscription and quotes 14:20–21: link; chapter 14's note adds the high priest's plate (`[[Ex. 28:36]]`) and the inscription on the Church's temples, from a source that says so, once.
