The first word of the Lord in Zechariah is a promise with two halves: “Turn ye unto me, saith the LORD of hosts, and I will turn unto you, saith the LORD of hosts” ([[Zech. 1:3]]). One Hebrew verb, *šûb*, stands in both halves. It is a command to the people and then a promise in the Lord's own voice.[@oshb-wlc] The same sentence in the Lord's own voice, “unto me … unto you” with the same verb on both sides, stands in one other place in the Old Testament, in Malachi: “Return unto me, and I will return unto you, saith the LORD of hosts” ([[Mal. 3:7]]).[@oshb-wlc]

As a sentence, its order is plain. The people turn, and the Lord answers.

That is the order the promise states. The book tells more than the promise does. Three months after the promise, the Lord says “I am returned,” while the people's turning has begun and is not yet complete, and He gives His own jealous love as the reason. In the later chapters His verb changes, and the turning He asked of them becomes something He does to them. At the end, a call He once refused to hear is answered. I read Zechariah as showing the Lord, whose turning was offered on a condition, turning before His people's turning is complete, on the ground of His own love rather than of a finished repentance. And when His Shepherd is smitten in chapter 13, it is this same Lord who turns His hand back to the scattered little ones.

<h2 id="turn-ye-unto-me">“Turn ye unto me, and I will turn unto you”</h2>

In 1:3 the people's verb is an imperative and the Lord's is an imperfect, “I will turn.”[@oshb-wlc] The second half waits on the first. (Chapter 1's note on [1:3](/zechariah/1/) follows the verb through the chapter.)

The next verses say why the condition matters. The hearers' fathers had been given the same command and refused it: “Turn ye now from your evil ways, and from your evil doings: but they did not hear, nor hearken unto me, saith the LORD” ([[Zech. 1:4]]). Then the Lord asks whether His words did not overtake them, “and they returned and said, Like as the LORD of hosts thought to do unto us, according to our ways, and according to our doings, so hath he dealt with us” ([[Zech. 1:6]]).

Those who “returned” in 1:6 are the fathers, and the verb is the *šûb* of 1:3 and 1:4. The sense is “turned,” as at 1:3 and 1:4: they were brought at last to do what they had refused to do.[@cambridge-zechariah][@oshb-wlc] Their turning came after the judgment, and it took the form of a confession. The prophets' threats reached them, “so that they were obliged to confess that God had really done to them what He threatened.”[@keil-zechariah][@ot-manual-33] The hearers in 1:3 are asked to turn under a promise, before any such judgment falls on them.

Hezekiah's letters and Deuteronomy keep the same order. Hezekiah's letters said, “turn again unto the LORD … and he will return to the remnant of you” ([[2 Chr. 30:6]]). Deuteronomy sets the Lord's return second: “And shalt return unto the LORD thy God … That then the LORD thy God will turn thy captivity, and have compassion upon thee, and will return and gather thee” ([[Deut. 30:2–3]]). Zechariah 1:3 has been read by that rule, “God promises his favour on their repentance,” with Malachi 3:7 and Hezekiah's letters set beside it.[@pulpit-zechariah] Whatever the book goes on to do, the promise itself is a condition.

<h2 id="i-am-returned">“I am returned to Jerusalem with mercies”</h2>

The promise was given “in the eighth month” ([[Zech. 1:1]]). The night visions came “upon the four and twentieth day of the eleventh month” ([[Zech. 1:7]]), three months later.[@cambridge-zechariah] In the first vision the riders report that the earth is at rest, and the angel of the LORD asks a question: “O LORD of hosts, how long wilt thou not have mercy on Jerusalem and on the cities of Judah, against which thou hast had indignation these threescore and ten years?” ([[Zech. 1:12]]). “And the LORD answered the angel that talked with me with good words and comfortable words” ([[Zech. 1:13]]).

The answer is given in full: “I am jealous for Jerusalem and for Zion with a great jealousy. And I am very sore displeased with the heathen that are at ease … Therefore thus saith the LORD; I am returned to Jerusalem with mercies: my house shall be built in it” ([[Zech. 1:14–16]]).

The word to weigh is “Therefore.” The commentators agree on what it rests on, and it is not the people's turning. It is the Lord's own jealousy for Jerusalem and His anger at the nations, the two verses just before it: “because Jehovah is jealous with love for His people, and very angry with the heathen, He has now turned with compassion towards Jerusalem.”[@keil-zechariah][@cambridge-zechariah][@pulpit-zechariah] The return is also read as made “according to the promise in ver. 3.”[@pulpit-zechariah] The verb is a perfect, *šabtî*.[@oshb-wlc] It is not only a prophecy of what will be. It describes the return “as having already commenced, and as still continuing.”[@keil-zechariah] “‘I am returned,’ God says, although the effects of His return were yet to come.”[@barnes-zechariah]

The people had begun to turn, and the sources say so plainly. Under Haggai they had taken up the temple again. The visions come “on the basis of the repentance, of which the people were giving practical proof in their honest endeavours to rebuild the Temple.”[@cambridge-zechariah] “In some degree they had turned to God,” but there was need of “a more inward, more complete turning.”[@barnes-zechariah] Both sides stand in one sentence: the people's return “was not a perfect one, not a thorough conversion of heart,” and yet the Lord “had also turned to the people again.”[@keil-zechariah] So the Lord did not turn before His people had begun. He turned before their turning was complete.

That is what 1:3 lets a reader see in 1:16. 1:16 can be read as the promise of 1:3 kept on a repentance already begun.[@pulpit-zechariah][@cambridge-zechariah] I take the verse to go beyond the condition, because of the ground it states and the tense it uses. The promise made the Lord's turning depend on the people's. Three months later their turning is not yet complete. The Lord answers a plea for mercy, gives His own jealous love as the reason, not a finished repentance, and says that His half is already under way.

The words come back in chapter 8: “I am returned unto Zion, and will dwell in the midst of Jerusalem” ([[Zech. 8:3]]), the same perfect.[@oshb-wlc] A few verses later the verb appears again where the English hides it: “As I thought to punish you, when your fathers provoked me to wrath, saith the LORD of hosts, and I repented not: So again have I thought in these days to do well unto Jerusalem” ([[Zech. 8:14–15]]). The King James “again” is *šabtî*, “I have turned,” followed by *zāmamtî*, “I have purposed.” That is the verb of 8:14's “I thought to punish.”[@oshb-wlc] In 8:15, then, the Lord turns from purposing harm to purposing good. The change has been tied to the people's diligence in the work of restoration,[@pulpit-zechariah] and the text gives it in His voice: He has turned.

<h2 id="i-will-bring-them-again">“I will bring them again”</h2>

From chapter 9 on, the Lord's turning takes another form. The first half of the book uses the simple stem: “I will turn,” “I am returned.” Now He uses the causative stem, “I will cause to return.”[@oshb-wlc] Verse 9:12 sets both halves side by side once more: “Turn you to the strong hold, ye prisoners of hope: even to day do I declare that I will render double unto thee” ([[Zech. 9:12]]). The people's verb is the imperative of 1:3. The Lord's is the causative: He will bring back double.[@oshb-wlc] Then the object of the verb is the people themselves: “I will bring them again also out of the land of Egypt, and gather them out of Assyria” ([[Zech. 10:10]]). And at 13:7 the object is His hand: “I will turn mine hand upon the little ones” ([[Zech. 13:7]]).[@oshb-wlc]

So the Lord is the subject of this verb seven times in the book. He turns in the simple stem at 1:3, 1:16, 8:3 and 8:15, and in the causative at 9:12, 10:10 and 13:7.[@oshb-wlc] I leave 10:6 out of that count. Its “I will bring them again to place them” is one Hebrew word, tagged as *šûb* in the Hebrew text but listed by the lexicon under *yāšab*, “dwell,” as “probably text error”; one reading is “I make them dwell,” and on another the King James “unwarrantably combines both.”[@oshb-wlc][@bdb][@keil-zechariah][@pulpit-zechariah]

The rest of 10:6 is not in dispute: “for I have mercy upon them: and they shall be as though I had not cast them off: for I am the LORD their God, and will hear them” ([[Zech. 10:6]]). “I have mercy” is *riḥamtîm*, the verb of the angel's plea in 1:12, “how long wilt thou not have mercy.”[@oshb-wlc][@bdb] The plea of the first vision is answered in the Lord's own words.

The people still turn. Scattered, “they shall remember me in far countries; and they shall live with their children, and turn again” ([[Zech. 10:9]]). The Church's footnote there sends the reader to Deuteronomy 30:2.[@lds-scriptures] But their turning now stands inside His work. He sows them, they remember Him, and He brings them home. The turning commanded in 1:3 has become something the Lord does to them. The causative is also the form of the prayer that closes Lamentations, “Turn thou us unto thee, O LORD, and we shall be turned” ([[Lam. 5:21]]),[@oshb-wlc] which Lamentations' [“Three Things Asked of the Lord”](/lamentations/themes/asked-of-the-lord/) follows.

<h2 id="i-will-hear-them">“They shall call on my name, and I will hear them”</h2>

In chapter 7 stands the book's one refusal. Of the fathers who would not listen to the former prophets, the Lord says: “as he cried, and they would not hear; so they cried, and I would not hear, saith the LORD of hosts” ([[Zech. 7:13]]). The second half is in the imperfect: “so they shall cry, and I will not hear.”[@pulpit-zechariah][@keil-zechariah] It reads as a divine talio, a penalty that matches the offence. They did not hear Him, so He will not hear them when they call in distress.[@keil-zechariah]

The book ends this account with the opposite sentence. Of the third part brought through the fire, the Lord says: “they shall call on my name, and I will hear them: I will say, It is my people: and they shall say, The LORD is my God” ([[Zech. 13:9]]).

I read 13:9 as the reversal of 7:13. No source I have read joins the two, and the Hebrew supports the join only in part. The verb for calling is the same in both, *qārāʾ*. The verb for hearing is not. In 7:13 it is *šāmaʿ*, “hear.” In 13:9 it is *ʿānâ*, “answer.”[@oshb-wlc] The Church's footnote gives that sense at 10:6, where the same verb stands: “OR answer.”[@lds-scriptures] But the difference helps the reading more than it hurts it. *ʿĀnâ* is the verb of 1:13, “the LORD answered the angel.” It is the verb of 10:6, “and will hear them,” and of 13:9.[@oshb-wlc] The Lord's answering runs through the book, from the first vision to the refined third. The one time He will not hear is 7:13, and at the end those who call are answered.

The exchange at the end of 13:9 is Hosea's: “I will say to them which were not my people, Thou art my people; and they shall say, Thou art my God” ([[Hos. 2:23]]). The Church's footnotes join the two verses in both directions, and the reader of 13:9 is sent to Hosea for “the thought of the whole verse.”[@lds-scriptures][@keil-zechariah] The order matters here too. “I will bring the third part through the fire, and will refine them” comes before “they shall call.” The Lord acts, they call, and He answers.

<h2 id="i-will-go-before-you">“After I am risen again, I will go before you”</h2>

The Lord who speaks in Zechariah is Jehovah, and “Jesus Christ and Jehovah, the Lord God of the Old Testament, are, in fact, the same person.”[@olmstead-saviors-use] The Church reads chapters 9–14 as prophecy of His ministry and His coming.[@scripture-helps-hag-1-2] The Shepherd of 13:7 is Jesus Christ.[@ot-manual-33] On the night He was taken, He applied the verse to Himself and His disciples: “All ye shall be offended because of me this night: for it is written, I will smite the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock shall be scattered abroad. But after I am risen again, I will go before you into Galilee” ([[Matt. 26:31–32]]). (The sword's verse has its own page, [“Awake, O Sword, against My Shepherd”](/zechariah/themes/awake-o-sword/), and chapter 13's notes on [13:7](/zechariah/13/).)

The last clause of 13:7 can be read in a good sense: the Lord would again make the little ones “the object of his active care.”[@keil-zechariah] That reading reaches the night in Gethsemane: “The offending of the disciples took place when Jesus was taken prisoner, and they all fled … But the Lord soon brought back His hand over the disciples. The promise, ‘But after my resurrection I will go before you into Galilee,’ is a practical exposition of the bringing back of the hand over the small ones.”[@ot-manual-33] Verses 13:8–9 are read as showing the same turning of the hand in the remnant of the nation.[@keil-zechariah]

Read beside 1:3 and 1:16, the night of 13:7 shows the book's whole order in a few hours. I take the smitten Shepherd to be the Lord of the opening promise, turning before His people's turning is complete. He gave the promise of Galilee before the disciples fled, not after they came back. The little ones scattered from Him are gathered by a turning He began before theirs. The Lord who said “I am returned” while Jerusalem's turning was not yet complete goes before His disciples while they are still offended.

Something near this order is said of the Lord's jealousy in 1:14. God pursues the sinner in love, “that he may yet be converted and live. But for God's love to him and the solicitations of His grace, while yet impenitent and displeasing Him, he could not turn and please Him.”[@barnes-zechariah]

<h2 id="draw-near">“Draw near unto me and I will draw near unto you”</h2>

The promise of 1:3 stands in latter-day revelation in the same two halves: “Draw near unto me and I will draw near unto you; seek me diligently and ye shall find me” ([[D&C 88:63]]). The Church's footnotes join that verse to Zechariah 1:3 and to James 4:8, “Draw nigh to God, and he will draw nigh to you.”[@lds-scriptures] The condition has not been withdrawn. On one reading of 1:3, which holds for any disciple, the more earnestly and the more thoroughly the people turned to Him, the more He would bestow His grace.[@keil-zechariah]

But Zechariah shows the Lord who set the condition turning before His people's turning is complete. Malachi's form of the promise, “Return unto me, and I will return unto you,” has been set beside the father in the parable. That father, when his son “was yet a great way off … saw him, and had compassion, and ran” ([[Luke 15:20]]). Of that parable: “the most important aspect of this parable is what it tells us of our Heavenly Father's reaction when we turn toward Him: He runs to embrace us.”[@curtis-he-ran] I carry that reading back to Zechariah, which tells the same order in a nation's history. A disciple's turning is real and required. The one who turns toward the Lord, however partly, turns toward One who has already said, “I am returned … with mercies.”
