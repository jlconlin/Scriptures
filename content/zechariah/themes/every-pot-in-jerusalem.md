title: Every Pot in Jerusalem
blurb: The words on every pot at the end of Zechariah, “HOLINESS UNTO THE LORD,” are the words of the gold plate the high priest wore so that he could bear the iniquity of the people's holy things. Between the plate and the pots, Zechariah shows the high priest made clean before the turban that carried it is set on his head, the Lord promising to remove the land's iniquity in one day, and a fountain opened for sin. The words come from the law. Moses was told to make “a plate of pure gold, and grave upon it, like the engravings of a signet, HOLINESS TO THE LORD” ([[Ex. 28:36]]), and to tie it to the front of Aaron's mitre. Verse 38 gives the reason: “And it shall be upon Aaron’s forehead, that Aaron may bear the iniquity of the holy things, which the children of Israel shall hallow in all their holy gifts; and it shall be always upon his forehead, that they may be accepted before the LORD” ([[Ex. 28:38]]).

Zechariah's book ends with the same words in places no priest would wear them: “In that day shall there be upon the bells of the horses, HOLINESS UNTO THE LORD; and the pots in the LORD’s house shall be like the bowls before the altar. Yea, every pot in Jerusalem and in Judah shall be holiness unto the LORD of hosts” ([[Zech. 14:20–21]]). The Hebrew is the same two words in both places, *qōdeš laYHWH*, and the Church's edition points each verse to the other.[@oshb-wlc][@lds-scriptures]

I read what lies between those two verses as the account of how the plate's work was finished. Zechariah shows the high priest standing in filthy garments, and the Lord taking his iniquity away before the turban that carried the plate is set on his head (3:3–5). The Lord promises to remove the iniquity of the whole land “in one day” (3:9), and opens a fountain “for sin and for uncleanness” (13:1). When the inscription reaches the horses' bells there is no iniquity left in the people's holy things for a priest to bear. The holiness that was gathered on one man's forehead to cover the people now stands on everything, because the Lord has removed what it covered.

The commentators tie each end of that arc to the plate, but separately, and none that I have read joins Joshua's turban to the pots. The nearest is a sentence on Zechariah 14:20–21: the line between holy and profane “can only cease, however, when the sin and moral defilement which first evoked this distinction … have been entirely removed and wiped away.”[@keil-zechariah] On that reading the removal comes at the last judgment, when “sin with all its consequences be cleansed away by the judgment.”[@keil-zechariah] This page builds on that condition and adds, as its own reading, the way Zechariah's book shows it met: the forgiveness of chapter 3 and the fountain of chapter 13. It does not follow one Hebrew word through the book. The word for iniquity in Exodus 28:38, *ʿāwōn*, appears in Zechariah only at 3:4 and 3:9; chapter 13 uses other words for sin and uncleanness, and the verses about the pots use none of them.[@oshb-wlc] What runs through the book is the plate and what it was for.

<h2 id="bear-the-iniquity">“That Aaron may bear the iniquity of the holy things”</h2>

In “bear the iniquity,” the verb is *nāśāʾ* and the noun is *ʿāwōn*, and the lexicon puts this verse beside Leviticus 10:17 under the sense “bear the punishment for iniquity of others.”[@oshb-wlc][@bdb] The Church's footnote gives a second rendering of “bear”: “OR atone for.”[@lds-scriptures]

Readers differ on what the iniquity in the holy things was. One reading makes the high priest an atoning mediator, given power “to exterminate the sin which clung to the holy offerings of the people on account of the unholiness of their nature.”[@keil-exodus] Another makes the guilt narrower, “any ritual error or mistake made accidentally in offering the holy things,” with the plate marking him as “the people’s specially holy representative before God.”[@cambridge-exodus] The note on [Exodus 28:38](/exodus/28/), “How holy things could carry iniquity,” sets out the readings.

On either reading the arrangement has the same shape, and the argument here needs only that. The holiness was concentrated on one man. The iniquity was in what the people brought. Because he wore the plate, and wore it “always,” their gifts were accepted. Even on the reading that makes his bearing a taking away, the removal of sin by the priesthood of the law “had to be continually repeated,”[@keil-zechariah] and Hebrews says of every such priest that he offers “oftentimes the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins” ([[Heb. 10:11]]).

<h2 id="iniquity-to-pass">“I have caused thine iniquity to pass from thee”</h2>

Zechariah's fourth vision puts that office on trial. The prophet sees “Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the LORD, and Satan standing at his right hand to resist him” ([[Zech. 3:1]]); the Church's footnote gives the Hebrew sense of “Satan” as “the Adversary or Accuser.”[@lds-scriptures] Joshua stands there for more than himself. The commentators read his garments as the people's sins on their representative: “the high priest, and the nation in him,” or the priest's vesture “symbolically defiled through the sins of the people.”[@keil-zechariah][@barnes-zechariah]

“Now Joshua was clothed with filthy garments, and stood before the angel. And he answered and spake unto those that stood before him, saying, Take away the filthy garments from him. And unto him he said, Behold, I have caused thine iniquity to pass from thee, and I will clothe thee with change of raiment” ([[Zech. 3:3–4]]). The man whose office was to bear the people's iniquity has iniquity on himself, and the word is the noun of Exodus 28:38.[@oshb-wlc] “Caused … to pass” is not a phrase Zechariah coined. It is a common way of speaking of forgiveness, the verb Nathan used to David, “The LORD also hath put away thy sin” ([[2 Sam. 12:13]]), and the lexicon lists the two verses together.[@bdb][@sears-intercession] It means, in plain words, “I have forgiven thy sin.”[@keil-zechariah]

Then the prophet speaks: “And I said, Let them set a fair mitre upon his head. So they set a fair mitre upon his head, and clothed him with garments” ([[Zech. 3:5]]). The Hebrew is “a clean (pure) cap.”[@lds-scriptures] Zechariah's word, *ṣānîp*, is not the word Exodus uses for the high priest's mitre, but the commentators take it for the same headdress; one writes that “there is no doubt that the same thing is intended.”[@oshb-wlc][@cambridge-zechariah][@keil-zechariah] That turban was “the substratum for the golden plate,” and setting it on Joshua showed him “qualified to intercede for the people.”[@keil-zechariah][@pulpit-zechariah]

The order of the scene is the point I draw from it. The Lord takes the iniquity away first, and only then is the turban that carried “HOLINESS TO THE LORD” set on Joshua's head. The turban that carried the plate did not make Joshua clean. It was set on a man the Lord had already made clean. Joshua says nothing and does nothing until it is done; the first thing asked of him comes after: “If thou wilt walk in my ways, and if thou wilt keep my charge, then thou shalt also judge my house” ([[Zech. 3:7]]). The cleansing is given. The walking is asked.

<h2 id="in-one-day">“I will remove the iniquity of that land in one day”</h2>

The vision does not stop with one man. Joshua and his fellows are “men wondered at: for, behold, I will bring forth my servant the BRANCH” ([[Zech. 3:8]]), and the next verse widens the cleansing: “For behold the stone that I have laid before Joshua; upon one stone shall be seven eyes: behold, I will engrave the graving thereof, saith the LORD of hosts, and I will remove the iniquity of that land in one day” ([[Zech. 3:9]]). Joshua and the priests with him were “symbolic of the coming Branch, who would remove the iniquity of the land in a single day.”[@scripture-helps-hag-1-2]

Two details tie the verse to the plate. “Engrave the graving” uses the verb and noun of the plate's “engravings of a signet”; the verb occurs three times in Exodus 28, once “of the golden plate on his mitre.”[@oshb-wlc][@cambridge-zechariah] And the iniquity is again *ʿāwōn*, the word of Exodus 28:38 and of Zechariah 3:4, now the iniquity of the whole land.[@oshb-wlc][@bdb]

Faithful readers place the “one day” differently. The Church's chapter heading reads the verse of the Second Coming: “At the Second Coming, iniquity will be removed in one day.”[@lds-scriptures] Elder Bruce R. McConkie read it as the day when “the wicked shall be destroyed and the millennial era of peace and righteousness commence.”[@ot-seminary-teacher-156] The older commentators read it as the day of the Crucifixion. One compares it to the “once for all” of Hebrews: the removal of sin by the Messiah “will not resemble that effected by the typical priesthood, which had to be continually repeated, but will be all finished at once. This one day is the day of Golgotha.”[@keil-zechariah] One of them sets it against the Day of Atonement, whose “yearly repetition showed that nothing lasting was effected,”[@barnes-zechariah] and another allows that the Day of Atonement may be glanced at before the prophecy reaches the same day of the Cross.[@cambridge-zechariah] The notes on [Zechariah 3:9](/zechariah/3/) give the readings.

I do not choose between them. They differ on the day. They agree on whose act it is: “I will remove,” says the LORD of hosts. That is the change from Exodus 28. The plate was on Aaron's forehead “always,” and the priesthood's work against sin had to be done again and again. Zechariah promises a day when the Lord removes the iniquity of the land.

<h2 id="for-sin-and-for-uncleanness">“For sin and for uncleanness”</h2>

The second half of the book brings the same promise to the city. “I will pour upon the house of David, and upon the inhabitants of Jerusalem, the spirit of grace and of supplications: and they shall look upon me whom they have pierced, and they shall mourn for him” ([[Zech. 12:10]]). John quotes the verse at the cross: “They shall look on him whom they pierced” ([[John 19:37]]). In latter-day revelation the Lord says what the mourners will see, “I am Jesus that was crucified,” and “then shall they weep because of their iniquities” ([[D&C 45:52–53]]).

The next verse answers the mourning: “In that day there shall be a fountain opened to the house of David and to the inhabitants of Jerusalem for sin and for uncleanness” ([[Zech. 13:1]]). The two nouns are *ḥaṭṭāʾt* (sin) and *niddâ* (uncleanness), and both stand in one verse of the law, the water made from the ashes of the red heifer, kept “for a water of separation: it is a purification for sin” ([[Num. 19:9]]).[@oshb-wlc] The Church's footnote there explains “water of separation” as “water for removal of impurity.”[@lds-scriptures] The law's water was laid up and sprinkled; Zechariah's is a fountain. The lustral waters, “contained and renewed in bowl or laver,” give way to a fountain “opened once but remaining open ever.”[@cambridge-zechariah] The Church's footnote on “fountain” points to baptism, and its heading to the chapter says that “the Jews will gain forgiveness at the Second Coming.”[@lds-scriptures] The note on [Zechariah 13:1](/zechariah/13/) explains *niddâ*, and the Ezekiel page [A New Heart and a New Spirit](/ezekiel/themes/new-heart-new-spirit/) follows the outpouring of the Spirit.

The verse after uses Joshua's verb again: “I will cause the prophets and the unclean spirit to pass out of the land” ([[Zech. 13:2]]). The lexicon lists it with Zechariah 3:4 under one sense, “cause to pass away, take away.”[@oshb-wlc][@bdb] What the Lord did for Joshua's iniquity in chapter 3, He does in chapter 13 for the uncleanness of the land.

<h2 id="upon-the-bells-of-the-horses">“Upon the bells of the horses”</h2>

The inscription the high priest wore is on the bells of the horses, so that the bells “will resemble the head-dress of the high priest in holiness.”[@keil-zechariah] The cooking pots of the temple, the vessels of lesser holiness used for boiling the sacrificial meat, become equal to the bowls that held the blood at the altar; “the lower equal to the highest.”[@pulpit-zechariah] Then every pot in Judah is holy, “and all they that sacrifice shall come and take of them, and seethe therein: and in that day there shall be no more the Canaanite in the house of the LORD of hosts” ([[Zech. 14:21]]).

Two readings of these verses are on record, and they do not contradict each other. The Church's reading sees holiness reaching ordinary things: in the Millennium everything, “symbolized by such trifles as the trappings on horses and earthen jars,” shall be “holy and pure.”[@ot-manual-33] The older commentators add that the distinction itself is gone: “All distinction between sacred and secular shall be at an end, because all shall now be alike holy.”[@cambridge-zechariah] One of them guards against misreading this: the prophet “does not bring down what is sacred to a level with common things, but he uplifts ordinary things.”[@barnes-zechariah] “The Canaanite” is read as the merchant, as any unclean person, or as the wicked; the Church's reading is “the wicked,” and the note on [Zechariah 14:21](/zechariah/14/) gives the rest.[@bdb][@cambridge-zechariah][@ot-manual-33] The commentators see the verse fulfilled in the city of Revelation, where “there shall in no wise enter into it any thing that defileth” ([[Rev. 21:27]]).[@cambridge-zechariah][@barnes-zechariah]

Here the sentence this page began from does its work. The line between holy and common can cease only when the sin that made it necessary has been “entirely removed and wiped away.”[@keil-zechariah] That reading places its fulfillment at the judgment: “In the perfected kingdom of God there will be no more sinners, but only such as are righteous and holy,” and “the Canaanite” of 14:21, a people under the curse and “exterminated by the judgment,” stands for “the godless members of the covenant nation.”[@keil-zechariah] The Church's reading of the Canaanite as “the wicked” looks the same way.[@ot-manual-33] My reading adds what Zechariah's own book has already shown of forgiveness and cleansing, as the way the condition is met: the iniquity caused to pass from the priest (3:4), the iniquity of the land removed in one day (3:9), a fountain opened for sin and uncleanness (13:1), and the unclean spirit caused to pass out of the land (13:2).

So I read the words on the bells as the sign that the plate's work is finished. The plate was worn so that one man could bear the iniquity of the holy things and the people's gifts be accepted. On a horse's bell no priest is bearing iniquity. The words can stand on every pot because there is no iniquity left in the people's holy things for anyone to carry. The pots are not holy because the plate's holiness has been spread thin. They are holy because what kept them common has been taken away.

<h2 id="a-great-high-priest">“A great high priest”</h2>

Joshua himself points beyond his day. He “was a type of Jesus Christ, the great High Priest (see Hebrews 4:14). The chapter is messianic.”[@ot-manual-33] Summing up Hebrews, the Bible Dictionary calls Jesus Christ the great High Priest, “of whom all the others were pre-figures,”[@bd-high-priest] and it reads the comfort of Zechariah 3 the same way: the ministries among the people “are but types of a more perfect one, when through the Branch (Messiah) the Lord will remove the iniquity of the land in one day.”[@bd-zechariah]

Hebrews names what divides the type from the One it points to. Jesus is the high priest “Who needeth not daily, as those high priests, to offer up sacrifice, first for his own sins, and then for the people’s: for this he did once, when he offered up himself” ([[Heb. 7:27]]). “By the which will we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” ([[Heb. 10:10]]). Those are the verses set beside Zechariah's “one day.”[@keil-zechariah] The garments are tied to the Lamb as well. Joshua's clean clothing “symbolizes the forgiveness of his sins (compare Exodus 28:36–39),” and beside it stand those who “have washed their robes, and made them white in the blood of the Lamb” ([[Rev. 7:14]]).[@ot-minute-zech][@keil-zechariah]

Read with Zechariah 3, Hebrews 7:27 marks the difference I see. Joshua's own iniquity had to be taken away before the turban was set on his head. The High Priest of Hebrews needed no offering for His own sins; the one man the plate's words described, “perfectly holy before God,”[@ot-manual-ex-25-30] is the One who removes the iniquity the plate was worn to bear. Aaron's bearing had to go on always; Jesus Christ's offering was made once. Whether the “one day” of 3:9 is read of Golgotha or of His coming, the removal is His. The holiness that spreads to the bells and the pots spreads from His work, not from the priest's.

<h2 id="if-thou-wilt-walk-in-my-ways">“If thou wilt walk in my ways”</h2>

The cleansing is the Lord's work through Jesus Christ, given as His people repent. As Joshua's filthy garments were taken away, “if we repent, Jesus Christ can take away our sins. We are forgiven and can become more holy.”[@liahona-2022-12-holy] We become more holy “when we exercise faith in Christ, demonstrate our obedience, repent, sacrifice for Him, receive sacred ordinances, and keep our covenants with Him.”[@liahona-2022-12-holy] The walking is asked: “If thou wilt walk in my ways” ([[Zech. 3:7]]), and “forgiveness is the springboard for discipleship.”[@ot-minute-zech]

The end of the book reaches the disciple's ordinary days. Elder D. Todd Christofferson recalled that the pioneer Saints put “Holiness to the Lord” on sacrament cups, ordination certificates, a hammer and a doorknob, and said that these places “suggest just how pervasive and constant our focus on holiness needs to be,” and that it is sought “even in the details of daily living: our speech, our dress, our thoughts.”[@christofferson-living-bread] The inscription “must also be engraved on every heart.”[@cfm-ot-2026-50] What Zechariah adds is the reason it can be. The words reach the pots because the Lord has removed the iniquity the plate was worn to cover, and they reach a disciple's speech and thoughts by the same work of Christ.

Haggai's priests ruled that holy flesh carried in the skirt of a garment does not make holy the bread the garment touches ([[Hag. 2:12]]), and the essay [Shall It Be Holy?](/haggai/themes/shall-it-be-holy/) follows that ruling to the presence of the Lord. Read beside it, Zechariah's ending gives the other half of the answer as I see it: holiness carried in a garment's fold does not reach the bread, and Zechariah's pots are holy because the Lord has taken sin away.
