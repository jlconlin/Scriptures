## What a chapter is for here

Zephaniah is 53 verses in three chapters, nearly all judgment until the last thirteen verses, which are nearly all joy. A reader asks: *Who was Zephaniah, and why does he give four generations of ancestors? Is “Hizkiah” King Hezekiah? When under Josiah did he preach, before or after the book of the law was found? Why does a judgment on Judah begin with every man, beast, bird and fish? What are the Chemarims, Malcham, the host of heaven on the housetops? What is “strange apparel,” and who “leap on the threshold”? Where were the fish gate, the second quarter, Maktesh? What does “settled on their lees” mean, and why candles? Is the day of the LORD Judah's fall or the Second Coming? Why these nations, and what became of them? Who are the meek, and why only “it may be ye shall be hid”? What is the “pure language”? Who are the suppliants beyond the rivers of Ethiopia? How can the Lord “rest in his love,” and does God sing?* A chapter answers those from the text first.

**Judgment first, then the remnant.** The book turns on 2:3 (seek the Lord, “it may be ye shall be hid”) and on 3:9 (“For then…”), where judgment becomes purification. Each chapter's thread says where it stands on that road. The first and last chapters answer each other: the Lord who will “utterly consume all things” (1:2) is the Lord who “will rejoice over thee with joy” (3:17); the “day” of wrath (1:15) becomes “that day” of “Fear thou not” (3:16).

**Say what the prophecy meant in Josiah's Jerusalem before what it foreshadows.** The Church's sources read it as both: *Scripture Helps*, “the words of Zephaniah can apply to both his day and the future,” with the destruction compared “to the destruction of the wicked that will occur before the Second Coming”; the chapter heading, “symbolic of the Second Coming.” Each chapter gives the near fulfilment (Judah's fall to Babylon; Nineveh's in 612 B.C.; the Philistines' end) from a source, then the latter-day reading as the Church's.

## Divisions

**Proposed: three divisions, one for each chapter** (`book.yaml`). Chapter 1 (`zephday`): the day of the LORD on all the earth and on Judah. Chapter 2 (`zephnations`): the call to the meek (2:1–3) and the oracles on the nations to the west, east, south and north (2:4–15). Chapter 3 (`zephzion`): woe to Jerusalem (3:1–8), then the purified peoples, the remnant, and the Lord rejoicing in Zion (3:9–20). The Bible Dictionary divides the book in two, “universal judgment (Zeph. 1:1–3:8)” and “universal salvation in the knowledge of Jehovah (3:9–20)”; Keil in three (chapter 1; 2:1–3:8; 3:9–20), marking the refrain “devoured by the fire of his jealousy” (1:18; 3:8) and the two woes (2:5; 3:1). The site's divisions are chapter ranges, so neither can be followed exactly; chapter 3's setting says where the turn falls, from the Bible Dictionary. *Scripture Helps* gives no divisions.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, which later scripture takes up which line) is the coordinator's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **When.** “In the days of Josiah the son of Amon” (1:1). The dates differ by a year among the Church's sources: Bible Dictionary “Zephaniah,” “639–608 B.C.”; “Josiah,” “641–610 B.C.”; *Scripture Helps*, “the mid-to-late 600s BC”; Van Dyke and Ogden, Josiah “(640–609 BC).” The site's timeline guide uses 640–609 and notes the Bible Dictionary's 641–610. **Proposed: every chapter's `when` is “Zephaniah prophesied in the reign of Josiah of Judah (about 640–609 B.C.), probably early in it,”** with a ledger row from Van Dyke and Ogden or the Bible Dictionary. *When* in the reign is a contested question (below), argued once, in chapter 1's setting.
- **Who.** Nothing is known of him but 1:1 (the Student Manual: “Nothing is known of the life of Zephaniah beyond what can be inferred from his book”). He is not the priest Zephaniah of Jeremiah 21:1 and 29:25 (the Bible Dictionary lists them apart; the site's notes at `[[Jer. 29:26]]` and `[[Jer. 37:3]]` are on the priest): one clause in chapter 1. His contemporaries were Jeremiah, Lehi, Nahum and Habakkuk (KnoWhy #441: “Zephaniah ca. 640–609 bc”; Welch).
- **The name.** Bible Dictionary: “The Lord hides.” Ball: from *ṣāpan*, “to hide, protect, cover, or treasure up,” so “one who is protected or hidden by the Lord,” and “The name seems to summarize the prophet's message.” The Hebrew of 2:3, “it may be ye shall be hid,” is a different verb (`hebrew.mjs zephaniah 2:3`: H5641, *sātar*); Ball calls the pair “a clever wordplay.” Bowen sees the name echoed in “the north” (*ṣāpôn*, 2:13). **Proposed:** the name is explained once, in chapter 1's setting, from the Bible Dictionary and Ball; the wordplay of 2:3 is one Language note in chapter 2, as Ball's; Bowen's echo at 2:13 is one clause, as his. Ball's further claim, that the army of 1:10–12 “never materialized in Jerusalem in Josiah's day” because of Josiah's efforts, is his reading; give it as such or not at all.
- **The day of the LORD.** Already explained on the site: `[[Joel 1:15]]`, `[[Isa. 13:6]]`, `[[Joel 2:31]]`, `[[Mal. 4:5]]`, and Amos 5's thread (`[[Amos 5:18]]`). **Link, don't repeat.** Zephaniah's own contribution is said where it occurs: the day as the LORD's “sacrifice” with bidden “guests” (1:7–8; footnote `[[Hab. 2:20]]`; Keil traces 1:7 to Habakkuk, Joel and Isaiah, as his reading); “day” piled up in 1:14–16 (Parry: in 1:7–18 “the word day is used fourteen times”); and being “hid in the day of the LORD's anger” (2:3). The footnotes at 1:14 point to `[[Mal. 4:5]]` and `[[D&C 110:16]]`: one sentence at 1:14 or in chapter 1's `christ`.
- **Josiah's reform and the idolatry of 1:4–6.** The background is `[[2 Kgs. 21–23]]`: Manasseh's idolatry (Van Dyke and Ogden: Jehovah's worship “merged so completely with idolatry that it was barely distinguishable from paganism”), Amon, Josiah. The guides [Babylon and the Exile](/old-testament/guides/babylon-and-the-exile/#rulers) and [A Timeline of the Old Testament](/old-testament/guides/israels-story/#babylon) carry the kings: point to them. 1:4–5 is explained once, in chapter 1, from the footnotes and `[[2 Kgs. 23:4–13]]`, where Josiah removes the same things: “Chemarims” (footnote “OR idolatrous priests”; check the Hebrew of `[[2 Kgs. 23:5]]` before saying it is the same word); “the host of heaven upon the housetops” (*Scripture Helps* 36: “false gods associated with planets and stars”; the site's note at `[[Jer. 19:13]]`: link); “Malcham” (*Scripture Helps*: “Many scholars believe this is a reference to Milcom, an Ammonite deity, or Molech”; the site's `[[Lev. 18:21]]` note: link; Keil reads “their king”: one clause if needed).
- **Places in Jerusalem (1:10–11).** The footnotes: “the second quarter, a district of Jerusalem”; Maktesh, “a district of Jerusalem”; fish gate, `[[2 Chr. 33:14]]`. The Student Manual (21-4): the fish gate “on the north end of the city,” Maktesh “the merchant quarter”; Brown: “landmarks on the north side of Jerusalem past which the invaders were to come.” One note; no guessed locations.
- **The nations (2:4–15).** Philistia west, Moab and Ammon east, Cush south, Assyria north: the compass is the point (Van Dyke and Ogden: “doom on the unbelieving nations to the north, south, east, and west of Judah,” and by bundling Judah with them Zephaniah “suggests that God's covenant people equal and surpass the wickedness of their neighbors”; Keil: Ethiopia as “the furthest kingdom or people in the south that was known to the Hebrews”). Each people is explained once, from the Bible Dictionary, and the site already covers much: **link, don't repeat**: Philistines `[[Jer. 47:1]]`, `[[Jer. 47:7]]` (quotes Zeph. 2:4, 7); Cherethites `[[Ezek. 25:16]]`; Moab's reproach `[[Jer. 48:26]]` (quotes 2:8); Ammon `[[Ezek. 25:3]]`; Nineveh's boast (2:15) `[[Isa. 47:8]]`. “Ethiopians” is Cush, south of Egypt (`bd-ethiopia`); Johnson's “(people of Egypt)” is not followed. Bowen's wordplay on Gaza and Ekron (2:4): one sentence, his. Nineveh fell in 612 B.C. (Bowen), which dates the oracle before it; Nahum is the companion prophecy (link once written). The cormorant and bittern (2:14): a lexicon's or modern translation's reading, not Johnson's “porcupines or hedgehogs.”
- **Jerusalem's leaders (3:1–4).** Princes, judges, prophets, priests: the site's notes at `[[Ezek. 22:25]]` and Ezekiel 22's `explore` already set Ezekiel's list beside this one: link. “Done violence to the law” (3:4): footnote “HEB torah.”
- **Other books on the site.** Link, and don't say who borrowed from whom except as a source read says. 1:2–3 and the Flood (`[[Gen. 6:7]]`; Keil); 1:7 and `[[Hab. 2:20]]`; 1:13 and `[[Amos 5:11]]`, `[[Deut. 28:30]]` (footnotes); 1:15 and `[[Joel 2:2]]` (the site's `[[Ezek. 30:3]]` note sets them together); 1:18 and `[[Ezek. 7:19]]` (the site's note: “nearly identical”); 2:3 and `[[Ps. 76:9]]` (its note quotes Zeph. 2:3) and `[[Amos 5:15]]` (the note “Perhaps”; Spencer quotes Jeremias that Amos's “perhaps” has “a significant history of influence,” naming Zephaniah); 2:11 and `[[Mal. 1:11]]`; 3:8 and `[[Joel 3:2]]` (the site's `[[Joel 3:12]]` note quotes Elder Joseph Fielding Smith setting Zephaniah beside Joel); 3:14–17 and `[[Isa. 62:5]]` (its note quotes 3:17) and the “daughter of Zion” (KnoWhy #550).
- **Modern revelation and the Book of Mormon.** No comparison panel. Worth a note or a sentence, each from a source read: Lehi preached in the same Jerusalem a generation later (Welch sets 1:2–9, 18 beside Lehi's message and 3:19–20 beside “the eventual recovery of the scattered remnant,” `[[1 Ne. 1:13]]`, `[[1 Ne. 1:19]]`); 2:3 and `[[3 Ne. 12:5]]` (footnote); 3:17 and `[[3 Ne. 17]]` (*Come, Follow Me*); 3:9 and `[[Moses 6:6]]` (below). A cross-reference that only repeats a footnote is not a note.
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** None: the footnotes of all three chapters have no Joseph Smith Translation reading.
- **Names of God.** “The LORD,” “the Lord GOD” (1:7), “the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel” (2:9), “the king of Israel, even the LORD” (3:15). Copy every phrase from `data/kjv/zephaniah.json`. The Church's footnote at 3:15 is “TG Jesus Christ, Millennial Reign”: say once, at 3:15, that the King who is “in the midst of thee” is the Lord Jesus Christ, from a Church source read.
- **Verse numbers.** The Hebrew and King James chapters and verses agree throughout (`hebrew.mjs` prints no Hebrew reference beside any verse).
- **Theme candidates.** Reported by writers; the coordinator adds them to `THEME-CANDIDATES.md` (not yet created).

**Habits to avoid** are listed in Amos's brief and hold here. In Zephaniah especially: **don't make the judgments of 1:2–3 and 3:8 literal geography of the end of the world**, and don't tie the day of the LORD to a date or to current events. **Describe the slaughter of 1:17 rather than dwelling on it** (`STANDARDS.md` §3).

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that three chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-23.

- **“The son of Hizkiah”: is it King Hezekiah? (1:1; chapter 1's setting).** The Hebrew is the king's name (`hebrew.mjs zephaniah 1:1`: H2396; the lexicon page lists the King James renderings “Hezekiah, Hizkiah, Hizkijah”). Keil: since usually only the father is named, Hezekiah “must have been a celebrated man, and that in all probability the king of that name is intended”; “king of Judah” is missing because it follows “immediately afterwards in connection with Josiah's name,” and the generations fit because “Manasseh reigned for fifty-five years.” The Student Manual (21-2): “It is not known whether this individual was the same as Hezekiah the king.” Johnson states it as fact. **Proposed:** the note says the name is the king's, that the four generations are why many take him to be the king (Keil's argument, briefly, as one reading), and that the text does not say so and the Church's manual leaves it open; nothing else is built on it. Johnson's “only Old Testament prophet who included his genealogy” is not used (Zechariah 1:1 names a grandfather).
- **The date, and Josiah's reform (chapter 1's setting).** Josiah began “to seek after the God of David” in his eighth year, “to purge Judah and Jerusalem” in his twelfth, and the book of the law was found in his eighteenth (`[[2 Chr. 34:3]]`, `[[2 Chr. 34:8]]`; `[[2 Kgs. 22:3–8]]`). Van Dyke and Ogden place Zephaniah “sometime during the earlier period of Josiah's reign,” his teachings having “girded up Josiah's initial reforms,” since “he is addressing problems that were generally done away with by the later reforms” (note 17): before 622 B.C. The Student Manual: his ministry “probably played an important part in the reform movement.” Keil reads “the remnant of Baal” (1:4) as showing the reform “had already commenced.” **Proposed:** the setting says the idolatry the book condemns is what the reform removed (2 Kgs. 23), so most place it early, before 622 B.C. (Van Dyke and Ogden), with “the remnant of Baal” as the phrase the date turns on; Keil's later date, if given, one clause. Don't say Zephaniah caused the reform.
- **The foe from the north, and the Scythians.** The Student Manual (21-1) prints Dummelow that the enemy was “the barbarous Scythians”; Keil rejects it (“not a trace discoverable in the whole book of any great commotion among the nations”), and Brown notes that Zephaniah “did not name this foe.” **Proposed:** no chapter names the Scythians as the enemy; if a reader would ask (1:10, 1:16), one sentence: an older view tied the book to a Scythian raid; the book names no invader.
- **The day of the LORD: Judah's fall or the end of the world? (1:2–3, 14–18; 3:8).** The Church's frame is both: the chapter heading, *Scripture Helps* 35, the Student Manual (21-3: “the dualism so common in the writings of Hebrew prophets”), Van Dyke and Ogden (“in that day” and “at that time” “dually describe Judah … and covenant people in the latter days”). Keil: the universal judgment of 1:2–3 is “an integral part of his prophecy.” **Proposed:** each setting says the prophecy was fulfilled first in Judah's fall to Babylon (586 B.C.) and is read by the Church as a type of the judgments before the Second Coming; notes on 1:2–3 and 3:8 say the scope is the whole earth. The Student Manual's “usually refers in the scriptures to the Second Coming” is not the site's definition (`[[Joel 1:15]]` has it).
- **“Dies irae” (1:15).** The Vulgate of 1:15 begins “Dies irae dies illa” (`vulgate`), the opening of the medieval judgment hymn. Reception history (`STANDARDS.md` §1), but a reader who knows the Requiem will ask. **Proposed:** at most one sentence at 1:15, giving the Vulgate's words; the hymn is named only if the writer reads a source (not a wiki) that ties it to the verse. None read for this brief does.
- **“Settled on their lees” (1:12).** Footnote “OR complacent, indifferent,” and `[[Jer. 48:11]]` (the site's note “Wine left on its lees”: link, don't repeat). Add only what is new: the candles, and their saying “The LORD will not do good, neither will he do evil,” men who think God inactive (in a source's words).
- **The nations' judgment, and the remnant's gain (2:4–15).** The coast and Moab's land go to “the remnant” (2:7, 9). Johnson dates the Philistines' end to Nebuchadnezzar in 604 B.C.: check a second source (the site's `[[Jer. 47:1]]` note) before using it. **Not a charter for conquest:** 2:11 gives the purpose, “he will famish all the gods of the earth; and men shall worship him.” Johnson on “famish” (gods thought “fed by the fumes and smoke” of offerings): hers. If a note needs a frame for judgment on nations, `scripture-helps-violence`, not a defence.
- **“It may be ye shall be hid” (2:1–3).** The book's one call to repent. “Meek” (*ʿănāwîm*, `hebrew.mjs zephaniah 2:3`); the footnotes point to `[[Ps. 76:9]]`, `[[3 Ne. 12:5]]` and `[[D&C 88:17]]`. The Student Manual (21-5, quoting the *New Bible Commentary*): Zephaniah “does not see any way of escape for any but for the humble.” “It may be” is honest uncertainty, not a threat withheld: the site's note at `[[Amos 5:15]]` treats the same “perhaps”: link it. The wordplay with the prophet's name is under “The name,” above. **Proposed:** chapter 2's thread and `christ` turn on this verse.
- **“A pure language” (3:9): handle as the Church's sources do.** The Hebrew is *śāpâ bĕrûrâ*, literally “a purified lip” (`hebrew.mjs zephaniah 3:9`: H8193, H1305; BDB, “to purify, select”). *Śāpâ* is Babel's “one language” (`[[Gen. 11:1]]`; the site's note there, “One lip, one set of words,” explains it and gives the Restoration's teaching on the language of Adam: link). Modern translations read lips or speech: NIV “Then I will purify the lips of the peoples”; NRSVue and ESV “a pure speech.” Keil: the lip “of a man who is defiled by sin,” and “purity of the lips involves or presupposes the purification of the heart” (`[[Isa. 6:5]]`). The Restoration readings: the chapter heading, “Men will have a pure language”; footnote “TG Communication; Language”; Nyman, “the return of a pure language to the people of the Lord”; Johnson, perhaps “a reversal of what happened at the tower of Babel”; Cowdery (1834), “the righteous shall speak a pure language.” **Proposed:** the note gives the Hebrew and its plain sense first (lips made clean, so that the peoples “call upon the name of the LORD” “with one consent”: footnote “TG Common Consent; Unity”; Keil's “one shoulder”); then the echo of Babel, which the shared word makes real; then the Restoration reading as a reading, with `[[Moses 6:6]]` (“a language which was pure and undefiled”) as Restoration scripture's own phrase. It does not say the language of Adam will be restored or what it was: no Church source read says this verse means that. How the note ends is the one question for the author (the coordinator files it): recommended, the reading “as the chapter heading puts it,” with Moses 6:6 and “no revelation says this verse refers to it,” rather than stating the restoration of Adam's language as the site's reading.
- **“My suppliants … the daughter of my dispersed” (3:10).** Cush bringing offerings (footnote `[[Isa. 18:1]]`). Two readings, given as readings: the nations' worshippers (Keil), or scattered Israel brought home (Cowdery).
- **The remnant (2:7, 9; 3:12–13, 18–20).** “An afflicted and poor people” who “trust in the name of the LORD” (3:12). Explained once, in chapter 3; Micah's notes on “the remnant of Jacob” (once written) and *Scripture Helps*' section on Micah 5:7–15 are linked, not repeated. 3:19, “her that halteth” (footnote “OR is lame”): Calabro lists 3:18–20 among prophecies of “a culmination of the Lord's mercy in healing disabilities.” The gathering (3:19–20; footnote “TG Israel, Gathering of”; “turn back your captivity,” as at 2:7, footnote `[[Deut. 30:3]]`): in the latter-day reading, the Church's doctrine of the gathering, from a Church source read.
- **The Lord rejoicing over Zion with singing (3:14–17).** The book's most quoted verses. “He will rest in his love” is *yaḥărîš*, “he will be silent” (H2790, “to be silent, to be still”), which translators handle differently: ESV “he will quiet you by his love,” NIV “in his love he will no longer rebuke you,” NRSVue “he will renew you in his love” (as the Greek: Brenton, “shall refresh thee with his love”). Keil: “Silence in His love is an expression used to denote love deeply felt, which is absorbed in its object.” **This is where the Hebrew pays: one Language note at 3:17.** “He will joy over thee with singing”: it is the Lord who sings; say so plainly. KnoWhy #372: “the joy and love that God feels when He is with us.” **Proposed:** chapter 3's `christ` is the King in the midst of His people (3:15, 17) and the Saviour's joy over the Nephites (`[[3 Ne. 17:20–21]]`, the comparison *Come, Follow Me* draws), stated, not asked. The site's `[[Isa. 62:5]]` note already quotes 3:17: link.
