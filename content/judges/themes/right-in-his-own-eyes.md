Judges ends with a sentence that has become its summary: “In those days there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes” ([[Judg. 21:25]]). The usual way to read it is as a verdict on the chapters just before it, Micah’s shrine, the night at Gibeah, the war on Benjamin, and as a glance ahead to the kings who come next. The Church’s study helps read it that way, and so do the notes on [chapter 17](/judges/17/) and [chapter 21](/judges/21/) of this site. That reading is right as far as it goes. But the sentence has a longer history inside the book than the five chapters it frames. For twelve chapters Judges measures Israel by another pair of eyes, and the English hides it. “Did evil in the sight of the LORD” is, in Hebrew, “did the evil in the eyes of the LORD,” and “right in his own eyes” is the same noun with a different owner. The book opens by measuring Israel with the LORD’s eyes and closes by measuring with man’s, and between the two it tells the story of a judge who chose a wife because she was “right in my eyes” and died praying about “my two eyes.”

I want to argue that this change of eyes is the book’s own account of what went wrong in Israel. I also want to offer a reading of my own about a second thing the book stops saying, that the land had rest. The refrain is Moses’ sentence, and Moses tied it to a people “not as yet come to the rest”: in his mouth a people measuring by its own eyes and a people not yet at rest are one condition, and Judges, as I read it, shows the first condition returning as the rests stop. Read with his words in mind, Judges, which follows a book that says the LORD gave Israel rest ([[Josh. 21:44]]), is the record of a people living as though they had not arrived. It leaves its reader looking past the judges to the Judge whose eyes are the measure, and to “a rest to the people of God” that Hebrews says remains ([[Heb. 4:9]]).

## In the eyes of the LORD

The book’s frame begins at [[Judg. 2:11]]: “And the children of Israel did evil in the sight of the LORD, and served Baalim.” The sentence returns at the head of each oppression, at 3:7 and 3:12 (where it comes twice), 4:1, 6:1, 10:6, and for the last time at [[Judg. 13:1]]. In Hebrew it is *hāraʿ bĕʿênê YHWH*, “the evil in the eyes of the LORD.”[@oshb-wlc] “Sight” is a fair rendering, but it loses the noun, and the noun is what this essay follows.

What the phrase does is name whose judgment counts. It does not say that Israel thought it was doing evil. It says that in the LORD’s eyes it was, and the law had made those eyes the standard. Deuteronomy uses the same measure in its positive form, “thou shalt do that which is right and good in the sight of the LORD” ([[Deut. 6:18]]), and once the King James keeps the noun, “that which is right in the eyes of the LORD thy God” ([[Deut. 13:18]]). The refrain of Judges is a departure from that covenant standard, and Goff makes the comparison directly, setting Deuteronomy 6 beside it.[@goff-plagiary] Under the law, the LORD’s eyes decided what was right. Judges opens with Israel measured by them.

The frame has a second clause, which belongs with the first. After each deliverance “the land had rest” ([[Judg. 3:11]]), and the sentence is repeated at 3:30, 5:31, and at 8:28, where the King James has “the country was in quietness forty years in the days of Gideon.” The Cambridge commentary calls the line a formula of the book’s editor and lists its four uses with the two in Joshua, “the land had rest from war.”[@cambridge-judges] One Hebrew verb stands behind all of them, *šāqaṭ*, to be quiet.[@oshb-wlc] So the cycle runs from one formula to the other: evil in the LORD’s eyes, an oppressor, a cry, a deliverer, and the land quiet.

Then both formulas stop. The land is last said to be quiet in Gideon’s days ([[Judg. 8:28]]), and no judge after him is said to bring it rest, as the thread of [chapter 8](/judges/8/) observes. The narrator last says Israel did evil in the eyes of the LORD at [[Judg. 13:1]]. Israel does not stop doing evil after that; the narrator stops measuring it by those eyes.

## Right in my eyes

The chapter after the last “eyes of the LORD” opens with Samson seeing a woman. He tells his parents, “I have seen a woman in Timnath of the daughters of the Philistines: now therefore get her for me to wife” ([[Judg. 14:2]]). They object, and he answers them with nothing but a sentence about himself: “Get her for me; for she pleaseth me well” ([[Judg. 14:3]]).

“She pleaseth me well” is the translators’ smoothing of three Hebrew words, *kî-hîʾ yāšrâ bĕʿênāy*, “for she is right in my eyes.” The verb is *yāšar*, to be straight or right, and the phrase that follows it is the phrase of the frame with a new owner: no longer “in the eyes of the LORD” but “in my eyes.”[@oshb-wlc] The narrator then says it again in his own voice, “she pleased Samson well” ([[Judg. 14:7]]), which in Hebrew is *she was right in Samson’s eyes*.[@oshb-wlc] These are the only two places Judges uses the verb. The book’s refrain, “right in his own eyes,” will use the adjective of the same root. So the first person in Judges to measure a thing by his own eyes is a judge of Israel, and the sentence the book will later say of every man is first said by Samson of a wife.

No Church manual draws this sequence. Goff, a Latter-day Saint writer in the *Interpreter* journal, quotes the biblical scholar Stuart Lasine: “in the book of Judges there is a shift in emphasis from doing what is evil in Yahweh’s eyes (2:11; 3:7, 12; 4:1; 6:1; 10:6; 13:1) to doing what is right in one’s own eyes (14:3; 17:6; 21:25).”[@goff-plagiary] What the sentence establishes is the order. The last “eyes of the LORD” is 13:1, the first “my eyes” is 14:3, and the handover happens between two chapters, in the mouth of the man the LORD had raised up for the hour.

The narrator adds at once that his parents did not know “that it was of the LORD, that he sought an occasion against the Philistines” ([[Judg. 14:4]]): the LORD was at work in the marriage, and the verse gives no verdict on the choice. The verdict the narrator withholds, *Scripture Helps* gives in general terms: Samson’s “focus frequently drifted toward fulfilling immoral and selfish desires,” and he “failed to fulfill his divine calling because of selfish and unrighteous choices.”[@scripture-helps-judges] The essay claims no more than that. Samson’s eyes were his measure, and the book says so in its own vocabulary.

Then the eyes come back, and the book makes a reader look at them. When Delilah has had his secret, “the Philistines took him, and put out his eyes, and brought him down to Gaza” ([[Judg. 16:21]]). And the last words Samson speaks are a prayer about them: “strengthen me, I pray thee, only this once, O God, that I may be at once avenged of the Philistines for my two eyes” ([[Judg. 16:28]]).

What follows is my reading. Putting out a captive’s eyes was a common punishment in the ancient world, as Zedekiah’s blinding shows,[@ot-minute-judg-16] and the text does not call Samson’s a judgment on what he said at Timnath; neither do I. But Samson’s story is told between two sentences about his eyes. It begins with a man who makes his eyes the measure of what is right, and it ends with that man having no eyes, grinding in a prison, praying for the two he lost. The book lets the reader watch what his eyes came to.

Samson is one man. In chapter 17 the measure becomes everyone’s.

## Every man in his own eyes

“In those days there was no king in Israel, but every man did that which was right in his own eyes” ([[Judg. 17:6]]). The sentence is repeated word for word in the Hebrew as the book’s last verse, and in its first half at 18:1 and 19:1. In Hebrew the key word is *hayyāšār*, the adjective from Samson’s verb, and the phrase that follows is *bĕʿênāyw*, “in his eyes”; the King James supplies “own.”[@oshb-wlc] The Church’s footnotes at the verse send “right” to Deuteronomy 12:8 and “own eyes” to Proverbs 16:2.[@lds-scriptures]

The two footnotes point at two different things. The first is where the sentence came from, which the next section takes up. The second, Proverbs’ “All the ways of a man are clean in his own eyes; but the LORD weigheth the spirits” ([[Prov. 16:2]]), is what it means: a man’s own eyes as the measure, set against the LORD’s. Keil says the same of Deuteronomy 12:8, taking the phrase of “actions performed according to a man’s own judgment, rather than according to the standard of objective right and the law of God,” and cross-refers it to Judges 17:6 and 21:25.[@keil-deuteronomy] Of Judges 17:6 itself he says that it is offered “in explanation of this unlawful conduct,” Micah’s shrine.[@keil-judges] The phrase does not say that every man did wrong. It says that every man decided for himself what was right, and the book shows what those decisions were: silver dedicated to the LORD made into an image, a Levite hired as a priest, a tribe carrying off both, a host offering two women to a mob, a nation keeping its oaths with a town’s dead and a feast’s daughters. The Student Manual’s summary is blunt: “Nothing in the stories show the Israelites doing what was right.”[@ot-manual-judges-13-21]

A Latter-day Saint study of the book draws the line this essay has been following. The statement that “each man did what was right in his own eyes,” Schade writes, “links theologically back to ‘the Israelites did evil in the eyes of the Lord’ (lit. translation of 2:11; 3:7; 3:12; 4:1; 6:1; 10:6; 13:1). When the Israelites rebelled against the Lord as their covenantal king, they had no king as envisioned by the covenant, so everyone did what was right in their own eyes.”[@schade-broken-laws] Schade credits the observation to Younger, and he reads the missing king as the LORD. *Scripture Helps* reads the same chapters as highlighting Israel’s “lack of unity” and says they “may be implying the need for a king”;[@scripture-helps-judges] the Bible Dictionary says the closing sentence calls that lack of unity to the reader’s attention.[@bd-judges] This essay does not choose between the throne and the kingship of the LORD, and the notes on [[Judg. 21:25]] give both. Whichever king the sentence looks toward, what it says has been lost is a measure outside the man.

One more verse in these chapters is usually counted with the refrain. When the men of Gibeah demand the Levite, the old host offers his daughter and the concubine: “humble ye them, and do with them what seemeth good unto you” ([[Judg. 19:24]]). “What seemeth good unto you” is, in Hebrew, “the good in your eyes,” *haṭṭôb bĕʿênêkem*.[@oshb-wlc] It is not the word of the refrain; the refrain says “right,” and this says “good.” What it is, as Goff notes, following Lasine, is Lot’s sentence at Sodom, “do ye to them as is good in your eyes” ([[Gen. 19:8]]), and the note on [[Judg. 19:22]] explains how the whole scene is told to recall Sodom.[@goff-plagiary]

But the phrase has been used once before in Judges itself, and no source I read notices where. In chapter 10, under Ammon, Israel confesses and says to the LORD: “We have sinned: do thou unto us whatsoever seemeth good unto thee” ([[Judg. 10:15]]). In Hebrew that is *kol-haṭṭôb bĕʿênêkā*, “all the good in thine eyes”: the same two words, “the good” and “in … eyes,” that the host will use, with the LORD’s eyes where the mob’s will be.[@oshb-wlc] It is the one place in the book where Israel, in its own words, puts its case under the LORD’s eyes and leaves the outcome to Him, and the verse that follows says His soul could not bear their misery (the note on [[Judg. 10:16]] has the Hebrew). Nine chapters later the same words are spoken at a door in Gibeah, to a mob, about two women, and the concubine is given. The two words are the same. The eyes they defer to have changed. That comparison is mine; the two verses are there to be checked.

## Not yet come to the rest

The Church’s footnote at 17:6 sends the reader to Moses, and the verse it names deserves to be read with its neighbors:

> Ye shall not do after all the things that we do here this day, every man whatsoever is right in his own eyes. For ye are not as yet come to the rest and to the inheritance, which the LORD your God giveth you. But when ye go over Jordan, and dwell in the land which the LORD your God giveth you to inherit, and when he giveth you rest from all your enemies round about, so that ye dwell in safety; ([[Deut. 12:8–10]])

“Every man whatsoever is right in his own eyes” is the refrain of Judges exactly, *hayyāšār bĕʿênāyw*, and the Church’s footnote at Deuteronomy 12:8 points forward to Judges 17:6 and 21:25 as the footnote at Judges points back.[@oshb-wlc][@lds-scriptures] The note on [[Judg. 17:6]] and the thread of [Deuteronomy 12](/deuteronomy/12/) already say that the words are Moses’.

In his mouth the sentence is not a complaint. It is a description of a stage. Israel in the wilderness was doing what was right in its own eyes, and the reason, Keil says, is that “they had not yet come to the rest and to the inheritance which the Lord was about to give them.”[@keil-deuteronomy] Once the LORD gave them rest from their enemies round about, so that they dwelt in safety, that stage was to end, and worship would go to the one place He chose. The refrain, in its first use, names the condition of a people before the rest.

Joshua says the rest came. “The LORD gave them rest round about, according to all that he sware unto their fathers” ([[Josh. 21:44]]), and that is the verb of Deuteronomy 12:10, “he giveth you rest.”[@oshb-wlc] What Joshua gave, and what it left undone, and why Hebrews says another rest remained, is argued on Joshua’s page [Rest Round About](/joshua/themes/rest-round-about/).

Judges never uses Moses’ verb for rest. Moses’ noun for it, “the rest” of Deuteronomy 12:9, appears once in the book, at the end, where Israel “trode them down with ease” ([[Judg. 20:43]]).[@oshb-wlc] Judges’ own word for the land’s quiet after a deliverance is *šāqaṭ*, the verb Joshua used of the land resting from war,[@cambridge-judges] and it is said four times, after Othniel, after Ehud, after Deborah, and after Gideon, and then no more.

The verb appears twice more after Gideon, and not of Israel. When the five Danite spies come to Laish, they find “the people that were therein, how they dwelt careless, after the manner of the Zidonians, quiet and secure” ([[Judg. 18:7]]). “Quiet” is *šōqēṭ*, the participle of the verb of the land’s rests, and “secure” is its companion, *bōṭēaḥ*. “Dwelt careless” is *yôšebet lābeṭaḥ*, dwelling in safety, and *beṭaḥ* is the word Moses used at Deuteronomy 12:10 for what Israel would have when the LORD gave it rest: “so that ye dwell in safety.”[@oshb-wlc] The narrator says it again at the moment of the attack: the Danites came “unto a people that were at quiet and secure: and they smote them with the edge of the sword, and burnt the city with fire” ([[Judg. 18:27]]), and “there was no deliverer” ([[Judg. 18:28]]).

The connection I draw from this is my own; no commentary I read has it. The last people in Judges to dwell in safety as Deuteronomy 12:10 promised Israel, and to be quiet in the word Judges used for the land’s rests, are the Canaanites of Laish. Israel, under the sentence “there was no king in Israel” ([[Judg. 18:1]]), comes to that quiet with the sword, burns the city, and sets up Micah’s image in it. Judges does not say that Dan destroyed a rest it had failed to enter. It says that the city was quiet, in the word it had used for Israel’s rest, and that there was no deliverer, in a word the book had used of the LORD Himself, “who had delivered them out of the hands of all their enemies on every side” ([[Judg. 8:34]]), and not the word it uses for its judges.[@oshb-wlc] I read the two sentences together.

So Moses’ sentence can be read back over the book, and I do that here as my own reading. The book begins by measuring Israel by the LORD’s eyes and giving the land its quiet, and it ends with every man right in his own eyes and the last quiet in the book a Canaanite town’s. Read by Deuteronomy 12, the end of Judges looks like the wilderness again. I take this to mean that Israel, in the land where Joshua says the LORD gave it rest ([[Josh. 21:44]]), was living as though it had not yet come to the rest. Hebrews draws its conclusion, “There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God” ([[Heb. 4:9]]), from the Psalm and from Joshua, and Joshua’s page follows it there; on this reading, Judges shows why it was needed.

## The LORD the Judge

Does the book say whose eyes should have been the measure? Once, in a speech. Jephthah, arguing with the king of Ammon, ends his case with a sentence that gives the book’s own title to God: “the LORD the Judge be judge this day between the children of Israel and the children of Ammon” ([[Judg. 11:27]]). The Hebrew sets the verb beside the title, *yišpōṭ YHWH haššōpēṭ*, and the title is the one that every judge in the book carries.[@oshb-wlc] The speech says nothing of eyes. What it does is hand a dispute about right and wrong, “I have not sinned against thee, but thou doest me wrong” ([[Judg. 11:27]]), to the LORD to decide, with the verb used here of deciding between two parties at war. To call the LORD “the Judge” is to name whose judgment settles what is right, and that is the measure the formula of 2:11 kept and the refrain lost. That step is mine; the Church’s footnote takes the next. The footnote at “Judge” sends the reader to the Topical Guide’s “Jesus Christ, Judge,”[@lds-scriptures] and that entry lists, among its many lines, Jephthah’s sentence, “Lord the Judge be judge this day, Judg. 11:27,” and Isaiah’s, “shall he judge the poor, Isa. 11:4.”[@tg-jesus-christ-judge]

Of the one Isaiah describes there, whom the Church’s chapter heading calls “the stem of Jesse (Christ),”[@lds-scriptures] he says: “he shall not judge after the sight of his eyes, neither reprove after the hearing of his ears: But with righteousness shall he judge the poor” ([[Isa. 11:3–4]]). Samson judged after the sight of his eyes in the plainest sense, a woman seen at Timnath; the refrain gives every man the same measure, his own judgment of what is right; and Isaiah describes the Judge’s judging as not after the sight of His eyes. When He came, He said in His own voice what His measure was: “I can of mine own self do nothing: as I hear, I judge: and my judgment is just; because I seek not mine own will, but the will of the Father which hath sent me” ([[John 5:30]]).

Setting Isaiah 11 and John 5 beside Judges is my doing, and I have found no source that does it; what the passages say about the Savior is theirs and the Church’s. What the comparison shows is this. The measure Judges loses, an eye outside the man that decides what is right, is not an abstraction. It is a person, the LORD the Judge of Jephthah’s sentence, the premortal Christ (see the note at [[Ex. 3:14]]), whose judging Isaiah describes by what it is not. Schade reads the whole book as “a prophetic call to acknowledge the kingship of the Lord, the true King and Judge of Israel,”[@schade-broken-laws] and Gideon had said it in one line: “the LORD shall rule over you” ([[Judg. 8:23]]). A Church seminary manual says the same of the last chapters and names what was forfeited: Israel “didn’t realize they could have made the Lord their king and that He would have given them peace.”[@seminary-ot-study-guide-judges-17-21]

Deuteronomy ties the two halves of this essay together in one verse. Doing “that which is right and good in the sight of the LORD” is the condition on which Israel would “go in and possess the good land” ([[Deut. 6:18]]), and Deuteronomy 12:8–10 puts every man’s own eyes on the far side of the rest. Judges adds the negative: the book of a people who, on this reading, lived as though they had never arrived, written so that the reader can see where they turned aside.

## Whose eyes decide

The refrain outlived Judges as a proverb. “Every way of a man is right in his own eyes: but the LORD pondereth the hearts” ([[Prov. 21:2]]) has the refrain’s own words, *yāšār bĕʿênāyw*.[@oshb-wlc] The counsel that answers it is Solomon’s: “Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding… Be not wise in thine own eyes: fear the LORD, and depart from evil” ([[Prov. 3:5–7]]).

The Lord described a like condition in His own words when He opened the Doctrine and Covenants: “every man walketh in his own way, and after the image of his own god” ([[D&C 1:16]]). I read Micah as that sentence drawn as a portrait, and the reading is mine: a man who walked after the image of his own god, silver dedicated to the LORD “to make a graven image and a molten image” ([[Judg. 17:3]]), with a priest of his own hiring, sure that the LORD would do him good. The Church’s footnote at “own” in that verse points to Isaiah: “we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all” ([[Isa. 53:6]]).[@lds-scriptures]

So the question Judges puts to a disciple is the one it put to Israel, and it is not whether to do right but whose eyes decide what right is. Solomon’s counsel is for trust in the LORD rather than reliance on one’s own understanding, and it keeps the measure the book loses. The one who keeps it stands under the Judge who does not judge by the sight of His eyes. And the rest Judges shows Israel living as though it lacked is, on this essay’s reading, of a piece with the rest Hebrews says remains to the people of God: the one is the land’s quiet, the other the Lord’s own rest, into which Alma says a person enters through mercy: “whosoever repenteth, and hardeneth not his heart, he shall have claim on mercy through mine Only Begotten Son, unto a remission of his sins; and these shall enter into my rest” ([[Alma 12:34]]).

And the way of one’s own eyes, which Judges records at a nation’s length, is not left where the book leaves it. Isaiah’s verse says what became of such a way, and the step from Judges to that verse is mine. Every one turned to his own way, and the LORD laid the iniquity of all of them on Him, the Lord the Topical Guide calls Judge. The eyes that should have been Israel’s measure belong to the One who bore what Israel did when it measured for itself.
