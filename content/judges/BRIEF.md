## What a chapter is for here

Judges is 618 verses in 21 chapters: an opening on the land not taken and the cycle (1:1–3:6), the stories of the judges (3:7–16:31), and two closing stories (17–21). The reader's questions: *What is a “judge,” and why does the LORD keep raising them up for a people who keep falling away? Why does Judges 1 say Israel did not drive out the Canaanites, after Joshua said the land was taken? Was Ehud's deception and killing of Eglon approved? How could a woman judge Israel, and what is a “prophetess”? Was Jael right to kill a guest? Was Gideon wrong to ask for signs, and why cut the army to three hundred? Why did he refuse to be king and then make an ephod that became an idol? What is Jotham's fable about? Did Jephthah really sacrifice his daughter, and why is he praised in Hebrews? What does “Shibboleth” mean? Who was the angel of the LORD, and why was his name “secret”? What was a Nazarite, and was Samson's strength in his hair? Why does the Spirit of the LORD come upon a man like Samson? Why does Micah make an idol from silver dedicated to the LORD, and who was the Levite? Why did Dan move north? What happened at Gibeah, why did the Levite cut up the woman, and how could Israel nearly destroy a tribe and then take wives for it by force? What does “no king in Israel” mean, and is the book arguing for a king?* (The `christ` section then applies the chapter to the reader, in statements: `STANDARDS.md` §1.)

**Say what the story is doing before what it means.** The book tells its own frame: Israel “did evil in the sight of the LORD,” the LORD gave them to an enemy, they “cried unto the LORD,” He “raised up a deliverer,” and the land had rest until the judge died (2:11–19; 3:7–11). The Bible Dictionary calls the middle of the book “a succession of periods of rebellion against God, and the oppressions and deliverances by which they were followed” (`bd-judges` [1]). A note says first where the episode sits in that frame (the evil, the oppression, the cry, the deliverer, the rest, the relapse), then what it says, then how scripture and the Church's sources read it.

**The book judges its judges; a note follows the text and the sources, not a verdict of its own.** The Church's sources are frank: Samson “failed to fulfill his divine calling because of selfish and unrighteous choices” (*Scripture Helps* [1]); Gideon's ephod “perhaps unintentionally, led the people into idolatry” (`bd-gideon`); Jephthah's was a “rash vow” (`bd-jephthah`). Schade warns that if “the judge rather than God becomes the focal point,” the reader meets “moral confusion” (`schade-broken-laws` [8]); Haws notes that the narrator ascribes nothing in Judges 8 to the Lord's direction (`haws-better-way` [12]). **Proposed:** a note says what the judge did, what the text says of it (often nothing), and the Church's reading; it does not praise a judge the sources criticize or condemn one beyond them. Hebrews 11:32 is explained where it is cited, not used to clear a judge.

**The short judges and the lists get a thread, not a note per name.** Shamgar (3:31), Tola and Jair (10:1–5), Ibzan, Elon and Abdon (12:8–15) and the unconquered towns (1:27–36) are explained in the chapter's `thread`, with a note only where a reader stops (Jair's thirty sons on thirty asses, 10:4; Jerusalem and the Jebusites, 1:21). Chapters 10 and 12 may be short; `setting` may fall under the checker's word count; ignore the warning. **Don't pad.** A place that matters is linked to the guide *The Land* (`#places`, `#inheritance`) or the period guide's map, not retold.

**Graphic passages** (Adoni-bezek, 1:6–7; Eglon, 3:21–22; the tent nail, 4:21; Succoth, 8:16; the tower of Shechem, 9:49; Jephthah's daughter, 11:34–40; Samson's wife burned, 15:6; the blinding, 16:21; the concubine, 19:22–30; Benjamin and Jabesh-gilead, 20:43–48; 21:10–12; the women of Shiloh, 21:19–23): say what the text says plainly and briefly; describe rather than quote (`STANDARDS.md` §3).

## Divisions

The Bible Dictionary gives three parts: “(1) an introduction (Judg. 1:1–3:6); (2) the history of the Twelve Judges … (3:7–16:31); (3) two narratives, which specially show the tendency to idolatry and lawlessness (Judg. 17–21)” (`bd-judges` [1]); *Scripture Helps* has the same three [6–9]. The *Old Testament Minute* covers only 1–8 and 13–16 and gives no divisions. **Proposed:** five divisions, the Bible Dictionary's three with the middle split at Gideon's death and Samson's birth, where the stories change: deliverers who bring rest (Othniel to Gideon); Gideon's house and the judges after him, where the rests stop and the LORD says “I will deliver you no more” (10:13); Samson, the one judge given a birth story. The introduction ends at 3:6, inside chapter 3; chapter 3 goes with the judges, and its `setting` says that 3:1–6 closes the introduction. The titles are the King James' words (2:10; 3:9; 10:13; 13:5; 17:6 and 21:25).

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named, with its paragraph number. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, which later scripture takes up which line, which verse a source might help) is the drafter's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **`when`.** The book gives years of oppression and rest, not dates (3:8, 11, 30; Jephthah's “three hundred years,” 11:26; Samson's “twenty years,” 15:20). The Bible Dictionary: the compiler “generally gives his figures in round numbers,” and “two judges might hold office at the same time” (`bd-judges` [2]). **Proposed:** a chapter's `when` is from the text (“After the death of Joshua,” “In the days of Shamgar” (5:6), “When the Philistines ruled Israel,” “When there was no king in Israel”); no B.C. year, and the figures are never added up into a chronology. The volume guide *The Conquest, the Judges, and One Kingdom* gives the one dating the Church's pages offer; chapter 1's `setting` may point there in a clause. The appendix's own clues (Phinehas, 20:28; Moses' grandson, 18:30) may be named where they answer when 17–21 happened.
- **Names of God.** Copy any phrase with LORD, Lord or GOD from `data/kjv/judges.json` (6:22 and 16:28 have “Lord GOD”). The LORD of Judges is the premortal Christ: said where it answers a question, in a clause, pointing to `[[Ex. 3:14]]`.
- **Hebrew only where it answers a reader's question** (`STANDARDS.md` §3). Words that pay: *šōpēṭ*, “judge” (2:16; Schade's note [146], Shannon [27], the manual 22-9 [30]); *môšîaʿ*, “deliverer” (3:9, 15; `[[Obad. 1:21]]` on the site); “covereth his feet” (3:24, Smoot's note 3 on chapter 3); “Jerubbaal” and Gideon’s name (`ot-minute-judg-6` [10]); *ʾelef*, “thousand,” where numbers puzzle (8:10, `ot-minute-judg-8` [5]); *ʿōlâ*, “burnt offering,” in the vow (11:31); “Shibboleth” (12:6, BDB 7641); *pilʾî*, “secret” (13:18); Lehi, “jawbone,” and En-hakkore (15:17–19, `ot-minute-judg-15` [5]); “played the whore” and the Septuagint's “departed” (19:2; the guide has it). No verse of Judges divides differently in the Hebrew. No more than about a quarter of a chapter's notes are Language notes.
- **The Joseph Smith Translation.** The Church's footnotes have one JST reading in Judges, at 2:18 (“for the Lord hearkened because of”; `fn2.txt`, 18a), and Nyman confirms it is the only one: “The single change in Judges” is 2:18, which “illustrates a change in the Lord’s actions based on the condition of the people” (`nyman-jst-historical` [10]). It is beside the verse, and *Scripture Helps* [13] points to its Genesis 6:6 section. **Proposed:** the note at 2:18 is short: the LORD's change answers Israel's groaning; point to `[[Gen. 6:6]]`, and cite Nyman for what he says the change shows. No other note uses the JST.
- **Scripture reads Judges.** The New Testament: Gideon, Barak, Samson and Jephthah among the faithful ([[Heb. 11:32]]; the Bible Dictionary on why Jephthah is named, `bd-jephthah`); the birth of Samson as an “annunciation scene” (`ot-minute-judg-13` [5]) beside [[Luke 1:13–17]]. The Book of Mormon: Nephi's bands quote Samson's (Judg. 15:14 at [[1 Ne. 7:18]], KnoWhy #410 [28]); the “flaxen cord” of [[2 Ne. 26:22]] beside 15:13–14 and 16:9 (KnoWhy #551); the cycle beside Helaman 12 (manual 22-10 [32]) and, as Smoot's own suggestion, the “pride cycle” (`ot-minute-judg-2` [10]); the priests of Noah's abduction of the Lamanite daughters ([[Mosiah 20:1–5]]) beside Shiloh (`goff-plagiary` [53]; `goff-stealing`); Mormon's Desolation ([[Morm. 3:4–16]]; 4:1–5) beside the war on Benjamin (`sc-evidence-302`: the three battles, “sore battle,” the oath, “cut off”); Nephi's people wanting a king (KnoWhy #717 [11–12], quoting Goff on 19:1). Where scripture quotes a passage, say so; an echo someone identifies is that author's. **No comparison panel, and nothing is written for a Book of Mormon chapter** (`AUTHORING.md`).
- **Joseph Smith on Judges.** Schade quotes him on Samson (“may my power cease, like the strength of Samson, when he was shorn of his locks,” [58], from History E-1, p. 1778) and the First Lecture on Faith's use of Heb. 11:32 [56]. Read both in the Joseph Smith Papers before quoting (`jsp-lectures-faith-1` is proposed; the History page is not yet confirmed).
- **Other books on the site and the volume guides.** Besides the table, `occurrences.mjs --site all` finds what the site says (Joshua 13, 15, 19, 24; Numbers 6, 21; Deuteronomy 2, 12, 13; Genesis 16, 19, 32; Psalms 78, 83, 106; Isaiah 9; Hosea 9, 10; Obadiah 1:21; Habakkuk 3:7): read and agree. The guides: *The Conquest, the Judges, and One Kingdom* (`#rulers`, `#nations`, `#map`; its rows already summarize each judge), *A Timeline of the Old Testament* (`#judges`), *Women and Marriage* (`#the-women`, `#hard-passages`), *The Land* (`#places`, `#inheritance`, `#regions`), *The Temple and Worship* (`#tabernacle`, `#priests`, `#worship`). Point in a clause; don't retell.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports an arc a reader would not see chapter by chapter (the judges' downward arc; the vows of 11 and 21; the Levites of 17 and 19 beside Phinehas at 20:28); `propose-themes` and Codex propose the list at the end of the book. No theme page is written until the author chooses it.

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter; point with a `[[reference]]` rather than re-explain; a Witness of Christ does not open by saying what the chapter lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; don't join two verses yourself and call it the text's meaning; application only in `christ`, as statements, never questions or exhortation; **no military commentary** beyond what the reader needs to follow the story; **no moral verdict on a judge in the writer's own words**; **a comparison brought in (Saul's JST beside 9:23, an abuse study beside Jephthah's daughter) says what it shows about this verse, from a source, or is left out**: in chapters 1–11 that was two of the three notes the language pass sent back.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that 21 chapters say the same thing. Quotations were copied from the pages named on 2026-11-03; re-read the page before using one. **Schade's chapter quotes other scholars at length** (Webb, Younger, Conway and others in [12], [19], [34], [53], [57], [62]): cite such a passage as quoted in Schade, never as his words.

- **The cycle and the arc downward (2:11–19; 21:25).** The text gives the cycle; *Scripture Helps* (“This pattern is repeated multiple times throughout the book” [5]), *Come, Follow Me* (the Lord “raised up a deliverer, a military leader called a ‘judge’” [4]) and the manual (22-10 [32]) name it; Smoot sets out its steps and says its purpose “was explicitly said to have been to test Israel’s faith” (2:22; `ot-minute-judg-2` [10]); Schade: the book was “not compiled to condone brutality or idolatry” but to show what happened when covenants were broken [10]. **Proposed:** chapter 2's `thread` explains the cycle in full, with the text's own verbs; each judge's chapter names the steps in a clause. The arc downward is named where the text shows it (2:19, “corrupted themselves more than their fathers”; no rest recorded after Gideon, 8:28; “I will deliver you no more,” 10:13; Samson who only begins to deliver, 13:5) and stated fully once, at `[[Judg. 21:25]]`.
- **Judges 1 beside Joshua.** Smoot: Judges's frame “appears to contradict the historical portrait given in the book of Joshua” (`ot-minute-judg-1` [5]); *Scripture Helps*: “Some of the tribes failed to drive out all the inhabitants” [1]. Joshua's brief settles the rule (side by side, not harmonized), and `[[Josh. 13:1]]` already sets Joshua's two pictures together. **Proposed:** chapter 1's `setting` says what Judges 1 adds (the tribes one by one; “could not” and “did not drive out,” 1:19–36), gives the reasons the text itself gives (a league made, 2:1–3; a test left, 2:20–23; 3:1–4), and points to Josh. 13:1. Smoot's suggestion that 1:1–2:5 “was a later insertion” (`ot-minute-judg-2` [5]) is his, said once at 2:6, if at all.
- **Who compiled Judges, and when.** The Bible Dictionary: “The book was compiled long after the events it records; in 18:30 there is a reference to the captivity of the ten tribes,” with earlier writings worked in (“the Song of Deborah, the parable of Jotham, and some of the utterances of Samson”) (`bd-judges` [2]); Schade: reading Judges through Deuteronomy “does not mean that the book of Judges was written later or in hindsight as an attempt to justify the monarchy” (note 6, [87]). **Proposed:** chapter 1's `setting` says in two sentences what the Bible Dictionary says. **No chapter builds a reading on a Deuteronomist, argues the date, or assigns verses to sources** (Joshua's brief has the same rule). The Song's age (Smoot, “one of the oldest portions of the Hebrew Bible,” `ot-minute-judg-5` [5]; KnoWhy #853) is said once, in chapter 5's `setting`.
- **“No king in Israel … right in his own eyes” (17:6; 18:1; 19:1; 21:25).** *Scripture Helps*' endnote 2: the last episodes “highlight Israel’s lack of unity in this era and may be implying the need for a king”; the Bible Dictionary: the lack of unity “is vividly called to the reader’s attention in the closing sentence” ([23]); the manual: “Nothing in the stories show the Israelites doing what was right” (23-12 [26]). The text's counterweight is Gideon's “the LORD shall rule over you” (8:23; manual 22-24 [90]) and Jotham's fable. **Proposed:** 17:6 says what the refrain means in the text (each his own judge; Deut. 12:8 gives the phrase its law); 21:25 says the book ends on it and gives *Scripture Helps*' “may be implying,” with 8:23 beside it. **No note says the book argues that kingship is the cure**, only that the sources read the line as pointing toward the monarchy that follows.
- **Ehud (3:15–30).** The text calls him the LORD's “deliverer” (3:15) and tells the killing without comment. Smoot: “By using misleading language, Ehud caused Eglon to temporarily drop his guard,” in a story “rich with deep irony and humor” (`ot-minute-judg-3` [6–7]); the Bible Dictionary: “by a feat of valor” (`bd-ehud`). **Proposed:** 3:20 says what Ehud said (a “message,” *dāḇār*, word or thing, from God) and did, that the text gives no verdict, and Smoot's reading as his; 3:24 explains the euphemism once, plainly. No note calls the deceit righteous or sinful (the same rule as Rahab's, `[[Josh. 2:4]]`).
- **Deborah and Jael (4–5).** Deborah is “the only female judge mentioned in the Old Testament,” and a prophetess is “a woman who has received a testimony of Jesus and enjoys the spirit of revelation” (*Scripture Helps* [14], from `gs-prophetess`). Shannon: “she is the only judge in the entire book of Judges to whom prophetic gifts are ascribed” and quotes the Guide's “A prophetess does not hold the priesthood or its keys” [26]; she alone judges cases (4:5) [27]; “mother in Israel” rests on “her active role in leading, judging, and delivering Israel” [31]. The manual's “Deborah did not direct Israel in any official sense” (22-14 [58]) is older; follow *Scripture Helps*. **Jael:** the Bible Dictionary gives the two accounts (asleep in 4:21, drinking in 5:24–27, `bd-jael`); Smoot: Deborah's word that the honor would go to a woman is fulfilled (`ot-minute-judg-4` [6–7]). **Proposed:** 4:4 goes where *Scripture Helps* stops (Shannon; the caution about keys once); 5:7 explains “mother in Israel” from Shannon; 4:21 says plainly what Jael did, that the song blesses her (5:24), and that she broke a guest's trust in a war Deborah had foretold a woman would end (4:9); no note praises or condemns beyond the text. Link *Women and Marriage* (`#the-women`).
- **Gideon (6–8).** The sign: *Scripture Helps* [16] (“he sought for a sign in faith,” with Oaks) is beside 6:11–24; Haws asks whether the repeated signs are “cautionary” [11]; Smoot: the call “parallels Moses’ prophetic commission in Exodus 3–4” (`ot-minute-judg-6` [8]). The three hundred: the LORD's reason (7:2); the drinking test's purpose “is not clear” (`ot-minute-judg-7` [5]). The ephod: *Scripture Helps* [20], the Bible Dictionary (“perhaps unintentionally”), the manual (22-25 [92]); Smoot, “a rather ambivalent note” (`ot-minute-judg-8` [7]); Haws on Succoth, Penuel and the kings [6–7, 12–13]. **Proposed:** 6:37 gives *Scripture Helps*' distinction in a clause and goes where it stops (the fleece came after the fire from the rock, 6:21, and the Spirit, 6:34, as Haws notes [11]); 7:2 answers from the verse; 8:23 and 8:27 together carry the turn of his story; Haws's questions are his readings, not judgments. Smoot puts Midian “west of the Jordan River” (`ot-minute-judg-6` [5]); the Bible Dictionary makes them “wandering Arab tribes” and 6:33 has them cross the Jordan: don't repeat the slip.
- **Abimelech (9).** The manual reads the fable (the bramble is Abimelech, 22-26 [94]); Haws: “Judges 9 is painful to read” [9]. **Proposed:** 9:8 explains the fable from the text and the manual's reading as the manual's; 9:56–57 gives the text's verdict (“Thus God rendered the wickedness of Abimelech”). Abimelech is not counted among the judges.
- **Jephthah's vow and his daughter (11:29–40).** The most sensitive question in the book. The text: the Spirit of the LORD came upon him (11:29); he vowed that “whatsoever cometh forth of the doors of my house … shall surely be the LORD's, and I will offer it up for a burnt offering” (11:31); his daughter came out; she asked two months to “bewail [her] virginity” (11:37); he “did with her according to his vow,” and “she knew no man” (11:39); the daughters of Israel lamented her yearly (11:40). The text does not say the LORD asked for or accepted the vow. **The Church's sources:** the chapter heading, “He makes a rash vow, which leads to the sacrifice of his only daughter” (`fn11.txt`); the Bible Dictionary, “his rash vow, in consequence of which he offered his own daughter in sacrifice,” commended in Hebrews “probably because of his delivery of Israel from Ammon” (`bd-jephthah` [1]); Litchman, “a foolish vow that inadvertently led to the sacrifice of his daughter” (`litchman-deborah` [22]). The Student Manual grants that “a literal reading of the text may support that view,” then prints Keil's reading that “Jephthah was compelled by his vow to dedicate his daughter to Jehovah in a lifelong virginity” (22-28 [97–98]) and sets Hannah's vow beside it [99]. The law forbids such offerings (Deut. 12:31, which *Scripture Helps* [12] quotes). The guide (`#hard-passages`) already gives both readings, the Bible Dictionary's first. **Proposed:** a note at 11:31 (what the vow's words say, *ʿōlâ* included) and one at 11:39 (what was done): the text; then the chapter heading and Bible Dictionary, plainly, that he sacrificed her; then the dedication reading as the manual prints it, from Keil, with the details it rests on (11:37, 39); then what is not known. Hebrews 11:32 with the Bible Dictionary's “probably.” In `christ`, as theirs: Easton, that such texts “create a biblically sanctioned space to name and discuss abuse” (`easton-recognizing` [45]); Smith, after Beavis, Jairus's daughter in Mark 5 as a “motif inversion” of this story (`smith-redemptive-mark` [10]), the one Christ-centered reading a source gives. **No note says the vow pleased God, that the Spirit of 11:29 sanctioned it, that she was certainly not killed, or that Jephthah was condemned beyond what the sources say; the writer does not choose between the two readings.**
- **Samson (13–16).** His calling is to “begin to deliver Israel” (13:5). The Spirit on him “seems to acknowledge only his remarkable physical strength, which came as a gift from God” (*Scripture Helps* [22]; the manual's “spiritual gifts,” 23-5 [15], agrees). His character: “Samson’s focus frequently drifted toward fulfilling immoral and selfish desires” ([23]); the Bible Dictionary, “weak in intellectual and moral character” (`bd-samson`). His hair was “not the source of his strength. Rather, it was the symbol of his covenant relationship with God” ([24]; manual 23-9 [22]). His last prayer was “self-centered,” and “it is possible that the Lord granted it to demonstrate His power over Dagon” ([25]); Smoot, “more a prayer for vengeance,” granted as “a final act of mercy,” and Samson “only fulfilled his divine mission … by giving up his own life” (`ot-minute-judg-16` [9]). Delilah: the text “does not necessarily portray her as deliberately attempting to seduce Samson” (`ot-minute-judg-16` [5]); she “betrayed him” (`bd-delilah`). **Proposed:** notes beside *Scripture Helps* (14:6; 16:17; 16:28) go where it stops: the Nazarite's rules broken one by one as the text shows them (the carcass, 14:8–9; the feast, 14:10; the razor, 16:17: check against Num. 6 before saying so; no scheme of the writer's); “the LORD … sought an occasion against the Philistines” (14:4; Smoot on 14 [5]); the hair growing again (16:22) with *Scripture Helps*' “symbol” (Smoot's “since his hair had regrown” [9] makes it a cause: *Scripture Helps* leads). 16:30 says plainly that he died with the Philistines; **no note calls it suicide or martyrdom**; no source read does. Joseph Smith's comparison may stand at 16:19–20.
- **The angel and the “secret” name (13:17–22).** Smoot: one of the visitors “was God Himself” (`ot-minute-judg-13` [6]); at Gideon's call the angel is “(i.e., Jehovah)” (`ot-minute-judg-6` [9]); the Church's footnote sends “secret” to Gen. 32:29, which `[[Gen. 32:24]]` on the site already joins. The word is *pilʾî* (BDB 6383), kin to *peleʾ*, “Wonderful,” of `[[Isa. 9:6]]` (BDB 6382). **No Latter-day Saint source read makes a Christ reading of 13:18.** **Proposed:** 13:18 says the visitor is the LORD (Smoot; the Genesis and Exodus notes), that the name is withheld as at Peniel, and what the word means; it may say, as language, that Isaiah uses the same root, if BDB's page joins them, and stops there. **No chapter builds a reading of “Wonderful” as Christ in the writer's words**, and the flame of 13:20 is not made a type.
- **Micah, the Levite and Dan (17–18).** The Bible Dictionary: local sanctuaries were common, “sometimes in private hands, as in the case of Micah” (`bd-judges` [23]); to use teraphim “was probably not to worship strange gods but to worship the true God in a corrupt manner” (`bd-teraphim`); the ephod here “may possibly … have been used to denote an image” (`bd-ephod`); Dan could not hold its land (`bd-dan`). **Proposed:** 17:3 shows the irony from the text (silver dedicated “unto the LORD” made an image; 17:13); 17:10 says what a Levite was for (the guide, `#priests`) and that this one served for hire; 18:30 gives the priest's line and the Bible Dictionary on “the captivity” (the King James “Manasseh” and the Hebrew's raised letter: a lead, to check in the WLC before saying so).
- **The Levite's concubine and Gibeah (19).** The text: the men of Gibeah demanded the Levite; the host offered his daughter and the concubine; the Levite put her out; she was abused through the night and found dead at the door (19:22–28). The site already tells it (`[[Hos. 9:9]]`), and the guide quotes 19:25 (`#hard-passages`): don't quote it again. The sources: the manual, “samples of Israel’s worst days” (23-12 [26]) and Clarke on the twelve pieces as a summons (23-13 [30]); Schade, that the book's portrayal “is not an acceptance or tolerance of the unacceptable but a condemnation of the deplorable (Judges 19–21)” [10]; Easton [45]. **Proposed:** say plainly and briefly what was done; describe, don't quote; show the Levite's part as the text does (he “brought her forth,” 19:25; “Up, and let us be going,” 19:28); set Gen. 19 beside it in a clause (`[[Gen. 19:5]]`, `[[Gen. 19:8]]`: agree). 19:29 gives Clarke's reading (relayed by the manual) as one older reading, and 19:30 Israel's own verdict. **No note dwells on the abuse, speculates about the woman's guilt (19:2 is explained once, as the guide does), or excuses the Levite or the host.** A `christ` section may state, from Schade and Easton, that the book records these things to condemn them.
- **The war on Benjamin and the wives (20–21).** The text: Israel asks the LORD three times and is told to go up (20:18, 23, 28), loses twice, wins the third time; six hundred of Benjamin are left (20:47); two oaths (21:1, 5) lead to the destruction of Jabesh-gilead, four hundred young women taken (21:10–14), and the daughters of Shiloh seized (21:19–23). The manual: wives provided “although not in a righteous manner” (23-12 [28]). **Proposed:** each episode in a clause, plainly; 20:27–28 explains the ark and Phinehas (the one place the ark is named in Judges, `bd-judges` [23]); the LORD's answers are stated, not explained away or made into approval of chapter 21; violence points to `[[Josh. 6:21]]` and the appendix. Jephthah's vow and the oaths of 21 are joined only if a source read joins them.
- **Judges as types of Christ the deliverer.** *Come, Follow Me* [28]: the pattern of Judges 3 (“did evil,” “cried unto the Lord,” “raised up a deliverer”) “teaches about the Savior’s power to deliver us from sin”; `[[Obad. 1:21]]` on the site reads the “saviours” through 3:9. **Proposed:** a `christ` section may say, with *Come, Follow Me*, that the Deliverer the judges point to is the Lord who raises them, through His mercy in the cycle (2:16, 18; 10:16); the judges themselves are not made types of Christ in the writer's words (not Samson at the pillars, not Gideon's lamps, not Jephthah's daughter).

## Settled without the author

Decided by the standard, not asked of the author (Psalms' brief has the rule):

- **No Church, BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* source answers a reader's real question, and an older commentary does:** give its answer as “one older reading,” within the limit of a third of the notes. If the question isn't one a reader would ask, there is no note. The studies cluster on 2–8 and 13–16; chapters 9–12 and 17–21 have no *Old Testament Minute* and no *Scripture Helps* and will need this most.
- **A scholar's reading that an RSC, BYU or *Interpreter* publication prints**, or that the *Old Testament Minute* reports (Alter on the type scene and 3:24; Younger, Webb, Block and Conway in Schade), is cited as that publication's author's, or as quoted in it, beside the Church's, never above it.
- **Two Church sources read a verse differently** (Jephthah: the Bible Dictionary and the manual): give both, the newer and the Church's own first, without choosing; where *Scripture Helps* speaks, it leads.
- **Judges and Joshua tell the land differently:** say what each says, side by side; a reason only from a source read.
- **A source quoted inside a Church manual** whose work can't be read (Rasmussen; Cassel in Keil) is cited as the manual; Keil and Clarke, whom it quotes, may be read on Bible Hub and cited as themselves.
- **The Church's footnote points somewhere the note does not go:** leave it.
