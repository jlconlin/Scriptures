## What the author has decided

Nothing for Ecclesiastes in particular beyond choosing it. The standing rules are in `STANDARDS.md` §1 and `AUTHORING.md`. How three land on this book:

- **Newest trumps oldest.** *Scripture Helps: Old Testament* (2025; `scripture-helps-proverbs`) gives Ecclesiastes four sections: what the book is [29], its three parts [30]–[33], “under the sun” and “vanity” at 1:2, 14 [34], bread upon the waters at 11:1, with Elder Gong [35], and the goads of 12:11 [36]. The Student Manual (`ot-manual-proverbs`, 2-46 to 2-60) is older; where they differ, follow *Scripture Helps*.
- **Don't replicate the Gospel Library.** Those four sections are beside the verse, and so is the Bible Dictionary's entry, which the Student Manual quotes whole. A note at 1:2, 1:14, 11:1 or 12:11 goes where they stop (the study an endnote cites, the Hebrew, the scripture that takes the verse up), or it is not written. Chapters 4–10 have no *Scripture Helps*.
- **Quote the Church's modern material sparingly.** The Bible Dictionary's reading of “under the sun” and of 9:5, 10 is the key to the book and will be wanted in many chapters: it is said in the site's words, quoted in a phrase at most, once in full at the place the table names, and pointed to elsewhere. *Come, Follow Me* (`cfm-ot-2026-37`, Sept. 7–13) is what the Church asks a reader to see, not commentary.

## What a chapter is for here

Ecclesiastes is 222 verses in 12 chapters. It is neither a story nor a collection: one voice, “the Preacher,” reasons in the first person about what lasts, tries things and reports what he found, and breaks off into proverbs; a second voice speaks of him in the third person at the start and the end (the seminary guide: “In Ecclesiastes 1:1–11 and 12:8–14, the text refers to the preacher in the third person,” `seminary-intro-eccl` [5]). The reader's questions: *Who is the Preacher, and is he Solomon? What does “vanity” mean, if not conceit? What is “under the sun”? Is the book as hopeless as it sounds, and why is it in the Bible? Does 9:5 mean the dead know nothing? Is “eat, drink and be merry” his advice, and how is it different from the false teachers of 2 Nephi 28? Does 12:7 teach that the spirit lived with God before birth? What do the images of 12:1–7 stand for? Did someone else write the ending? Why does he say one thing and then the opposite (2:2 and 7:3; 8:12–13 and 8:14)? Why “be not righteous over much” (7:16)? What is “a time to every purpose” (3:1–8) for, and what does 3:11 mean by “the world in their heart”?* (The `christ` section then applies the chapter to the reader, in statements: `STANDARDS.md` §1.)

**The chapter divisions cut across the argument.** The Preacher's sections do not end where the chapters do (the poem of times runs 3:1–8 and its reflection to 3:15; the King James 5:1 is 4:17 in the Hebrew, and `hebrew.mjs` prints both). Each chapter's `setting` says where it picks up the Preacher's argument and what it carries forward; `thread` follows the argument within the chapter, the way a discourse is followed in Proverbs 1–9. **Proposed:** say what the Preacher is testing in the chapter (pleasure and works, 2; the times God sets, 3; oppression and toil, 4; riches, 5–6; what wisdom can and cannot do, 7–8; death and chance, 9; the fool, 10; youth and old age, 11–12), what he concludes, and where he turns toward God in it (2:24–26; 3:11–17; 5:1–7, 18–20; 7:13–14; 8:12–13; 11:9; 12:1, 13–14).

**Chapters 5, 7 and 10 are partly proverbs** (the Student Manual: 5:1–17 “in the form of brief proverbs or wise sayings,” 2-49; chapter 10 “primarily a collection of proverbs,” 2-54). Where a run of separate sayings stands, follow Proverbs' brief (“Chapters 10–29”): sections group the sayings the text joins, `thread` says honestly that this is a string of sayings and what recurs, and only the sayings a careful reader stops at get a note.

**Graphic images** (the stillborn, 6:3–5; the unburied): describe rather than quote at length (`STANDARDS.md` §3).

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, which scripture takes up which line) is the drafter's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **`when`.** The text dates nothing. **Proposed:** `when` says “The Preacher, speaking as a son of David, king in Jerusalem” (chapter 1), “The Preacher's reflections” (2–11), and “The Preacher's last words, and the epilogue” (12). No B.C. year.
- **No Context link** (`noPeriod` in `book.yaml`), as for Proverbs: the book has no historical setting of its own in the text. The volume timeline lists Ecclesiastes under the united kingdom (`/old-testament/guides/israels-story/#united-kingdom`), and the intro links it; a chapter that needs Solomon's reign points there in a clause.
- **The speaker.** Call him “the Preacher,” as the King James does; give *Qoheleth* once, at 1:1. Don't call him Solomon in running text: the text never names him, and the Church's sources say “traditionally” (“Contested”). Where he speaks as a king (1:12–2:11), say “the Preacher, speaking as king.”
- **Names of God.** Ecclesiastes says “God” (*'elohim*) throughout and never the LORD's name (check with `occurrences.mjs ecclesiastes "LORD"`, which should find none). A note says so only where it answers a question; it does not make the book's God less than Israel's.
- **The Preacher's own voice is not doctrine** (the Bible Dictionary: 9:5, 10 “are observations by the Preacher about how things appear to men on the earth ‘under the sun’”; the seminary guide: “although the preacher is a believer, he often poses questions and makes statements as if he were not,” `seminary-intro-eccl` [6]). So a note says *what the Preacher concludes*, and, where he says something the Restoration answers (the dead, the end of a man and a beast, the uselessness of toil), it says what scripture teaches beside him without saying the Preacher was wrong. A reflection is paraphrased as his reflection (“he concludes,” “it seems to him”), not as a fact about the world.
- **Hebrew only where it answers a reader's question** (`STANDARDS.md` §3). Words that pay: *Qoheleth*, the Preacher (1:1; the Bible Dictionary; Pike [18], “Assembler” and “Teacher”); *hebel*, “vanity” (1:2; KnoWhy #868, `sc-knowhy-868`; Barlow, “mist” or “vapor,” “a puff of air,” `barlow-public-relations` [19]; the footnote at 1:2: “IE empty, fleeting, unsubstantial”); *yitron*, “profit” (1:3; check BDB); *re'ut ruach*, “vexation of spirit” (1:14; Pike [35]: “now often rendered as ‘striving after wind’”); *'olam* at 3:11 (“the world,” where modern translations read “eternity”: read the translations, `nrsvue`, `niv`, `esv`, and BDB 5769, and say what changes); *ruach* at 3:19–21; 11:5; 12:7 (Pike [25]–[42]). Not: the Hebrew behind an ordinary English word, or counting “vanity.” No more than about a quarter of a chapter's notes are Language notes.
- **The Joseph Smith Translation.** The Church's footnotes have no JST reading in Ecclesiastes (`fn1.txt`–`fn12.txt`). McConkie says “There was only one change in Ecclesiastes (3:1), and it is of little consequence” (`mcconkie-poetic-writings` [9]). No note on it unless a writer reads the manuscript itself (the Joseph Smith Papers) and a reader would ask.
- **Scripture reads Ecclesiastes.** The Church's own footnotes are the first finder: 1:3 points to [[Matt. 16:26]] and [[2 Ne. 9:51]] (“labor for that which cannot satisfy”). Beside the Preacher's search: the rich fool ([[Luke 12:16–21]], who says “eat, drink, and be merry,” 12:19) beside 2:18–21 and 5:13–15; treasures in heaven ([[Matt. 6:19–21]]) beside 5:10–15; “If in this life only we have hope in Christ, we are of all men most miserable” ([[1 Cor. 15:19]]) beside the book's “under the sun”; [[D&C 130:18–19]] beside 7:12 (the Student Manual: the Preacher “comes close to the truth” there, 2-51); “the sun ariseth on the evil and on the good” ([[Matt. 5:45]]) beside 9:2, 11 (Barlow [25]); Jacob's “our lives passed away like as it were unto us a dream” ([[Jacob 7:26]]) beside *hebel* (Barlow's comparison [20]–[21]); the false teachers' “Eat, drink, and be merry” ([[2 Ne. 28:7–8]]) at 8:15 (“Contested”). Where scripture says what a passage means, say so as scripture's reading; an echo an author identifies is that author's. **No comparison panel, and nothing is written for a Book of Mormon chapter** (`AUTHORING.md`).
- **Proverbs and the other books on the site.** *Come, Follow Me* sets the two books side by side: Proverbs says that if you seek God's wisdom “life will go well,” and Ecclesiastes “seems to say, ‘It's not that simple’” [4], yet “they teach similar truths” [5]. Where a chapter meets Proverbs (the proverbs of 7 and 10; the fool; 5:1–7 and the fear of God), it points to the Proverbs note and does not repeat it. Proverbs 22:6's note already names Ecclesiastes among the canon's counterweights to reading a proverb as a promise: agree. `occurrences.mjs ecclesiastes "<phrase>" --site all` finds the rest (Psalms 39, 49, 73, 90; Isaiah 57:11 already quotes 8:11). The volume guides: *Hebrew Poetry* (`#parallelism`), *A Timeline of the Old Testament* (`#united-kingdom`).
- **The `christ` section.** Ecclesiastes has no prophecy and no messiah, and no Church source read reads a figure in it as Christ. **It does not read anything in the book as a type of Christ** (not the “one shepherd” of 12:11 unless a Church source does, and then as that source's). It says where the chapter's question meets Jesus Christ from what scripture and the Church's sources say: what lasts beyond “under the sun” is answered by His Resurrection and judgment ([[1 Cor. 15:19–22]]; [[John 5:22]]; [[Alma 40:11]]; [[Moses 1:39]]), and the Preacher's “gift of God” (3:13; 5:19) beside the Savior's “I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly” ([[John 10:10]]), worded as comparisons. Then one to three plain sentences of application. Chapter 1's is the place to say once, in a sentence, that the book asks the question the gospel answers (the Student Manual: “there is meaning only through God and keeping His commandments,” 2-58); don't say it in every chapter.
- **No theme pages** (author, 2026-10-24: “No theme pages”). Fable's and Codex's candidates (what is “for ever,” *'olam*; the forgotten told to remember; “thou knowest not”; judgment; the portion) had real arcs inside the book, but each reached Christ only by setting another book's words beside it (author: “It sounds like all the Christ pointing in these themes comes from other books”), the weak kind `STANDARDS.md` §8 warns against. The book asks the question the gospel answers, and the chapters' `christ` sections carry that.

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter; don't re-explain what another chapter explains, point with a `[[reference]]`; a Witness of Christ does not open by saying what the chapter lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer, since the site shows it alone as the note's card; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; “In plain words” adds nothing to the verse; application only in `christ`, as statements resting on what it cites, never questions or exhortation; **no self-help commentary** (the Student Manual's sequence of life events at 3:1–8 and its “loving to work” at 5:12 are applications to a modern life: a note says what the verse meant and how scripture and the Church read it); **don't answer the Preacher in every note**: the gospel's answer belongs in the `christ` section and at the notes the table names, not appended to each reflection.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that 12 chapters say the same thing. Quotations were copied from the pages named on 2026-10-24; re-read the page before using one.

- **Who is the Preacher, and when (1:1, 12, 16; 2:4–9; 12:9).** The Church's sources: the Bible Dictionary, “The author describes himself as ‘son of David, king in Jerusalem’ (1:1)”; *Scripture Helps*, “a man called the Preacher, who is traditionally thought to be King Solomon” [29], citing study Bibles (endnotes 20–22, not readable); the seminary guide, “Little is known about the writer of Ecclesiastes beyond his own description of himself,” and the third person in 1:1–11 and 12:8–14 “may suggest that someone other than the preacher compiled his writings and teachings sometime after they were given” [4]–[5]. The Latter-day Saint studies: Pike, Solomon's “name is never actually mentioned in Ecclesiastes, some passages argue against Solomonic authorship, and the style and language of the book are generally seen as deriving from later in Israelite history,” so scholars “tend to attribute the authorship … to some unknown individual living five to seven centuries after Solomon” [18], and Seely and Ludlow “imply it is unlikely that Solomon wrote Ecclesiastes” (his note 17); Breitenstein, that the Preacher “was Solomon” (`ot-minute-ecclesiastes` [4]). **Proposed:** chapter 1's `setting` and the note at 1:1 say what the text says (a son of David, king in Jerusalem, never named), that the Church's sources call Solomon the traditional author, and Pike's point about the language and the name as his, in a sentence. **No chapter argues a date**, and none reads 1:12–2:11 as Solomon's biography: the king's experiment is the Preacher's.
- **Is the book as bleak as it sounds, and why is it scripture?** The Bible Dictionary: it “seems permeated with a pessimistic flavor but must be read in the light of one of its key phrases: ‘under the sun’ (1:9), meaning ‘from a worldly point of view,’” and “vanity” means “‘transitory’ or ‘fleeting’”; “The most spiritual part of the book appears in chapters 11 and 12.” *Scripture Helps*: the Preacher “emphasizes the futility of life without God,” and “trusting in God enables us to accept both our successes and our adversity” [29]. The Student Manual: “the Preacher is speaking from the viewpoint of a man without God,” and “The purpose of Ecclesiastes is not to grind us down between futility and despair but to help us remember that there is meaning only through God” (2-58). KnoWhy #868: “doing the right thing does not always work out the way one might hope, but we ought to do the right thing anyway” [8], with Christofferson's “cosmic vending machine” [9] (`christofferson-relationship-god`: read the talk's own page before quoting it). Barlow: it may serve, like Job, “as a balancing corrective” to the assurance that God “will dependably prosper the righteous and punish the wicked in visible, material ways in mortality” [25]; it “cannot be the last word for us” [28]. McConkie: “Neither of these books espouses gospel principles or contains so much as a word of prophecy” [8]. **Proposed:** follow the Bible Dictionary and *Scripture Helps* (the frame is “under the sun”; the end is God's judgment). The book's honesty about injustice and death is said as the text says it, not softened; the corrective reading (Barlow, the KnoWhy) is given where the Preacher sees the righteous suffer (7:15; 8:14; 9:2, 11). McConkie's judgment is not repeated (Proverbs' brief does the same).
- **“Eat, drink, and be merry” (2:24; 3:12–13, 22; 5:18–20; 8:15; 9:7–9).** The text: the Preacher calls it “from the hand of God” (2:24) and “the gift of God” (3:13; 5:19), and joins it to fearing God (8:12–13) and to judgment (11:9). *Scripture Helps*: in 11–12 “The Preacher invites everyone to enjoy life as a gift from God” [33]. Breitenstein reads 2:24 beside “Men are, that they might have joy” ([[2 Ne. 2:25]]) [30]. The Book of Mormon's false teachers say “Eat, drink, and be merry, for tomorrow we die; and it shall be well with us,” and “nevertheless, fear God—he will justify in committing a little sin” ([[2 Ne. 28:7–8]]); the rich fool says it in [[Luke 12:19]]. Allis-Pike's “Competing Depictions” (on 2 Ne. 28:8) is not readable online. **Proposed:** the note at 2:24 says what the Preacher means (enjoyment received from God in one's labor, not instead of it); the note at 8:15 sets 2 Nephi 28:7–8 and Luke 12:19 beside it from the texts and says what differs in the words (the Preacher's joy is God's gift, with judgment to come, 11:9; Nephi's teachers use the phrase to excuse “a little sin”). **No note says Nephi was quoting Ecclesiastes** unless a source read says so.
- **“The dead know not any thing” (9:5, 10; also 3:19–21).** The Bible Dictionary: these “should not be construed as theological pronouncements on the condition of the soul after death; rather, they are observations by the Preacher about how things appear to men on the earth ‘under the sun.’” The bibliography lists Joseph Fielding Smith, “What Is the Meaning of Ecclesiastes 9:4, 5, 10?” (*Improvement Era*, Sept. 1963), not found online. **Proposed:** the note at 9:5 gives the Bible Dictionary's reading in its own words (a short quotation is the Church's position), then what Restoration scripture teaches of the dead ([[Alma 40:11–14]]; [[D&C 138]]), worded as what scripture teaches, not as a correction of the Preacher. 9:10 points.
- **“The spirit shall return unto God who gave it” (12:7; 3:21).** The Church's sources read it of the spirit: the chapter heading, “At death the spirit will return to God who gave it”; the seminary guide, “one day we will die, our spirits will return to God” [11]; the Student Manual (2-59) quotes President Harold B. Lee, “Obviously we could not return to a place where we had never been,” so the verse teaches premortal life (1973); Pike lists apostles who cite the verse (Faust 2004, Nelson 2012; his note 3) and the Topical Guide and Guide to the Scriptures, which list it under premortal life [15]. Breitenstein: “at the end of the book, the Preacher acknowledged that our spirits would live on and return to God” [57]. Pike (BYU) reads the Hebrew otherwise: *ruach* is singular and in 3:19 is the “one breath” men and beasts share, and in the Old Testament it “rarely occurs … to designate a spirit being” [42]; the verse means the dust returns to the earth and “a divinely originating rûaḥ, a life breath or life force, leaves the body,” “perhaps the Light of Christ” [47]; “the biblical verse itself (12:7) does not teach that doctrine. This does not detract from the reality of spirit persons created by God and of the premortal existence of these spirits. It just means that support for such realities must be sought elsewhere” [48]. **Proposed:** the note at 12:7 gives first how the Church's sources read the verse, President Lee's words as a prophet's teaching; then that the Hebrew word is the same *ruach* that 3:19 calls the “one breath” of men and beasts, and that a Latter-day Saint scholar reads the line as the breath of life returning to God, as his reading; then the doctrine of premortal life from the scriptures that teach it plainly ([[Abr. 3:22–23]]; [[D&C 93:29]]; [[Moses 3:5]]), which neither reading questions. **The note does not decide between the two readings, and does not say the Church's sources are wrong.** **The author decided it** (2026-10-24, of having both readings: “Just in the note is fine”): both readings in the note, the Church's first; Pike is not added to `explore`. 3:21 points to 12:7.
- **The images of old age (12:1–7).** The Student Manual has nothing; Breitenstein reads them as old age (“senses would be dimmed,” the “grinders” as teeth, the “long home” as death) and the broken bowl and pitcher of 12:6 as water spilled, with [[2 Sam. 14:14]] [56]–[57]; Pike, “the long, dark days of misery encountered in old age and the inevitability of the grave” [20]. **Proposed:** one note for 12:2–6 says the passage is a picture of old age and death, and gives the readings of single images (the keepers, the grinders, the almond tree, the silver cord, the golden bowl) only as far as a source read gives them, with how certain each is; Delitzsch may be the only source for several, “one older reading.” Don't give every image a meaning; say plainly where the sources differ or are silent. The “silver cord” is not explained as a cord between spirit and body unless a Church source says so.
- **The epilogue (12:8–14).** *Scripture Helps*' endnote 23: “Many scholars believe that verses 9–14 in Ecclesiastes 12 were a later addition to Ecclesiastes,” citing Alter (not readable); Pike, “What follows in 12:8–14 is not presented as the Teacher's words” [21]; the seminary guide on the third person [5]; Barlow calls it the summary of “the second author” [24]. The Church's reading of the book rests on 12:13–14 (the Bible Dictionary; the Student Manual: “This one verse gives meaning to the entire book,” 2-60; *Come, Follow Me* [5]). **Proposed:** the note at 12:9 says the voice changes to speak of the Preacher, and that the Church's own study aid reports the scholars' view that 9–14 were added; the note at 12:13 gives the conclusion its full weight as the book's own last word and the Church's key to it. **No note uses the epilogue's origin to discount 12:13**, and none says the conclusion contradicts the Preacher; the book as the Church has it ends there.
- **Contradictions (2:2 and 7:3; 4:2 and 9:4; 8:12–13 and 8:14).** The Preacher sets one observation against another. **Proposed:** say what each says and what the text implies by placing them together, as Proverbs' brief does for 26:4–5; a reason only from a source read. Don't harmonize them yourself.
- **“Be not righteous over much” (7:16–18).** No Latter-day Saint source read comments on it. **Proposed:** a note, because a reader will stop there: what the verses say in their context (7:15, the just man who perishes in his righteousness), what “the fear of God” in 7:18 adds, and readings only from a source read (a modern translation, Delitzsch as one older reading). No note makes it permission for moderation in commandments.
- **The poem of times (3:1–8).** The Student Manual applies it to the sequence of a young man's life (mission, marriage, schooling, 2-47), and Breitenstein to agency and order [32]. **Proposed:** the note at 3:1 says what the poem says in its place (the times are God's, 3:11, 14, and a man cannot change them, 3:14–15); the Student Manual's application is not the verse's meaning and is left out or given as an application, once.

## Settled without the author

Decided by the standard, not asked of the author (Psalms' brief has the rule):

- **No Church, BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* source answers a reader's real question, and an older commentary does:** give its answer as “one older reading,” within the limit of a third of the notes. The Latter-day Saint studies cluster on 1–3 and 11–12 (*Scripture Helps*, *Old Testament Minute*, Pike) and on the book as a whole (Barlow, KnoWhy #868, the Bible Dictionary). **Chapters 4–10 will lean on the text, the scriptures that take it up, the Church's footnotes and the older commentaries**, and need fewer notes; a short chapter resting on the text is better than one filled with the lexicon.
- **A scholar's reading that a BYU or Scripture Central publication prints** (Longman and Fox in Pike, Lowell Bennion in Barlow) is cited as that publication's author's, as one reading, beside the Church's, never above it.
- **Two faithful sources read a verse differently** (12:7; the authorship): give both, the Church's first, without choosing beyond what the standard settles.
- **The Church's footnote points somewhere the note does not go:** leave it.
