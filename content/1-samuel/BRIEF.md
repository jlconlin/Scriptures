## What a chapter is for here

1 Samuel is 810 verses in 31 chapters: a story told in scenes, with few speeches and almost no commentary from the narrator. The reader knows Hannah, the boy Samuel, Saul, Goliath and Jonathan, and asks grown-up questions. Those a careful reader would ask, by chapter (a start, not a limit):

- **1–3:** *Who wrote this, and when? Was Elkanah a Levite or an Ephraimite? Why two wives? What vow did Hannah make, and was Samuel a Nazarite? Why does Eli think she is drunk? What did Eli's sons do with the meat, and what was Eli's fault? What is “his anointed” (2:10) before there was a king? Who is the “faithful priest” (2:35)? Why was the word “precious”? How old was Samuel?*
- **4–7:** *Why take the ark into battle? What did losing it mean (Ichabod)? Who was Dagon, and what were the “emerods”? Why golden mice? Why were the men of Beth-shemesh struck, and were there really 50,070? How could Samuel, not a priest, offer sacrifice (7:9)? What is Ebenezer?*
- **8–12:** *Was asking for a king wrong, when Deut. 17:14 allows one? What is a seer? Why was Saul chosen, and what is “captain” (nāgîd)? What does it mean that God gave him “another heart,” and that he prophesied? Why did he hide among the stuff? What did Nahash want with right eyes? Why does Samuel defend his own record?*
- **13–15:** *How old was Saul (13:1)? What was wrong with his offering, when he waited the seven days? Why did the Philistines control the smiths? What was Saul's oath, and was Jonathan guilty? Why destroy Amalek, children and animals too? What did Saul do wrong if he killed nearly all of them? Does God repent (15:11, 29, 35)? What does “to obey is better than sacrifice” mean?*
- **16–18:** *Why David, and what does “the LORD looketh on the heart” mean? What is the evil spirit “from the LORD,” and what does the Joseph Smith Translation say? Why does Saul not know David after the battle (17:55) when David was already his armourbearer (16:21)? How tall was Goliath? What did Jonathan's love and covenant mean? Why a hundred foreskins?*
- **19–23:** *What image did Michal put in the bed? Why does Saul prophesy naked at Naioth? What covenant did David and Jonathan make, and with what sign? Was eating the shewbread lawful? Why did David pretend to be mad? Why did Saul kill the priests of Nob, and who was Doeg? How did David “enquire of the LORD” with the ephod?*
- **24–26:** *Why would David not touch “the LORD's anointed”? What was the skirt of the robe? Was David running a protection racket with Nabal? Who was Abigail, and what did she say that turned him? Why did David marry her and Ahinoam?*
- **27–31:** *Did David lie to Achish, and kill whole villages? Was the woman of Endor a witch, and did Samuel really appear? What does the Joseph Smith Translation change? Why did the Philistines send David away? How did Saul die, and how does that fit 2 Samuel 1? Why did Jabesh-gilead rescue the bodies, and why burn them?*

A chapter answers those from the text first. (The `christ` section then applies the chapter to the reader, in statements: `STANDARDS.md` §1.)

**Read the story as a story.** The narrator rarely judges; he arranges. Hannah's song and David's psalm (2 Sam. 22) frame the whole (Olsen [174]). The “asked” of Hannah's naming (*šāʾal*, 1:20, 27–28) is the sound of Saul's name (Bowen [34]); Saul is the king the people “asked” (8:10; 12:13). The one who “looketh on the heart” (16:7) chose Saul too, “a choice young man, and a goodly” (9:2). The Spirit comes on Saul (10:6, 10; 11:6) and leaves him as it comes on David (16:13–14). The kingdom is “rent” from Saul as he tears Samuel's skirt (15:27–28), and David cuts Saul's skirt (24:4). Say what a repetition shows where a source shows it; don't build a scheme of the writer's own.

**Thirty-one chapters, many shared things.** Each is explained once, at the place this brief names; the other chapters point to it. Chapters 4–6, 11–12, 21–23 and 27–31 are where readers know least. Don't pad the chapters *Scripture Helps* already covers.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Quotations were copied from the page named, with its paragraph number; the rest is the drafter's direction of where to look. Nothing goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **`when`.** The book gives no years. The period guide dates Saul's accession about 1095 B.C. from the Bible Dictionary's chronology (`bible-chron-ot`), and the *Old Testament Minute* puts the narrative of both books “somewhere between 1100 and 961 BC” (`ot-minute-1-samuel-1` [4]). **Proposed:** `when` is from the text (“In the days of Eli, at Shiloh”; “After the ark had been twenty years at Kirjath-jearim,” 7:2; “When Saul had reigned two years,” 13:1; “While David fled from Saul”), with no B.C. year; chapter 1's and 8's `setting` may give the guide's date in a clause, as the guide's.
- **Names of God.** Copy any phrase with LORD or GOD from `data/kjv/1-samuel.json`. “LORD of hosts” is at 1:3; chapter 1's `setting` points to `[[Hag. 1:2]]` for it (no source read says it first appears here: don't say so). That the LORD is the premortal Christ is said where it answers a question, in a clause, pointing to `[[Ex. 3:14]]`.
- **Hebrew only where it answers a reader's question** (`STANDARDS.md` §3). Words that pay: *šāʾal*, “asked,” and the names Samuel and Saul (1:20, 27–28; Bowen [34]–[36]: Samuel is probably “his name is El,” and Hannah's “asked” fits Saul's name); *māšîaḥ*, “anointed” (2:10; footnote 2:10b: in the Septuagint *christos*); *bǝlîyaʿal*, “Belial” (2:12; Judd [9]); *kābôd*, “glory,” and Ichabod (4:21–22); *nāgîd*, “captain” (9:16; 10:1; 13:14); *nāḥam*, “repent” (15:11, 29, 35; footnote 15:11b: “The Hebrew root means ‘to sigh,’ therefore ‘to feel sorrow’”); *ḥērem*, “utterly destroy” (15:3; point to `[[Deut. 7:2]]` for the word); *bǝrît*, “covenant,” and *ḥesed*, “kindness” (18:3; 20:8, 14–15; Taggart [21]); *nābāl*, “fool” (25:25; Heal [18]); *ʾôb*, “familiar spirit” (28:3, 7–9; point to `[[Lev. 19:31]]`); *ʾĕlōhîm*, “gods,” at 28:13. No more than about a quarter of a chapter's notes are Language notes.
- **The Joseph Smith Translation.** The Gospel Library prints it at 15:11, 15:35, 16:14–16, 16:23, 18:10 and 19:9 (`church.md` §0.1 has the wording, and `jst-1-sam-16` the appendix page). Nyman (`nyman-jst-historical`) counts fourteen changes in 1 Samuel, six of them in chapter 28, which the Gospel Library does not print; give those as Nyman's and Jackson's (`jackson-restoring-doctrine`) report of the manuscript. **The Student Manual cites the correction of “repenting” as “JST, 1 Samuel 15:29”; the footnotes give 15:11 and 15:35: cite those.**
- **Large numbers.** 6:19 (50,070), 11:8 (330,000), 13:5 (30,000 chariots), 15:4. The Church's sources say so where they do (*Scripture Helps* 22 [13]: “Most scholars agree that the figure of 30,000 Philistine chariots is inaccurate,” 3,000 in the Septuagint; the Student Manual on 6:19), and the Student Manual's enrichment section (`ot-manual-large-numbers`) gives the general case. A note gives the figure as the text has it and what the Church's source says, never a figure of the writer's own.
- **Scripture reads Samuel.** Jesus cites the shewbread (`[[Matt. 12:3]]`–4); Paul's sermon at Antioch runs from Samuel to Saul's forty years to David (`[[Acts 13:20]]`–22, “a man after mine own heart”); Hannah's song and Mary's (`[[Luke 1:46]]`); Samuel the Lamanite's name and calling (KnoWhy #576); the sword of Laban beside Goliath's (KnoWhy #256, #411; `[[1 Ne. 4:18]]`); Mosiah 29 on kings. Where scripture quotes or names Samuel, say so; an echo someone identifies is that author's. **No comparison panel.**
- **Other books on the site and the volume guides.** Besides the table, the guides: *The Conquest, the Judges, and One Kingdom* (`#rulers`, where its rows already summarize Eli, Samuel and Saul; `#nations`, the Philistines; `#books`), *A Timeline of the Old Testament* (`#judges`), *Women and Marriage* (Hannah, Abigail, Michal), *The Land*, *Temple and Worship* (the ark, Shiloh). Point in a clause; don't retell.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports an arc a reader would not see chapter by chapter (the “asked” king and the chosen one; the Spirit coming and going; the anointed whom David will not touch; the rent garment); `propose-themes` and Codex propose each book's list with its last batch. No theme page is written until the author chooses it.
- **The older commentaries, after chapters 1–12** (2026-11-04). Where the Latter-day Saint sources are silent (4–7, 9–11), the first batch leaned on Keil and the Cambridge Bible: 10 of 12 notes in chapter 7 and 7 of 9 in chapter 10 cite one. ## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that thirty-one chapters say the same thing. Quotations were copied from the pages named on 2026-11-04; re-read the page before using one.

- **Who wrote Samuel, and when (1:1; 10:25; 25:1).** The Bible Dictionary: “In the Hebrew Bible these books form one. Our division into two books follows the Greek Bible”; “It is uncertain who the author was or when he wrote”; he used “the state chronicles (among which were writings by Samuel, Nathan, and Gad, 1 Sam. 10:25; 1 Chr. 29:29)” (`bd-samuel-books`); the seminary manual says the same (`ot-seminary-1-samuel-intro` [5]–[6]). Samuel's death is told at 25:1, and 2 Samuel carries on after it. **Proposed:** chapter 1's `setting` says the two books were one, that the writer is not known, that the Church's dictionary names the records of Samuel, Nathan and Gad among his sources, and that the books cover about 130 years (`bd-samuel-books`). The theory of a Deuteronomistic history (Dundas relays it) is not argued in any chapter. **No chapter treats the people of the book as other than real.**
- **The text of Samuel (10:27–11:2; 13:1; 13:5; 17:4; 1:23).** Samuel's Hebrew text is the least settled in the Old Testament. Parry: one Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel (4QSam-a) “has almost five hundred variant readings different from the Masoretic text, or Septuagint,” though “only 10 percent” of it survives (`dss-roundtable-2007` [57]); the *Encyclopedia of Mormonism*: the three Samuel scrolls “are much fuller texts than those of the Masoretic Bible” (`eom-dead-sea-scrolls`). The Church's own sources use the scrolls at two places: Goliath's height (*Scripture Helps* 23 [4]: “Some ancient texts, such as the Septuagint and the Dead Sea Scrolls, list Goliath's height as four cubits and a span—around 6.5 feet”; KnoWhy #856 reads the shorter height as a rebuke of Saul, his own height, `sc-knowhy-856` [3]–[5]: its reading) and Nahash (KnoWhy #855: an “extra verse at the end of 1 Samuel 10” that “helps make more sense of the condition Nahash required”; Strathearn [72]–[73]: the NRSV prints it; the ESV omits it). Julie M. Smith on 1:23: the scroll's “Only may the Lord fulfill what your mouth has uttered” “makes more sense in context” (`smith-hannah-temple` [13]). 13:1 is defective in Hebrew (the King James' “Saul reigned one year” renders it): no source read discusses it; a note may say the Hebrew lacks the numbers, as modern translations show, citing them. **Proposed:** a note gives the King James text first, then the other reading where a source in the hierarchy gives it, as that source's; **the writer never prefers a variant in his own voice**, and the Book of Mormon's closeness to the King James is not raised.
- **Elkanah's line and Samuel's priesthood (1:1; 7:9; 9:13; 10:8; 16:2–5).** 1:1 calls Elkanah “an Ephrathite” of mount Ephraim; 1 Chr. 6:27–28, 33–34 put Elkanah and Samuel among the Levites. *Scripture Helps*: “Samuel's lineage is unclear” (endnote 49) and, from Joseph Smith's “all the prophets had the Melchizedek Priesthood,” “Because Samuel held the Melchizedek Priesthood, he was authorized to offer sacrifices and perform other duties that were typically reserved for Levite priests” (`scripture-helps-ruth-samuel` [26]). The Bible Dictionary: “Though not a priest, he performed priestly functions and constantly offered sacrifice at various places” (`bd-samuel`). Olsen: “Samuel's dual descent may have qualified him, in terms of his lineage, to serve as priest as well as prophet and judge” (`olsen-birth-samuel` [140] n. 16); Millet gives the Prophet's statement (`millet-prophets-priesthood` [47]). **Proposed:** 1:1 says what the verse says and what Chronicles says, and that the Church's newest source leaves the lineage open; 7:9 carries *Scripture Helps*' answer (beside it: go where it stops, with the Prophet's words from `millet-prophets-priesthood` and the Bible Dictionary's sentence). **No note calls Samuel a high priest** (the *Old Testament Minute: 2 Samuel* does, [23]: not in the text).
- **“His anointed” before there was a king (2:10).** The Bible Dictionary: Hannah's song “contains the first reference to the title Messiah (‘his Anointed’)” (`bd-hannah`); the footnote: in the Septuagint “the noun is *christos*”; Julie M. Smith: “the first time in the Old Testament that the word is used to refer to someone that will be sent by the Lord in a saving capacity,” and “possible to read this part of the praise song as the exercise of a prophetic gift on Hannah's part” (`smith-hannah-temple` [24]). **Proposed:** 2:10 gives the word, the Bible Dictionary's sentence and Smith's reading as hers; `christ` of chapter 2 says it plainly. Peninnah (1:6): Spendlove reads “adversary” as Hannah's own trouble, not her rival (`spendlove-hannah-peninnah` [201]); BDB lists a separate noun “rival wife.” Give his reading as his, beside the King James, if 1:6 has a note; the text's “her adversary also provoked her sore” stands.
- **Beth-shemesh (6:19).** The text: “he smote of the people fifty thousand and threescore and ten men”; the footnote at “looked” points to Ex. 19:21. The Student Manual relays Rasmussen: “It is not a proper Hebrew expression for 50,070. The ‘fifty thousand men’ appears to be an added phrase, or gloss. The septuagint and Josephus both have merely ‘seventy men’” (`ot-manual-1-samuel-1` [79]), and Keil on “looked” as looking “with lust or malicious pleasure” [80]. **Proposed:** 6:19 says what the men did (looked into the ark, footnote, Num. 4:20 on the site), and gives the manual's note on the number as the manual's. No note decides how many died.
- **Asking for a king (8; 10:19; 12).** Deut. 17:14 allows a king “like as all the nations” (on the site: agree). *Scripture Helps*: Samuel was troubled “not necessarily because having a king was wrong, but more likely because of their reason for wanting one” (`scripture-helps-1-samuel-8` [4]); KnoWhy #153: “the moral was not necessarily that kingship was or is inherently evil” [12]; the Student Manual relays Keil: the desire “was not in itself at variance” with Israel's calling, “yet the motive … was both wrong and hostile to God” (`ot-manual-1-samuel-1` [86]–[88]); Dundas: the elders wanted better justice than Samuel's bribe-taking sons gave (`dundas-kingship` [85]). **Proposed:** 8:5 and 8:7 give the Church's reading: kingship was allowed; the motive, “like all the nations,” and the rejection of the LORD as king were the fault (8:7; 12:12); Elder Holland at most once. The Book of Mormon's verdict on kings (Mosiah 29) in `explore`.
- **Saul's offering (13:8–14).** Samuel had said seven days (10:8), and Saul waited “according to the set time” (13:8). *Scripture Helps*: “Saul did not have the priesthood authority required to perform burnt offerings … This act demonstrated that Saul's heart was not in line with the Lord's will as it had once been” [14–17]; the Student Manual quotes Talmage that his crown gave “no right to officiate even as a deacon” (`ot-manual-1-samuel-1` [127]–[129]); Ellsworth: he offered “as a means to hold people to him” (`ellsworth-tragic-saul` [13]). **Proposed:** 13:9 says what he did and why the text says he did it (13:11–12: the people scattered, the Philistines gathered, “I forced myself”); 13:13 gives *Scripture Helps*' two reasons. **No note says Samuel was late or unfair** in the writer's voice; the text says Samuel came “as soon as he had made an end of offering” (13:10).
- **Amalek and the ban (15:1–33).** The command (15:3) is the most troubling verse in the book. *Scripture Helps*: “We do not know all the reasons why God commanded Saul and his army to destroy all the Amalekites and their animals. However, we do know that God cares deeply for all of His children and that He takes ‘no pleasure in the death of the wicked’” (`scripture-helps-1-samuel-8` [18]–[19]; it points to the Gospel Topics entry “Religion vs. Violence,” not yet read: read it before writing 15:3); the seminary manual: the Amalekites were “a murderous people and … enemies of the Lord” (Deut. 25:17–19); Calabro: “Saul's crucial error was not that he failed to kill the king and the livestock, but that he retained them” (`calabro-herem` [200] n. 33), and the ban texts show “hagiographic hyperbole”: Amalekites are still fighting David in chapter 30 [142]. **Proposed:** 15:3 follows Deuteronomy's approach (its brief, “The herem”): the Church's reading first, the text's own reason (15:2; Ex. 17:14 on the site), Calabro's as his, then what is not known; it neither defends nor condemns beyond its sources. 15:9 and 15:21–22 say what Saul kept and why that was the disobedience. **No chapter says the command was not given or that Samuel misheard God.** Elder Hales's “selective obedience” at most once, at 15:22.
- **Does God repent? (15:11, 29, 35).** The King James says the LORD repented (15:11, 35) and that he “is not a man, that he should repent” (15:29). The Joseph Smith Translation (Gospel Library footnotes): at 15:11, “he repenteth not that he hath sinned”; at 15:35, “the Lord rent the kingdom from Saul.” The footnote at 15:11: the root means “to sigh,” “to feel sorrow.” *Scripture Helps* points to Gen. 6:6, where the word “can indicate regret, sorrow, compassion, or ‘a decision to change a course of action’” (`scripture-helps-gen-6-11` [9]); Nyman: “The Lord had indeed changed his pattern, but his previous course had not been erroneous” (`nyman-jst-historical` [10]). **Proposed:** 15:11 carries it: the word, the footnote, the JST, *Scripture Helps*' sense, and 15:29 as the chapter's own answer; 15:35 points.
- **The evil spirit “from the LORD” (16:14–23; 18:10; 19:9).** The JST: “an evil spirit which was not of the Lord troubled him” (16:14; “not of God,” 16:15–16, 23; 18:10; 19:9). *Scripture Helps*: “The Joseph Smith Translation clarifies the evil spirit … was not from the Lord. While Saul may have been directly influenced by an evil spirit, it is also possible that he was experiencing a spirit of discontent because of his sins” [28]; Nyman: “God does not send evil spirits” [13]; the Student Manual: “Recorded here are the first effects of Saul's rejection of the Lord” (`ot-manual-1-samuel-16` [19]); the footnote at 18:12 points to D&C 121:37. **Proposed:** 16:14 gives the King James, the JST, and *Scripture Helps*' two readings side by side; the other verses point. **No note diagnoses Saul** (depression, madness) in the writer's voice; Tanner's “a cultural and linguistic reference used to describe the madness” (`ot-minute-1-samuel-18` [8]) is his, if used at all.
- **David introduced twice (16:14–23; 17:55–58).** In chapter 16 David is Saul's harpist and armourbearer; in 17:55 Saul asks whose son he is. The Bible Dictionary: the writer “has combined together two different accounts of the same event and has not always been careful to reconcile the two (compare … 1 Sam. 16:14–23 and 17:1–18:5)” (`bd-samuel-books`); the *Encyclopedia of Mormonism* notes “different stories of his introduction at Saul's court” (`eom-david-king` [5]); Tanner: “several years had passed” (`ot-minute-1-samuel-17` [22]). **Proposed:** 17:55 gives the Bible Dictionary's statement first, Tanner's as his; no harmonizing scheme of the writer's own.
- **David and Jonathan (18:1–4; 19:1–7; 20; 23:16–18; 2 Sam. 1:26).** The text: “the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul” (18:1); “Then Jonathan and David made a covenant” (18:3); Jonathan gives him his robe, sword and bow (18:4); “thou shalt be king over Israel, and I shall be next unto thee” (23:17). The Church's sources read loyalty and covenant: *Scripture Helps*: he “showed his friendship and his support of David becoming the next king”; Elder Holland: “To a lesser man—or a lesser friend—than Jonathan, David would have been a terrible threat, a natural rival” (`holland-real-friendship`); the seminary manual: “Jonathan covenanted to warn David … and David covenanted to watch over Jonathan's family” (lesson 88 [37]); Hopkin: the gifts “cement their covenant relationship” (`hopkin-covenant` [11]); Taggart: David's *ḥesed* to Jonathan (`taggart-sworn-david` [21]); Matson: the covenant “is binding on the offspring” (`matson-amos-kinship` [60]). **No faithful source read takes up modern romantic or sexual readings of the friendship** (all three dossiers looked). **Proposed:** the notes say what the text says and what the covenant was (its terms, 20:14–17, 42; its keeping, 2 Sam. 9:1, 7; 21:7), and give *ḥesed* its sense from the sources. The heir apparent giving the future king his robe and weapons is the point the sources make: say it. **No note raises the modern reading, argues against it, or hints at it**; a writer who finds a source in the hierarchy that addresses it reports it, and the coordinator decides.
- **Lies and raids (21:2, 13; 27:8–12).** David deceives Ahimelech (21:2), feigns madness (21:13), and tells Achish he raided Judah while he destroyed whole towns and left “neither man nor woman alive” to tell (27:9–11). No Church source read comments. **Proposed:** say what the text says and what it gives as his reason (27:11, “Lest they should tell on us”), and that the deception at Nob cost the priests their lives (22:22, “I have occasioned the death”); no verdict and no excuse in the writer's voice.
- **Endor (28:3–25).** The text: Saul had put away the mediums (28:3); the woman sees “gods ascending” (28:13); “Saul perceived that it was Samuel” and “Samuel said to Saul” (28:14–15). The sources do not agree, and the brief does not choose between the Church's own two. The Bible Dictionary: “It is certain that a witch or other medium cannot by any means available to her bring up a prophet… Either Samuel came in spite of and not because of the witch, or some other spirit came impersonating him” (`bd-samuel`); the Guide to the Scriptures: the scene “could not have been a vision from God, because a witch or other spiritual medium cannot influence a prophet to appear at his or her request” (`gs-samuel`); Joseph Fielding Smith, reprinting Penrose: “Saul did not see the spirit that was called up”; “nothing … establishes the opinion that she was a prophetess of the Lord or that Samuel actually appeared” (`jfsmith-endor-1962`, pp. 566, 600; the Student Manual quotes it, `ot-manual-1-samuel-16` [99]–[102]); the JST, by Nyman's report, has the woman bring up “the words of” Samuel (28:11–12) but adds “And she said, I saw Samuel also” (`nyman-jst-historical` [14]–[15]; Jackson [93]); Ellsworth: “A deceiving spirit, appearing as Samuel” (`ellsworth-tragic-saul` [32]). **Proposed:** 28:7 explains the medium and the law against her (point to `[[Lev. 19:31]]`); 28:12–15 says what the narration says, then the Bible Dictionary's certainty and its two possibilities, the JST as Nyman reports it, and Joseph Fielding Smith's conclusion as his, in that order; `christ` of chapter 28 is about seeking the Lord's word where He gives it (28:6), not about the spirit world. **No note says that Samuel certainly came, or certainly did not**; Davis's Welsh tract (`davis-endor-treatise`) is history, not used.
- **The Church's David.** 1 Samuel's David is the anointed shepherd who will not touch the LORD's anointed, and the Church's sources honor him there (*Scripture Helps* 23 [21]: “the greatest king in Israel's history”). His fall is 2 Samuel's (its brief). **Proposed:** no 1 Samuel note looks ahead to Bathsheba except in `christ` of chapter 17 if *Come, Follow Me*'s question needs it (`cfm-ot-2026-25` [11]); the man after God's own heart (13:14) is explained at 13:14 with `[[Acts 13:22]]`.
- **Christ in 1 Samuel.** The anointed (2:10; 16:13; 24:6), Bethlehem (16:1; *Scripture Helps* 22 endnote 31: “Like David, Jesus Christ was born in Bethlehem”), the shepherd king (16:11; 17:34–35; Taggart [34]; `[[Ps. 23:1]]` on the site), the champion who fights for the people (17; “the battle is the LORD's,” 17:47), and Abigail, “willing to take Nabal's sin upon herself” (Yee, *Scripture Helps* 23 [13]). **Proposed:** each in `christ` or at the note named, in the source's words, after the story's meaning. **No chapter makes the five stones, the sling, the shewbread or the robe a type of Christ in the writer's words.**

## Settled without the author

Decided by the standard, not asked of the author (Psalms' brief has the rule):

- **No Church, BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* source answers a reader's real question, and an older commentary does:** give its answer as “one older reading,” within the limit of a third of the notes. If the question isn't one a reader would ask, there is no note.
- **A scholar's reading that an RSC, BYU or *Interpreter* publication prints** (McCarter in Dundas; Alter in Olsen; Johnson in KnoWhy #856; Levenson in Heal) is cited as that publication's author's, or as quoted in it, beside the Church's, never above it.
- **Two Church sources read a verse differently** (Endor: the Bible Dictionary and Joseph Fielding Smith; Samuel's priesthood: the Bible Dictionary and *Scripture Helps*): give both, the newer first, without choosing; where *Scripture Helps* speaks, it leads.
- **A source quoted inside a Church manual** whose work can't be read (Rasmussen, Clarke, Talmage) is cited as the manual; Keil, whom it relays, may be read on Bible Hub and cited as himself.
- **The Church's footnote points somewhere the note does not go:** leave it.
