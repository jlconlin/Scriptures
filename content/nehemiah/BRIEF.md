## What a chapter is for here

Nehemiah is thirteen chapters (406 verses): a governor's own account of rebuilding a city wall, framed by lists, with a public reading of the law, a long prayer, a written covenant, and a last chapter in which the covenant is broken and enforced. A reader asks: *Who was Nehemiah, what is a cupbearer, and why would a Persian king send one to govern Judah? Why was the wall still down nearly a century after the first return, and why did a wall matter so much? Why did he wait from Chisleu to Nisan? Who were Sanballat, Tobiah and Geshem, and why did they care? Why list who built which stretch of wall? How do you build with a sword at your side? Why were Jews selling their children to other Jews, and what was wrong with usury? Why would Nehemiah not go into the temple to save his life? How was a wall finished in fifty-two days? Why copy the register of Ezra 2 again? What did the Levites do when they “gave the sense,” why did the people weep, and why were they told not to? What were the booths, and how could they not have been kept since Joshua? What does a prayer that retells all of Israel's history do? What did the people promise, and why write it and seal it? Why did a tenth of the people have to be made to live in Jerusalem? Why does Nehemiah keep asking God to remember him, and to remember his enemies? Why did he throw Tobiah's furniture out of the temple, shut the gates on the Sabbath, and pull out men's hair? What has any of it to do with a Church that builds, keeps covenants, pays tithing and keeps the Sabbath?* A chapter answers those from the text first.

**The book's threads.** The Bible Dictionary divides the book in four parts (quoted under “Divisions”), *Scripture Helps* (17–19) in two (“The rebuilding of Jerusalem's walls,” 1–6; “The restoration of the community in Jerusalem and dedication of the walls,” 7–13). *Come, Follow Me* (4): the Lord made a way for His people to return “both to Jerusalem and, more importantly, to their covenants.” Welch (`welch-religious-authority`, 10–12) reads Ezra–Nehemiah as rebuilding religious authority by persuasion and instruction, with “the benevolent hand of God working behind the scenes,” and chapters 8–10 as the climax, where the people's “re-encounter with their own textual tradition” makes scripture's authority “personal and devotional.” **Proposed: two threads.** (1) **The good hand of God on a man who prays and then works** (1:4–11; 2:4, 8, 18, 20; 4:9, 14, 20; 6:9, 16): his enemies end by perceiving “that this work was wrought of our God” (6:16). (2) **A people rebuilt around the law and held to its covenant** (8–10, then 13). The text ties this thread's halves together: the covenant ends “we will not forsake the house of our God” (10:39), and Nehemiah's question on his return is “Why is the house of God forsaken?” (13:11); the marriages (10:30; 13:23–27), the Sabbath (10:31; 13:15–22) and the tithes (10:35–39; 13:10–13) all come back (*lead*: confirm each pairing). A chapter's `thread` says which thread its chapter carries; it does not restate the book.

**The lists are not padding, and not explained name by name** (chapter 3; 7:6–73; 10:1–27; 11:3–36; 12:1–26). **Proposed:** as in Ezra, at most two or three notes a list, on what the list *does*:

- **Chapter 3** shows a whole people at work, many “over against his house” (3:10, 23, 28–30), the high priest's company first (3:1), one refusal (“their nobles put not their necks to the work of their Lord,” 3:5), daughters building (3:12). Ludlow (`ludlow-nehemiah-minute`, 16) says the broad wall of 3:8 is “one of the few archeological sites from that period in the Old City of Jerusalem that is still visible today” (*lead*: it needs its own institutional source, or it is cut). The footnotes gloss the gates; no note per gate; the Student Manual's map (32-5) may be pointed to.
- **7:6–73** is Ezra 2 again (footnote at 7:6), already explained at `[[Ezra 2:2]]`, `[[Ezra 2:63]]` and `[[Ezra 2:64]]`: link. Chapter 7 adds why Nehemiah copied it (7:4–5: a large city with few people, and “my God put into mine heart”) and the JST's corrections (below). Ludlow (36) gives a later rabbinic view that Ezra's list is the first return and Nehemiah's the temple's dedication: a clause at most. The Nehemiah of 7:7 is another man (Bible Dictionary, Nehemiah, sense 2).
- **10:1–27** puts “Nehemiah, the Tirshatha” first (10:1); one note on what sealing a written covenant did (9:38). Ludlow's comparison with temple sealings (49) is not used.
- **11:1–36**: the lot “to bring one of ten to dwell in Jerusalem the holy city” (11:1), the volunteers blessed (11:2), 7:4 the reason; `[[Isa. 49:20]]` already reads it: link. The Student Manual's “one of every ten families in Persia” (32-16) contradicts 11:1 and is not used.
- **12:1–26** runs from Zerubbabel's priests (the “Ezra” of 12:1, 13 is one of them, not the scribe) to high priests after Nehemiah (12:10–11, 22); the Bible Chronology lists Eliashib (453 B.C.), Joiada (413), Johanan (373) and Jaddua (341) (`bible-chron-ot`, 79–90). **Proposed:** one sentence in the setting that the list reaches past Nehemiah's day; nothing on who added it. Iddo (12:16): `[[Zech. 1:1]]`; Abijah (12:4): the footnote's Luke 1:5.

### When: one chronology for all thirteen chapters

The guide [Persia and the Return](/old-testament/guides/persia-and-the-return/#rulers) carries the dates with their sources and already gives Nehemiah's commission as “445 or 444.” A chapter cites the verse and links `#rulers`; it does not retell the history.

- **The twentieth year of Artaxerxes** (1:1; 2:1): Artaxerxes I, “Longimanus. Son of Xerxes and king of Persia, 465–425 B.C.” (Bible Dictionary). The Church's sources differ by a year: *Scripture Helps* (16), the walls rebuilt “in about 444 BC”; the institute manual (66), “in about 444 BC”; the Bible Chronology (80), “444 | Nehemiah appointed governor of Judea”; Enrichment J (J-5, 35), “firmly dated at 445 B.C.”; Ludlow (5) and Keil, 445. **Proposed:** “445 or 444 B.C.” throughout, as the guide says.
- **Chisleu before Nisan in the same year** (1:1; 2:1). The footnote: Chisleu is “the ninth of the Hebrew months, beginning at the new moon of December”; Nisan is the first. Keil (2:5, last resort) explains it by a year counted from autumn (“Tishri is the first, Chisleu the third, and Nisan the seventh month”). **Proposed:** one sentence in chapter 1's or 2's setting, Keil's explanation labelled as such.
- **The wall** was finished on the twenty-fifth of Elul, “in fifty and two days” (6:15); the footnote: Elul is “the sixth Hebrew month, from the new moon of September to that of October.” Don't convert to a modern date without a source.
- **The seventh month** (7:73; 8:2): the reading on its first day, the booths from the fifteenth (8:14), the fast on the twenty-fourth (9:1), which Ludlow (46) notes comes after Tabernacles, unlike the Day of Atonement.
- **Twelve years as governor** (5:14) and **the return to the king** in the thirty-second year (13:6). Bible Chronology (81): “432 | Nehemiah's second mission to Jerusalem. Prophecy of Malachi.” Ludlow (27): governor “around 445–433 BC,” the second term's length “is not specified.” **Proposed:** “433 or 432 B.C.” “Artaxerxes king of Babylon” (13:6) is the Persian king by his title over Babylon (Keil, 13:16): a clause if a reader would stop.
- **The order of Ezra and Nehemiah.** As in Ezra: 458 B.C. and Artaxerxes I. Enrichment J (35): “The date of Ezra's mission is disputed… The scriptures seem to indicate that Ezra's group came to Jerusalem before Nehemiah.” Ludlow (42): their chronology is “notoriously difficult to determine,” but “there are touch points when they seem to have interacted.” The text puts them together at 8:9, 12:26 and 12:36. **Proposed:** chapter 8's setting says that Ezra, who came thirteen years before, now appears beside Nehemiah, and that the order is disputed, in a clause; no other chapter takes it up.
- **Proposed `when`, by chapter:**
  - 1: `'The month Chisleu in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes I (verse 1; 2:1), at Susa; Nehemiah's commission is dated 445 or 444 B.C.'`
  - 2: `'The month Nisan in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes I (verse 1), 445 or 444 B.C., and the first days in Jerusalem.'`
  - 3: `'The building of the wall, 445 or 444 B.C., finished on the twenty-fifth day of Elul (6:15).'`
  - 4: `'During the building of the wall, 445 or 444 B.C., when it was joined together “unto the half thereof” (verse 6).'`
  - 5: `'During the building of the wall, 445 or 444 B.C. Verses 14–19 look back over Nehemiah's twelve years as governor, to the thirty-second year of Artaxerxes.'`
  - 6: `'The wall finished on the twenty-fifth day of Elul, after fifty-two days (verse 15), 445 or 444 B.C.'`
  - 7: `'Just after the wall was finished (verse 1). The register copied in verses 6–73 is that of the first return under Cyrus, about 538 B.C. (Ezra 2).'`
  - 8: `'The seventh month (7:73; verse 2), in the year the wall was finished: the reading on the first day and the Feast of Tabernacles from the fifteenth (verse 14).'`
  - 9: `'The twenty-fourth day of the same seventh month (verse 1).'`
  - 10: `'The covenant of the same assembly, in the seventh month (9:38).'`
  - 11: `'After the wall was finished; the text gives no date.'`
  - 12: `'Verses 1–26 run from the first return, about 538 B.C., to high priests after Nehemiah's day; verses 27–47 tell of the dedication of the wall, which the text does not date.'`
  - 13: `'Verses 1–3 on the day of the dedication (“on that day”). Verses 4–31 after Nehemiah's return from the king in or after the thirty-second year of Artaxerxes (verse 6), 433 or 432 B.C.'`

### The book's make-up (only what a Latter-day Saint or Church source says)

- **One work with Ezra.** Bible Dictionary: “a continuation of Ezra, the two being regarded by the Jews as forming one book.” Chapter 1's setting, once.
- **Nehemiah's memoir.** Welch (4): Nehemiah's memoir is “principally in Nehemiah 1–7.” In the text the first person runs from 1:1 to 7:5, stops for the register and chapters 8–12:30 (Nehemiah in the third person at 8:9; 10:1; 12:26), and returns at 12:31 (“Then I brought up the princes of Judah upon the wall”) to the end. **Proposed:** chapter 1 says where the “I” begins, chapter 8 that it has stopped, chapter 12 that it returns; who joined the parts is not known from any source read, and no chapter speculates.
- **Who Nehemiah was.** Bible Dictionary: “Comfort of the Lord” (Ludlow, 5: “the Lord is compassionate”; *lead*: `hebrew.mjs nehemiah 1:1`); “either a Levite or of the tribe of Judah.” The cupbearer: *Scripture Helps* endnote 25 (“one of great responsibility and trust… The position also allowed access to the king on a regular basis”); already explained at `[[Gen. 40:1]]` (*mashqeh*): link. **Whether Nehemiah was a eunuch:** no Church or Latter-day Saint source read raises it (the Bible Dictionary's Eunuch entry does not name him); **no note raises it.**
- **Shushan**: Bible Dictionary, “Also known as Susa”; the footnote; `[[Dan. 8:2]]`. No note. Uchtdorf (24) and Amistad (7) put Nehemiah “in Babylon”; the site follows the text.
- **Tirshatha** (7:65, 70; 8:9; 10:1): as in Ezra (Bible Dictionary: “The Persian title for a local or provincial governor”); the footnotes gloss it. No note.

### Other conventions

- **“The good hand of my God upon me”** (2:8, 18): already at `[[Ezra 7:6]]` and `[[Ezra 8:22]]`, which cite Neh. 2:8. **Proposed:** one note at 2:8 on what is Nehemiah's own (he asked the king, then credited God); 2:18 points back.
- **Nehemiah's short prayers** (2:4, between the king's question and his answer; 4:9; 6:9): one note at 2:4. Amistad (9): “a prayer of the heart”; a phrase at most.
- **Ezra's chapters** are linked, never retold: `[[Ezra 4:21]]` (the wall stopped; its note quotes Neh. 1:3; 2:5, 8), `[[Ezra 7:6]]` and `[[Ezra 7:10]]` (both cite Neh. 8), `[[Ezra 8:15]]` (8:7), `[[Ezra 9:9]]` (the wall as a figure), `[[Ezra 9]]`, `[[Ezra 10:44]]`.
- **The gates and places** are glossed in the footnotes; no note unless a reader would stop for something they don't answer. `[[Jer. 31:38]]` and `[[Ps. 147:2]]` already read the rebuilt gates.
- **Looking up a Hebrew word.** `hebrew.mjs nehemiah <c>:<v>` takes King James numbers; the Hebrew numbers 4:1–6 as 3:33–38 and 9:38 as 10:1 (tested). Quote BDB paragraphs from Bible Hub, never its page title (Ezra's brief).
- **Theme candidates** (leads only; `THEME-CANDIDATES.md` not yet created): praying and working; the covenant made and kept (10 against 13); the word read and understood (8, with `[[Ezra 7:10]]` and `[[Deut. 31:10]]`); “Remember me, O my God.” Some may belong to Ezra–Nehemiah together.

**Habits to avoid** are listed in Amos's brief and hold here. In Nehemiah especially: **don't retell the return, the kings or Ezra**; **don't make every chapter a sermon on opposition or on “a great work”** (Uchtdorf belongs to chapter 6, once); **don't read the wall as allegory** (a `christ` section may say, as *Come, Follow Me* 4 does, “the spiritual protection we find in Him,” but the notes explain a real wall); **don't read chapter 13 as a lesson on modern marriage, descent or discipline**.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that thirteen chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-11-12.

- **Why the wall, and why still broken (1:3; 2:17).** *Scripture Helps* 20: rebuilding “was essential for safeguarding the city” and “helped reestablish Jerusalem's religious, economic, and political influence,” and neighbors “feared the impact it would have on their power and control.” The Student Manual (32-8): the walls were “an important physical symbol of the establishment of the Jews as a people.” When the wall of 1:3 was last broken no Latter-day Saint source says (Keil reports both views); `[[Ezra 4:21]]` already tells how Artaxerxes stopped it. **Proposed:** one History note at 1:3 linking Ezra 4:21 and saying the text does not date the ruin; *Scripture Helps*' reasons go in chapter 2, in the site's words.
- **The wait and the request (2:1–8).** KnoWhy #862 (`sc-knowhy-862`, 3–7) proposes that Nehemiah waited for Nisan because the Persian new year, Nowruz, fell then (“One common tradition during Nowruz was that the king would give gifts or grant requests to anyone who asked”), and that “sepulchres” and gates “consumed with fire” (2:3) would weigh with a Zoroastrian king; it rests on Fleishman (*JNSL* 34, 2008; not read). Keil: the reasons for the wait “are not stated.” **Proposed:** at most one note at 2:1–3, the KnoWhy's reading given as a plausible hypothesis from one source, with the text's own facts first (he prayed, waited, and was “very sore afraid,” 2:2).
- **Sanballat, Tobiah and Geshem (2:10, 19–20; 4; 6; 13).** *Scripture Helps* 23: “Sanballat was the governor of Samaria. Tobiah was an Ammonite official and an influential member of the community in Jerusalem,” and both feared “their influential positions might be compromised.” Bible Dictionary, Sanballat: “Called the Horonite, a leader of the Samaritan opposition to Nehemiah.” Ludlow (9): all three were “Persian officials,” opposed “primarily because it could mean a loss of their political authority in the region.” No Bible Dictionary entry for Tobiah or Geshem (“Gashmu” at 6:6). Nehemiah's answer is a claim of standing: “ye have no portion, nor right, nor memorial, in Jerusalem” (2:20). **Proposed:** one History note at 2:10 or 2:19 on who they were and why they cared, and 2:20 explained from the text. **As in Ezra: if their worship is the point, describe it as mixed; never call them a race;** the Student Manual's “deep bitterness” (32-4) is not used as a reason. Tobiah's ties inside Judah (6:17–19; 13:4) are told from the text.
- **The armed builders (4).** `[[Ps. 123:4]]` and `[[Ps. 102:14]]` already read 4:2–4. Nehemiah's charge, “fight for your brethren, your sons, and your daughters, your wives, and your houses” (4:14), has the footnote Alma 46:12; the Student Manual (32-6) and Ludlow (21) draw the parallel with Captain Moroni, and KnoWhy #410 lists it (“a text quoted in Nehemiah 4:14 is alluded to in Alma 46:12”; its plates-of-brass hypothesis is not used). **Proposed:** one Restoration note at 4:14 on Moroni's title of liberty. The Kirtland (Ludlow 20) or Nauvoo (Cannon, in the Student Manual 32-6) temple builders under arms: a clause at most, one of the two.
- **The imprecatory prayer and the prayers against enemies (4:4–5; 6:14; 13:29).** “Turn their reproach upon their own head… And cover not their iniquity, and let not their sin be blotted out” (4:4–5). No Church or Latter-day Saint source read comments (Ludlow, 31, only says that in 6:14 “he pled with the Lord to remember these evil deeds”). The site reads such prayers at `[[Ps. 137:8]]` (“The cursing psalms”). **Proposed:** one note at 4:4–5: the words are spoken to God and leave the act to Him, and the builders go on building with a watch set (4:6, 9); link `[[Ps. 137:8]]` for the site's reading and the Savior's higher law, without repeating it. Whether 4:5 echoes `[[Jer. 18:23]]` is a *lead*. Chapters 6 and 13 point back.
- **“Remember me, O my God”** (5:19; 13:14, 22, 31). Ludlow (69) names asking God “for assistance and remembrance” among the book's major themes. 13:22 adds “spare me according to the greatness of thy mercy.” `[[Isa. 38:2]]` already reads Hezekiah's prayer beside 13:31. **Proposed:** one note at 5:19, the first: the refrain asks God to keep the record of a work done for His house and people; it is a prayer, not a boast to the reader. 13:31 points back. No note calls Nehemiah self-righteous, and none defends him against the charge.
- **Usury and the enslaved children (5:1–13).** “We bring into bondage our sons and our daughters to be servants” (5:5). Bible Dictionary, Usury: “In biblical English it often means simply charging interest on a loan. At other times it implies an unduly high rate of interest”; the restriction “seems to be when lending to a fellow church member” (it lists Neh. 5:1–13 with Ex. 22:25, Lev. 25:35–38 and Deut. 23:19–21); “Latter-day revelation does not contain restrictions against interest charges.” Ludlow (24–25): debt slavery after loans, Persian taxes and the work; the redeemed sold back into bondage (5:8); the shaken lap (5:13); and Zion, which has no poor (Moses 7:18). `[[Isa. 62:8]]` and `[[Isa. 50:1]]` already cite 5:3–5. **Proposed:** a note at 5:1–5 and one at 5:7–13 (the law and the oath); Moses 7:18 at most once, as Ludlow's. **Don't** make the chapter a lesson on modern interest; the Bible Dictionary's last sentence is the guard.
- **The governor's bread (5:14–18).** The footnote gives Mosiah 2:14; the Student Manual (32-7): “The righteous kings in the Book of Mormon had the same sense of public morality.” `[[Mal. 1:8]]` cites 5:14–15. **Proposed:** one Restoration note at 5:14–15 with Benjamin's words.
- **Shemaiah and the temple (6:10–13).** *Scripture Helps* 24 answers beside the verse (only priests might enter; Shemaiah “had been hired by Sanballat and Tobiah”). **Proposed:** a note only if it says more: the text's reasons (“Should such a man as I flee?” 6:11; “matter for an evil report,” 6:13), the JST footnote at 6:11 (“mine enemy, that such a man as I would go”), and Num. 18:7 (endnote 34). Noadiah (6:14): a clause at most. The eunuch question is not raised.
- **Fifty-two days (6:3, 15–16).** *Scripture Helps* 21 has the speed and the enemies' admission; Uchtdorf (29): “an astonishing 52 days.” `[[Ezra 9:9]]` already says the city wall was finished only under Nehemiah. **Proposed:** chapter 6's center is 6:3 with 6:15–16: the refusal to come down, and the enemies' own conclusion. Uchtdorf once, at 6:3: that Nehemiah “refused to allow distractions to prevent him from doing what the Lord wanted him to do” (30). Ludlow (30) sets 6:2–4 beside Lehonti, who came down at the fourth invitation (Alma 47:10–11): an `explore` line. No other reckoning of the wall's duration is used.
- **The law read (8:1–12).** *Scripture Helps* 25–26 answers beside the verse: unclear “whether Ezra read the entire Pentateuch… or selected portions of it”; the Levites' help “likely involved explaining it and perhaps even translating it from Hebrew to Aramaic”; the weeping “likely because they felt remorse”; the occasion “marked the end of a restoration that began nearly a century earlier.” The footnote at 8:8: “gave the sense” is “gave a commentary by the power of the Holy Ghost” (the guide How It Came to Us quotes it). Bible Dictionary, Ezra: “Hitherto ‘the law’ had been to a great extent the exclusive possession of the priests. It was now brought within the reach of every Jew.” Welch (11–12): the scene “is shaped to remind readers of the ancient covenant given on Sinai.” Ludlow (42): Ezra on the wooden tower, “Like King Benjamin” (the footnote at 8:4 gives Mosiah 2:7). **Proposed:** three or four notes: 8:1–4 (who asked; the pulpit); 8:8 (“distinctly” and “gave the sense,” saying more than the footnote: read clearly, explained, perhaps translated; *lead*: `hebrew.mjs nehemiah 8:8`; the Aramaic Targum tradition only if a source read names it); 8:9–12 (why weep, why stop: the day is holy, “the joy of the LORD is your strength,” and portions for “them for whom nothing is prepared,” which the Student Manual, 32-11, calls care for the poor); 8:13–18 (the booths). **Not used:** the Student Manual's “first time a synagogue” (32-10) and Adam Clarke's “extremely corrupt” (printed there).
- **The booths “since the days of Jeshua the son of Nun” (8:17).** Footnotes: “This is the characteristic tradition of the feast of tabernacles” (8:14); “Jeshua,” OR Joshua (8:17). The reader asks how, when Solomon kept the feast and Ezra 3:4 says the returned exiles kept it. Ludlow (44): it “had not been observed in this fashion since the days of Joshua.” The site explains the booths at `[[Lev. 23:43]]` (citing 8:17), `[[Lev. 23:40]]` and `[[Lev. 23:36]]`, and the reading at `[[Deut. 31:10]]` (citing 8:2, 18). **Proposed:** one note at 8:17 answering from the text (the manner: the whole congregation in booths), Ludlow's reading as his, and `[[Ezra 3:4]]`; the meaning of the booths is linked. Bible Dictionary, Feasts: “the greatest and most joyful of all.”
- **The prayer of chapter 9.** Ludlow (47): “According to the ancient Greek version of the Old Testament, Ezra said this prayer, but in the Hebrew version no identification is made”; its weight on the Exodus “may be indicative of their own experience of an Exodus-like return.” Welch (10): it “frames the exile as divine judgment for disobedience, followed by divine mercy.” The site already reads many of its lines (Genesis 15's setting, `[[Ex. 18:11]]`, `[[Ex. 32:4]]`, `[[Judg. 3:9]]`, `[[Obad. 1:21]]`, `[[Ezek. 20:11]]`, `[[Ps. 143:10]]`, `[[Jer. 36:9]]`; Psalm 106 is its nearest psalm). **Proposed:** the setting carries the prayer's shape; notes go to what a reader asks, not to every echo: 9:17 (God's character; link `[[Ex. 34:6]]`), 9:20 (the good spirit to instruct), 9:36–37 (“we are servants this day”; link `[[Ezra 9:9]]`). The Student Manual's verse-by-verse list (32-13) is not copied. The Septuagint's Ezra: a clause, as Ludlow's, if at all.

The JST footnote at 10:29: “an oath, that a curse should come upon them if they did not walk,” and “their God” for “our Lord”; Nyman (`nyman-jst-historical`, 71) calls it “a significant change,” with “our” to “their” and “we” to “they”; the Student Manual (32-15) prints it. Seely (`seely-covenant-renewal`, 8) lists “Ezra 9–10 and Nehemiah 9–10” among covenant-renewal assemblies with Joshua 24, Josiah's and Benjamin's (Mosiah 1–6). The terms are already read at `[[Ex. 30:15]]`, `[[Num. 15:20]]`, `[[Num. 18:26]]` and `[[Mal. 3:10]]` (which gives 10:37–38 the Hebrew of “storehouse”): link. **Proposed:** a Joseph Smith Translation note at 10:29 (the JST as Joseph Smith's answer to what “entered into a curse” means); one note on covenant renewal; the terms linked, not explained one by one. **10:30** (heading: “The people covenant not to marry outside of Israel”) gets no note beyond a link to `[[Ezra 9]]`, under the same ruling as chapter 13.
- **Tobiah's chamber, the Levites and the Sabbath (13:4–22).** Ludlow (66–68): Eliashib, “one of Tobiah's relatives,” gave him a temple room; the unpaid Levites had gone to their fields; Nehemiah shut the gates; and “While we do not encourage lists of dos and don'ts in the Church today, we are invited to consider our activities on the Sabbath day to maintain its holiness.” Pike (`pike-sabbath-ot`, 64, 70, 84): Nehemiah 13 “draws on the content of this Jeremiah passage” (Jer. 17:21–27), and profaning the Sabbath “included the acts of buying and selling.” Bible Dictionary, Sabbath: Nehemiah made it “one of the chief points of his reformation.” Bible Chronology (81) sets Malachi beside the second mission; `[[Mal. 3:8]]`, `[[Mal. 3:10]]` and Malachi 3's setting cite 13:6–12, and `[[Jer. 17:21]]` cites 13:15–19. **Proposed:** a note at 13:4–9 (the chamber; the high priest's alliance from the text, 13:4, 28; the same Eliashib built the sheep gate, 3:1); one at 13:10–13 (link `[[Mal. 3:10]]`; 13:11 against 10:39); one at 13:15–22 (Jeremiah 17 behind it; 13:18's argument from the exile). - **13:1–3 and 13:23–31: the Ammonite and the Moabite, the wives of Ashdod, and the priest who married Sanballat's daughter.** The hardest part of the book. Ezra's ruling applies **exactly**.

Solomon, “beloved of his God… nevertheless even him did outlandish women cause to sin” (13:26). A grandson of the high priest was “son in law to Sanballat the Horonite: therefore I chased him from me”; “they have defiled the priesthood” (13:28–29). “Thus cleansed I them from all strangers” (13:30).
  - *The sources.* The heading: “Some Jews are rebuked for marrying non-Israelite women and defiling the priesthood.” Ludlow (65): the law forbade it “Because of past antagonism from the Ammonites and Moabites,” and the people “broadened this commandment to separate from all foreigners”; (69): such marriage “could bring spiritual harm,” and Solomon shows “the false religious influence his wives brought to him.” Bible Dictionary, Temple on Mount Gerizim: Josephus's Manasseh, who married Sanballat's daughter and became first high priest of the Samaritan temple, is compared with 13:28, “which from the names and circumstances probably relates to the same event,” though “Josephus places the event 90 years later” (the Student Manual, 32-18, draws the line to John 4). **Welch says nothing of chapter 13, and *Scripture Helps* has nothing on it: there is no Latter-day Saint judgment to quote, and none is supplied.**

The footnote's Jacob 2:24 (13:26) is another question and gets no note. The `christ` section rests on 13:22 and 13:31 (a man who asks for mercy and to be remembered for good) or on 13:11, not on the separation.
