The book of Nehemiah is remembered for a wall. It does not end with one. In its last chapter Nehemiah comes back to Jerusalem from the Persian court ([[Neh. 13:6]]) and finds that “the portions of the Levites had not been given them” ([[Neh. 13:10]]), and that the Sabbath has become a market day. The book's last words are not about stone at all. They are a man's request: “Remember me, O my God, for good” ([[Neh. 13:31]]). A few verses earlier he had asked for more than to be remembered: “spare me according to the greatness of thy mercy” ([[Neh. 13:22]]).

This essay follows two threads to that ending. One is a Hebrew verb, *ʿāzab*, “forsake.” It runs through the long prayer of chapter 9, which says three times that God did not forsake His people; through the covenant of chapter 10, where the people promise not to forsake His house; and into chapter 13, where the house has been forsaken. The other thread is the prayer “remember,” which opens the book and closes it and changes as Nehemiah prays it.

Read together, the two threads make one argument, and it is mine. Nehemiah sets a people's vow inside a prayer that has just confessed, three times over, that God did not forsake them, and then it shows the vow broken. In the book's own last prayers Nehemiah asks first that his good deeds stand, then to be spared by God's mercy. The gospel names where that mercy comes from: the Son who cried on the cross, “why hast thou forsaken me?”, in the verb the prayer of Nehemiah 9 uses for what God did not do to Israel.

<h2 id="forsookest-them-not">“And forsookest them not”</h2>

In chapter 9 the Levites lead the people in a prayer that retells their history from Abraham through the Exodus, the giving of the law, the conquest and the kings.[@ludlow-nehemiah-minute] It is a confession, and it is unsparing about the fathers. Three times it reaches a low point in that history, and three times it answers with the same words.

The first is Kadesh, on the border of the promised land. The fathers “refused to obey, neither were mindful of thy wonders that thou didst among them; but hardened their necks, and in their rebellion appointed a captain to return to their bondage” ([[Neh. 9:17]]). The captain comes almost word for word from Numbers 14:4, where the people proposed to go back to Egypt.[@keil-nehemiah] The prayer's answer follows in the same verse: “but thou art a God ready to pardon, gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and forsookest them not” ([[Neh. 9:17]]).

The second is Sinai. “Yea, when they had made them a molten calf, and said, This is thy God that brought thee up out of Egypt, and had wrought great provocations; yet thou in thy manifold mercies forsookest them not in the wilderness” ([[Neh. 9:18–19]]). The cloud still led them by day and the fire by night.

The third covers the centuries in the land. The fathers “cast thy law behind their backs, and slew thy prophets” ([[Neh. 9:26]]), and the prayer comes down to the conquest and the exile. Still: “Nevertheless for thy great mercies’ sake thou didst not utterly consume them, nor forsake them; for thou art a gracious and merciful God” ([[Neh. 9:31]]). God “did not utterly forsake His people, nor make a full end of them,” but “preserved a remnant.”[@keil-nehemiah]

In Hebrew the three answers are one verb in one form, *ʿăzabtām*, “thou didst forsake them,” each time after “not.”[@oshb-wlc] The failures differ in kind: a refusal of the land, an idol, the murder of the prophets. The answer does not differ at all. (The English “mercies” of verses 19 and 31 is a different word, *raḥămîm*, from the “great kindness,” *ḥesed*, of verse 17,[@oshb-wlc] so nothing here rests on the English word “mercy.”) The prayer is “an acknowledgement that though they had their shortcomings, God had not abandoned them and would still work with them.”[@ludlow-nehemiah-minute]

There is an exception, and the prayer itself states it. Verse 28 uses the same verb with God as its subject and no “not”: “But after they had rest, they did evil again before thee: therefore leftest thou them in the hand of their enemies” ([[Neh. 9:28]]).[@oshb-wlc] Here God is the one who forsakes, who abandons men.[@bdb] It is the verb of the Lord’s warning to Moses: “I will forsake them, and I will hide my face from them” ([[Deut. 31:17]]).[@oshb-wlc]

So the prayer does not pretend Israel never felt abandoned. It says that God did leave them to their enemies, and in the same breath, “yet when they returned, and cried unto thee, thou heardest them from heaven; and many times didst thou deliver them according to thy mercies” ([[Neh. 9:28]]). “Forsookest them not” is not a denial of that history. It is the prayer's verdict on the whole of it: He “didst not utterly consume them, nor forsake them” ([[Neh. 9:31]]). That verdict is what the people carry into the next chapter.

<h2 id="we-will-not-forsake">“We will not forsake the house of our God”</h2>

The prayer ends in a decision: “And because of all this we make a sure covenant, and write it” ([[Neh. 9:38]]). The people bind themselves by “a curse, and into an oath, to walk in God’s law” ([[Neh. 10:29]]). The terms follow: to marry within Israel, to keep the Sabbath and not buy from traders on it ([[Neh. 10:31]]), to pay the temple tax, to bring tithes and offerings, and to “do all things necessary to sustain the temple.”[@ot-manual-32]

The covenant's last line is this: “and we will not forsake the house of our God” ([[Neh. 10:39]]). It means that they will see the service of the house provided for.[@keil-nehemiah] It is “a forceful resolution.”[@ludlow-nehemiah-minute] It is also the verb of the prayer, *ʿāzab*, and this is the only place in the book where the people are its subject and God's house its object.[@oshb-wlc][@bdb]

I read that as no accident of vocabulary. The people answer God's faithfulness in His own word. They have just said, three times, what He did not do to them; now they promise not to do it to His house. “Because of all this” is the hinge. The covenant is offered as a response to a mercy already received, and it takes the mercy's shape: as He did not forsake them, they will not forsake Him.

That is a fitting vow, and it is also a large one. It puts the people, in their promise, where God stood in their prayer. The prayer had spent its whole length showing that Israel had never stood there for long.

<h2 id="why-forsaken">“Why is the house of God forsaken?”</h2>

Chapter 13 opens after an absence. Nehemiah had gone back to the king, and “after certain days” he returned ([[Neh. 13:6]]). He found the Levites unpaid and gone home to farm: “the Levites and the singers, that did the work, were fled every one to his field” ([[Neh. 13:10]]).

His rebuke uses the covenant's own word: “Then contended I with the rulers, and said, Why is the house of God forsaken?” ([[Neh. 13:11]]). It is the same verb, now passive, with the same object, the house of God.[@oshb-wlc][@bdb] The house was forsaken “through the non-delivery of the dues,” and the verse is to be compared with 10:39.[@keil-nehemiah] The chapter's note on [13:11](/nehemiah/13/) already observes that the question turns the covenant's last words on the men who made it.

The verb carries further back than the covenant, though. In chapter 9 it named what God had not done to Israel through the calf, Kadesh and the exile. In chapter 10 it named what Israel would not do to Him. In chapter 13 it names what has happened anyway. The vow that answered God in His own word has been broken.

The people did respond to the rebuke. “Then brought all Judah the tithe of the corn and the new wine and the oil unto the treasuries” ([[Neh. 13:12]]); “all Judah” is the whole community.[@keil-nehemiah] The book records that, and it should not be read as a people who would not listen. Nor does it record more: it says what they brought, not what they felt.

Then the Sabbath. Nehemiah saw men treading winepresses and Tyrians selling fish in Jerusalem on the holy day ([[Neh. 13:15–16]]), the trade the covenant had renounced. He asked the nobles, “Did not your fathers thus, and did not our God bring all this evil upon us, and upon this city?” ([[Neh. 13:18]]). The question is about the Sabbath, and the chapter's note on [13:18](/nehemiah/13/) traces it to Jeremiah's warning.

I set it beside chapter 9 as well. This is the community that stood and confessed its fathers' cycle: “after they had rest, they did evil again before thee” ([[Neh. 9:28]]). They have had their rest. The wall is built, the covenant sealed. And Nehemiah's question tells them they are back in the cycle they confessed. The history recited in chapter 9 was not only their fathers'.

<h2 id="remember-me">“Remember me, O my God”</h2>

The second thread begins on the book's first page. Nehemiah's first prayer asks God to keep His word: “Remember, I beseech thee, the word that thou commandedst thy servant Moses” ([[Neh. 1:8]]). And in chapter 9 the fathers' failure is put in the same verb, *zākar*: they “neither were mindful of thy wonders” ([[Neh. 9:17]]).[@oshb-wlc][@bdb] That verse holds both threads. They did not remember; He did not forsake.

Nehemiah's own prayers to be remembered begin early and appeal to what he has done: “Think upon me, my God, for good, according to all that I have done for this people” ([[Neh. 5:19]]). In chapter 13 the prayer comes three times, and it moves.

After he restores the Levites, he prays: “Remember me, O my God, concerning this, and wipe not out my good deeds that I have done for the house of my God” ([[Neh. 13:14]]). The “good deeds” are *ḥesed*, in the plural and with “my”: his own kindnesses, his pious acts.[@oshb-wlc][@bdb][@keil-nehemiah] It is the only place in the book where *ḥesed* is a human being's.[@oshb-wlc]

After he sets the Levites to guard the Sabbath, he prays again: “Remember me, O my God, concerning this also, and spare me according to the greatness of thy mercy” ([[Neh. 13:22]]). This “mercy” is *ḥesed* too, but now it is God's.[@oshb-wlc][@belnap-hesed] It is the word of the prayer of chapter 9, “of great kindness” ([[Neh. 9:17]]), and of Nehemiah's first prayer, to the God “that keepeth covenant and mercy” ([[Neh. 1:5]]).[@oshb-wlc] The chapter's note on [13:22](/nehemiah/13/) makes the same observation.

So the book's one human *ḥesed* stands eight verses before a plea to be spared by God's. And the last line is the barest of all: “Remember me, O my God, for good” ([[Neh. 13:31]]). The verse lists what he appointed, but the petition itself names no deed.

It would be too much to say that Nehemiah renounces his works. He does not. God's remembering can be read as an act of *ḥesed* “done in recognition of the hesed acts performed by the righteous,”[@belnap-hesed] and 13:14 has been read as a plea that God remember Nehemiah's “care and concern for the temple.”[@ludlow-nehemiah-minute] Nor are all his prayers about himself. He asks God to “think thou upon Tobiah and Sanballat according to these their works” ([[Neh. 6:14]]), to remember the priests who “have defiled the priesthood” ([[Neh. 13:29]]), and, of the mockers, “let not their sin be blotted out from before thee” ([[Neh. 4:5]]), the verb of “wipe not out my good deeds.”[@oshb-wlc]

What can be said is narrower and more striking. In one chapter the man who held the people to their covenant asks both that his kindnesses stand and that he be spared by a kindness greater than his. He does not set the second against the first.

<h2 id="spare-me">An ending with a request</h2>

Nehemiah closes without an answer. The vow of chapter 10 has failed once, and been repaired by a governor who had already been away once and, as I read the book's ending, could not be at the gates forever. The last word is a request.

The prayer of chapter 9 had already said where the people's hope lay, and it was not in the vow. It was in the God who “didst not utterly consume them, nor forsake them; for thou art a gracious and merciful God” ([[Neh. 9:31]]). Chapter 13 sends the reader back there. That is my reading of how the book is built: the covenant is real and it matters, but it is held up by the faithfulness it answered, not by the people who made it.

<h2 id="why-hast-thou-forsaken-me">“Why hast thou forsaken me?”</h2>

The God of the prayer has a name in Restoration scripture. To the Nephites the risen Lord said, “I am he that gave the law, and I am he who covenanted with my people Israel” ([[3 Ne. 15:5]]). The God who did not forsake Israel at Kadesh and Sinai is Jesus Christ. The page on [chapter 9](/nehemiah/9/) closes on the same verse.

Elsewhere in scripture this verb carries His promise. To Israel: “he will not fail thee, nor forsake thee” ([[Deut. 31:6]]); to Joshua: “I will not fail thee, nor forsake thee” ([[Josh. 1:5]]), the same verb.[@oshb-wlc] The epistle to the Hebrews brings the promise into the New Testament: “I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee” ([[Heb. 13:5]]), quoting the promise to Joshua.[@lds-scriptures] And when Zion complains, “The LORD hath forsaken me” ([[Isa. 49:14]]), the answer is, “I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands” ([[Isa. 49:16]]). In Isaiah 54 God even concedes the moment: “For a small moment have I forsaken thee; but with great mercies will I gather thee… with everlasting kindness will I have mercy on thee” ([[Isa. 54:7–8]]). The verb is *ʿāzab* again, and the “everlasting kindness” is *ḥesed*.[@oshb-wlc]

There is one more place where the verb stands, and the connection I draw to it is my own reading. The answer of Nehemiah 9, “thou didst not forsake them,” is in Hebrew the same verb, in the same tense and person, as the cry of Psalm 22, without the “not”: “My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?” ([[Ps. 22:1]]).[@oshb-wlc] The psalm's first line is what “the Messiah will say,”[@lds-scriptures] and on the cross Jesus “cried with a loud voice, saying, Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani? that is to say, My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?” ([[Matt. 27:46]]; [[Mark 15:34]]).

What the cry means is not left to inference. Elder Jeffrey R. Holland called it “that concluding descent into the paralyzing despair of divine withdrawal.”[@holland-none-with-him] He was careful to say what it was not: “a perfect Father did not forsake His Son in that hour.” Rather, “the Father briefly withdrew from Jesus the comfort of His Spirit,” because the Son “had to know how the rest of humankind—us, all of us—would feel when we did commit such sins.”[@holland-none-with-him] And he drew the consequence: “because Jesus walked such a long, lonely path utterly alone, we do not have to do so,” and “we will never be left alone nor unaided, even if sometimes we may feel that we are.”[@holland-none-with-him]

Set beside that teaching, Nehemiah 9 reads differently. The prayer praises a God who did not forsake a people who forsook Him, at Kadesh, at Sinai, in the land. I take the cross to be where that mercy is secured. The prayer of Nehemiah 9 admits that God “leftest” His people in the hand of their enemies ([[Neh. 9:28]]), but it does not end on that; its verdict is “thou didst not utterly consume them, nor forsake them” ([[Neh. 9:31]]). The Son, who had “never spoken ill nor done wrong,”[@holland-none-with-him] was the one left to feel the withdrawal, so that the promise “nor forsake thee” would hold for everyone else. This is not a claim that Nehemiah 9 foretells the cross, and the Father did not abandon the Son. It is the claim that the mercy the Levites praised and the mercy Elder Holland describes are one mercy, seen from its two ends.

<h2 id="apply-the-atoning-blood">“Apply the atoning blood of Christ”</h2>

The Book of Mormon has its own covenant assembly to set beside Nehemiah's. King Benjamin's people gathered and entered a covenant, and they have been read as following “the form of covenant renewal in Israel in detail,” with Nehemiah's assembly among the passages compared.[@welch-szink-festivals]

The two assemblies differ at one point. Before Benjamin's people covenant, they “all cried aloud with one voice, saying: O have mercy, and apply the atoning blood of Christ that we may receive forgiveness of our sins” ([[Mosiah 4:2]]). And “the Spirit of the Lord came upon them, and they were filled with joy, having received a remission of their sins” ([[Mosiah 4:3]]). Only then: “we are willing to enter into a covenant with our God to do his will” ([[Mosiah 5:5]]), and “because of the covenant which ye have made ye shall be called the children of Christ” ([[Mosiah 5:7]]).

Nehemiah 8–10 records no such thing. The people confess, and they covenant, and the record does not say they asked for or received a remission of sins. Benjamin's people covenanted after a remission they had asked for through the atoning blood of Christ, and Lehi states the rule behind it: “there is no flesh that can dwell in the presence of God, save it be through the merits, and mercy, and grace of the Holy Messiah” ([[2 Ne. 2:8]]).

One more pairing is mine, and it closes the thread of Nehemiah's prayers. He asked God to remember him and not to wipe out his good deeds. In Isaiah God uses the same two verbs the other way round, of sins: “I, even I, am he that blotteth out thy transgressions for mine own sake, and will not remember thy sins” ([[Isa. 43:25]]).[@oshb-wlc] Jeremiah's new covenant ends the same way: “I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more” ([[Jer. 31:34]]).[@oshb-wlc] Nehemiah asked to be remembered for good. The prophets' words go beyond the request: He blots out sins and does not remember them.

<h2 id="always-remember-him">“Do always remember him”</h2>

Latter-day Saints make a covenant every week, and it is a covenant of remembering. Over the bread they witness that they are willing “to take upon them the name of thy Son, and always remember him and keep his commandments” ([[D&C 20:77]]); over the water, that “they do always remember him” ([[D&C 20:79]]).

Benjamin told his people what to remember: “always retain in remembrance, the greatness of God, and your own nothingness, and his goodness and long-suffering towards you” ([[Mosiah 4:11]]). That is the prayer of Nehemiah 9 in a sentence. It remembers a people who forgot and a God who did not forsake them.

So the covenant is worth making again, and the reason is not that this time the promise will hold by itself. The vow of Nehemiah 10 did not. The reason is the One it is made with. He did not forsake Israel at Kadesh or Sinai or in exile, and on the cross He felt the comfort of the Father's Spirit withdrawn,[@holland-none-with-him] so that those who covenant with Him will “never be left alone nor unaided.”[@holland-none-with-him] A disciple who has failed the covenant prays as Nehemiah prayed at the end of his book, to be spared according to the greatness of God's mercy, and the gospel has given that mercy a name.
