## What a chapter is for here

Jonah is 48 verses in four chapters, and it is unlike the other prophets: “not a collection of Jonah's prophecies but a narrative account of his personal experiences” (*Scripture Helps*). Its one prophecy is five Hebrew words (3:4). A reader knows the story from childhood and asks grown-up questions: *Who was Jonah, and why would a prophet run? Why Tarshish? Was it a whale? Did this happen, or is it a parable, and does it matter? Is the prayer of chapter 2 said inside the fish? How big was Nineveh, and did it really repent? Did God change His mind? Why is Jonah angry when his preaching works? What is the gourd for? Who are the 120,000 who cannot tell their right hand from their left, and why the cattle? Why does the book end with a question?* A chapter answers those from the text first.

**Read the story as a story.** The book is told with care: repeated words, reversals, and a narrator who lets the reader see more than Jonah does. The sailors and the Ninevites, both outsiders, pray, fear the Lord and turn, while the prophet sleeps, sulks and argues. The same English word recurs where the Lord acts: He “prepared” a fish (1:17), a gourd (4:6), a worm (4:7) and an east wind (4:8); the English shows this without the Hebrew. The book's key, by the Bible Dictionary's reckoning, “is to be found in Jonah 3:10–4:11 in the reasons the prophet gives for his flight”: Jonah knew the Lord would be merciful (4:2). So chapter 1 does not settle why he fled; it says the book withholds the reason until 4:2, and points there.

**Say what the book meant before what it foreshadows.** The Saviour made Jonah a sign of His death and Resurrection, and the Church's sources read the book that way; so do the chapters, in each chapter's `christ` section and in the note on 1:17. But each first says what the story says about the Lord, Israel and the nations in its own terms, and keeps the type apart from the story.

**Four chapters, many repeated things.** Each is explained once, at the place this brief names, and the other chapters point to it.

## Divisions

**Proposed: two divisions, one for each call** (`book.yaml`). The book marks them itself: “And the word of the LORD came unto Jonah the second time” (3:1) repeats the call of 1:1–2 almost word for word. Chapters 1–2 (`jonahflight`): the first call, the flight, the storm, the fish and the prayer. Chapters 3–4 (`jonahnineveh`): the second call, Nineveh's repentance, Jonah's anger and the gourd. *Scripture Helps* gives no divisions. Scott reads it as “tripartite” and as a chiasm centred on 2:6; the two-part reading is the text's own, and the parallels between the halves (a call; outsiders in danger who cry to God; the Lord sparing them; Jonah's prayer) are worth a sentence in chapter 3's setting.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, which later scripture takes up which line) is the coordinator's direction of where to look, some of it from memory. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **When.** The prophet is dated by `[[2 Kgs. 14:25]]`: he foretold that Jeroboam II would restore Israel's border “from the entering of Hamath unto the sea of the plain.” *Scripture Helps*: he “ministered during the reign of Jeroboam II in the mid-eighth century BC”; the volume's timeline has Jeroboam II “c. 790–750.” The book itself gives no date, and the Bible Dictionary says it “does not claim to be from the hand of the prophet” and “is due to some later writer.” **Proposed: every chapter's `when` is “The prophet lived under Jeroboam II of Israel, about 790–750 B.C.; the book does not say when it was written,”** with a ledger row from the Bible Dictionary or *Scripture Helps*. When the book was written is said once, in chapter 1's setting (see “History or parable,” below).
- **Who.** “Jonah the son of Amittai” (1:1), “of Gath-hepher” (`[[2 Kgs. 14:25]]`), a town of Zebulun (the Bible Dictionary: “Of Gath-hepher in Zebulun”; Sperry, in the Student Manual, cites Joshua 19:10–13). Van Dyke and Ogden: “a court prophet from Gath-hepher, a small village north of Nazareth in Galilee,” with “a local mission to the court of Jeroboam II”; *Scripture Helps*: “from the northern territory of Zebulun.” The name means “dove” (Sperry in the Student Manual; Scott; KnoWhy #652). The point worth making, once, in chapter 1's setting: the prophet who foretold Israel's restored border is sent to the enemy that would end Israel. **There is no Bible Dictionary entry “Gath-hepher” or “Jonas”**: those addresses return the dictionary's index. Later traditions about Jonah (the widow's son of Zarephath; his tombs) are not used.
- **Nineveh and Assyria.** Explained once, at 1:2, from the Bible Dictionary (`bd-nineveh`, `bd-assyria`) and *Scripture Helps*, with Assyria's cruelty from Van Dyke and Ogden or Tvedtnes (described, not dwelt on: `STANDARDS.md` §3, “For graphic passages, describe rather than quote”). **Nineveh was not yet the capital in Jonah's day**: the Bible Dictionary says “Calah outstripped it in importance” for several centuries and “under Sennacherib it again became the capital”; *Scripture Helps*: it “later became its capital under King Sennacherib.” So no chapter calls it “the Assyrian capital” for Jonah's time; the book's own phrase is “that great city” (1:2; 3:2; 4:11). “The king of Nineveh” (3:6) is unnamed; that the book names no king is one of the points made against its historicity (Tvedtnes; Correa). The volume guides [Two Kingdoms and Assyria](/old-testament/guides/two-kingdoms-and-assyria/#nations) and [A Timeline of the Old Testament](/old-testament/guides/israels-story/#two-kingdoms) carry the kings and the empire: point to them and don't retell them.
- **Tarshish and Joppa** (1:3). Explained once, at 1:3. Tarshish: the Bible Dictionary, “Probably Tartessus in Spain” (`bd-tarshish`); *Scripture Helps*: “The exact location of Tarshish is unknown,” “in the opposite direction of Nineveh,” “as far away as he could.” The site's note on `[[Isa. 60:8]]` explains “ships of Tarshish”: link. Joppa: `bd-joppa`, “the nearest port to Jerusalem.” Wayment's point, that Peter received the call to the Gentiles in the same town (`[[Acts 10:5]]`), is his, and he calls the location “probably little more than happenstance” while allowing that Luke may have meant the echo: give it so, at 1:3 or in chapter 1's `christ` or `explore`.
- **“Went down.”** Jonah goes “down to Joppa,” “down into” the ship (1:3), “down into the sides of the ship” (1:5), and “down to the bottoms of the mountains” (2:6): the same Hebrew verb four times (`hebrew.mjs jonah --word 3381`), and the English keeps it. One sentence in chapter 1's thread, since the English shows it; no Language note.
- **“Evil.”** The Hebrew *raʿ* / *rāʿâ* runs through the book where the English varies: Nineveh's “wickedness” (1:2), the storm's “evil” (1:7), the Ninevites' “evil way” and the “evil” the Lord had threatened (3:8, 10), and, hidden by the King James, Jonah's reaction: “it displeased Jonah exceedingly” (4:1) is, in the Hebrew, a verb and a noun of the same root (`hebrew.mjs jonah 4:1`: H7489, H7451), “it was evil to Jonah, a great evil” (the coordinator's rendering; the writer takes a published one). This is where the Hebrew pays: one Language note at 4:1, from `hebrew.mjs` and the lexicon page, which can say that the Lord turned from evil and Jonah took it as evil. Isaiah 45:7 on the site already explains “evil” as calamity with Jonah 3:10: link.
- **Names of God.** “The LORD” (the divine name) and “God” (*Elohim*) alternate: the shipmaster speaks of “thy God” (1:6), the sailors end crying to and fearing “the LORD” (1:14, 16), the Ninevites speak only of “God” (3:5–9), and Jonah's confession is to “the LORD, the God of heaven, which hath made the sea and the dry land” (1:9). Copy every phrase from `data/kjv/jonah.json` (it has “the LORD God” at 4:6). The Church's footnote at 1:9 is “TG Jesus Christ, Jehovah”: say once, at 1:9, that the LORD of the Old Testament is the premortal Christ, from a Church source read (Scott says it too: “the Old Testament Jehovah is the premortal Jesus”).
- **Verse numbers.** **The Hebrew chapters differ.** King James 1:17 is Hebrew 2:1, and King James 2:1–10 is Hebrew 2:2–11; chapters 3 and 4 agree. `hebrew.mjs` prints both. A note quoting a translation or a lexicon that numbers by the Hebrew gives the King James number.
- **Other books on the site.** Link, and don't say who borrowed from whom except as a source read says. 1:3 and `[[Ps. 139:7–10]]` (the footnote); 1:4–6 and Jesus asleep in the storm (`[[Matt. 8:24]]`, the footnote; Scott sets them side by side); 1:4 and `[[1 Ne. 18:13]]`; 3:9 and `[[Joel 2:14]]` (“Who can tell…”: the site's note on Joel 2:13 already sets them together); 3:10 and `[[Jer. 18:7–10]]` (a lead: the principle that the Lord “repents” of the evil threatened when a nation turns; read the verses and the site's note there before using it), `[[Ex. 32:14]]`, `[[Amos 7:3]]`; 4:2 and `[[Ex. 34:6]]`, `[[Joel 2:13]]`, `[[Num. 14:18]]`; 4:3, 8 and Elijah under the juniper, `[[1 Kgs. 19:4]]` (Davies and Correa both make the comparison); 4:11 and `[[Amos 9:7]]` (Correa sets them together). Nahum's prophecy against Nineveh (`[[Nahum 3:1–4]]`, cited by the Student Manual) is the book's sequel in history; one sentence, in chapter 3's or 4's setting.
- **The New Testament.** The Saviour names Jonah three times: `[[Matt. 12:39–41]]`, `[[Matt. 16:4]]`, `[[Luke 11:29–32]]`. The Greek of Matthew 12:40 is *kētos*, which Davies glosses “huge fish” (the King James “whale”); the Hebrew of 1:17 is *dag gadol*, “great fish.” Explained at 1:17; 3:5 (“the men of Nineveh shall rise in judgment”) points there. The Church's footnotes have no Joseph Smith Translation change in those verses of Matthew.
- **Modern revelation and the Book of Mormon.** No comparison panel, and nothing moves to a Book of Mormon chapter. The footnotes give `[[Alma 36:18]]` at 2:2, `[[Alma 26:37]]` and `[[2 Ne. 26:33]]` at 4:11, `[[D&C 56:4]]` and `[[D&C 121:24]]` at 3:10, `[[Mosiah 12:6]]` at 4:8. A cross-reference that only repeats a footnote is not a note. Worth a note, each from a source read: Alma's three days and nights and his cry (Davies sets Alma 36 beside Jonah 2 at length; the Student Manual compares 2:7 with Alma 36:18); `[[D&C 122:7–8]]` beside 2:3–5 (the Student Manual; Scott); the brother of Jared's prayer, `[[Ether 3:2]]`, beside Jonah 2 (KnoWhy #526, which says it “may have been modeled after” the psalm: give it as that KnoWhy's reading); the sons of Mosiah, who wanted to preach to their enemies the Lamanites (`[[Mosiah 28:1–5]]`; `[[Alma 17:23–25]]`), the contrast *Come, Follow Me* (2026) draws; and `[[D&C 18:10]]`, the worth of souls, which Van Dyke and Ogden set beside 4:11.
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** Two changes, both in the footnotes, both in chapter 3 (`.cache/batch/jonah/fn3.txt`): 3:9 (“we will repent, and turn unto God, but he will turn away from us his fierce anger”) and 3:10 (“evil way and repented; and God turned away the evil that he had said he would bring upon them”). Each answers the question a reader asks of the King James, so each belongs in its note (`lds-scriptures`; the RSC transcript of the manuscripts confirms the wording). **The Joseph Smith Translation leaves 4:2 unchanged** (“repentest thee of the evil”); the Church's footnote there reads “HEB relentest.” Explained once, at 3:10 (see “Did God repent?” below); 4:2 points back.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports what runs across the book; the coordinator adds it to `THEME-CANDIDATES.md` (not yet created). A four-chapter book may not need a theme page.

**Habits to avoid** are listed in Amos's brief and hold here; in Jonah especially, **don't moralize about Jonah**: Correa calls him “too lazy to jump overboard” and his booth ineffective, and the Student Manual has him “pout”; describe what he does and let the text judge him.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that four chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-22.

- **History or parable (the whole book; chapter 1's setting and the note on 1:17).** The Church's sources differ in emphasis. *Scripture Helps* (2025) reads the book as “a narrative account of his personal experiences” and speaks of “Jonah's miraculous deliverance.” The Student Manual (9-2) says “That Jonah's story is a true one, and not an allegory as some scholars maintain, is evidenced by 2 Kings 14:25 and three New Testament references,” and at 9-8 prints Elder Joseph Fielding Smith (*Doctrines of Salvation*): “I believe … the story of Jonah. My chief reason for so believing is … that Jesus Christ, our Lord, believed it.” The Bible Dictionary says the book “does not claim to be from the hand of the prophet,” “is due to some later writer,” and calls it “a beautiful poem”; the Guide to the Scriptures: “Jonah probably did not write the book himself.” Latter-day Saint scholars: Davies (RSC, 1984) sets the sceptic's “It is fiction, a short story with a moral” against the defender's view and calls them “two widely divergent extremes, both of which are equally inaccurate,” noting that “There is no definite physical evidence known at the present time that can either prove or disprove the book of Jonah”; Scott (BYU Studies, 2014) dates the writing “most likely … between 450 and 300 BC” on its language and style and reads it as typology “whether literal or not”; Correa (Scripture Central): “as in a parable, the animals in the story fast.” **Proposed:** chapter 1's setting says, in two or three sentences, that the book tells these events as having happened to the prophet of 2 Kings 14:25, as *Scripture Helps* reads it; that the Saviour pointed to Jonah's three days as the sign of His own death and Resurrection; that the book does not name its author and the Church's Bible Dictionary takes it to be by a later writer; and that some scholars read it as a parable. It does not argue the question, and the reading of every other note does not depend on it. The note on 1:17 may quote Elder Smith once. **For the author** (`OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`, JN1): chapter 1's setting can say either (a) “The book tells these events as having happened to the prophet of 2 Kings 14:25, and the Saviour made them a sign of His own death and Resurrection; the book does not name its author, and some scholars read it as a parable,” or (b) “The book is history, as the Saviour's use of it shows,” leaving the parable reading out. (a) says only what the sources establish and keeps the Bible Dictionary's later writer; (b) follows Elder Smith and the Student Manual and claims more than the newest help does. Recommended: (a).
- **The great fish (1:17).** “A great fish,” *dag gadol* (Davies; the Hebrew), not a whale; the Saviour's Greek *kētos* is “huge fish” (Davies). The Lord “prepared” it: the miracle is His, and the book says so. **Don't defend it with modern parallels**: Tvedtnes tells of a whaler swallowed off the Falklands in 1891, and Davies dismisses such accounts as “Fantastic stories … manipulated, and propounded to show the historicity of the book”; neither is used, and neither is the whale shark. **The Student Manual's Hebrew is wrong**: it says “The Hebrew word taneen used in Jonah” (9-8); the word in 1:17 is *dāg* (`hebrew.mjs jonah 1:17`). Don't use that sentence. The Church's footnote at 1:17 (“three”: Matt. 16:4; TG Jesus Christ, Types of) frames the note: the sign of Jonah, from the Saviour's words, Davies, Scott and KnoWhy #652, once here. Whether Jesus was in the tomb three full days and nights: Scott, “we cannot know for certain the method of reckoning”; one sentence, as his.
- **Did Jonah die in the fish? (chapter 2).** Davies reads “the belly of hell” (2:2) as the spirit world: “Thus, the conclusion elucidated by the Hebrew text, Jonah was dead!”; Scott reports “Some scholars propose” that he drowned and the fish is a metaphor; KnoWhy #652: “We may understand that Jonah … died and was then brought back to life.” Scott himself: “according to the text Jonah is still alive.” **Proposed:** the chapter reads the text as it stands (Jonah prays “out of the fish's belly,” 2:1, and says the Lord “brought up my life from corruption,” 2:6); “hell” is Sheol, the realm of the dead (*Scripture Helps*' endnote; `bd-sheol`; the Student Manual: “the spirit world”), used of a man near death; the reading that he died is given once as Davies's and the KnoWhy's, not the site's. The Church's footnote at 2:5 (“even to the soul”: “IE to the point of death”) supports the plain reading.
- **The psalm in the fish (2:1–9).** *Scripture Helps*: “structured as a psalm of thanksgiving,” echoing “numerous themes from the book of Psalms,” describing “his descent into the sea as a symbolic descent into death.” A thanksgiving, not a plea: the prayer thanks God for an answer already given (Davies: it is “uttered in such a way as to show that by this time he is already out of the fish”; give that as his). The lines that echo psalms are already set side by side on the site: `[[Ps. 120:1]]` (2:2), `[[Ps. 42:7]]` and `[[Ps. 88:7]]` (2:3), `[[Ps. 31:22]]` (2:4), `[[Ps. 69:1]]` (2:5), `[[Ps. 31:6]]` (2:8), `[[Ps. 116:17]]` (2:9), `[[Ps. 3:8]]` (2:9). **Proposed: one note, a Related Scriptures note on the prayer as a whole, links them; no note repeats what a Psalms note says.** “Thy holy temple” (2:4, 7): Correa reads Solomon's prayer (`[[1 Kgs. 8:35–36]]`), praying toward the temple from far away: give as his. “Salvation is of the LORD” (2:9) is the book's centre for Scott; for the note on it, *Scripture Helps* and the site's Psalms 3 explore are enough.
- **“They that observe lying vanities forsake their own mercy” (2:8).** The site's note on `[[Ps. 31:6]]` explains “lying vanities” as idols. “Mercy” is *ḥesed* (`hebrew.mjs jonah 2:8`), which Elder Matthew S. Holland calls “God's special covenantal love for you” (October 2025), reading the verse as the Lord's mercy that is a person's “own,” “perfectly suited to you.” **Proposed:** the note gives what the line means in the prayer (idol-worshippers abandon the One who would be loyal to them), then Elder Holland's reading, with one short quotation, as a General Authority's teaching that this year's *Come, Follow Me* cites.
- **Nineveh: its size and its repentance (3:3–10).** “An exceeding great city of three days' journey” (3:3): the Church's footnotes read “HEB great city to God” and “IE through greater Nineveh with its environs.” The site's note on `[[Gen. 10:9]]` has “great unto God” as an idiom of size: link. The Student Manual (9-10), after Sperry, gives Nineveh a circumference of “about sixty miles”; **don't print that as fact**. What archaeology shows, from a source read: Uriona (*Interpreter*, 2023) quotes Frahm that in the seventh century, its height, “the city covered no less than 750 hectares”; Scott says archaeologists find it “nowhere near ‘three days' journey' in size.” Say what the text says, the footnote's reading, and the size from Uriona, and leave the arithmetic. **The repentance is not attested outside the Bible**: Scott, “not verified in ancient historical records”; Correa, “There is no other historical record of this ever happening”; Tvedtnes lists it among the objections. Say so once, in chapter 3's setting; don't import theories from memory about which Assyrian king or which eclipse lies behind it. The beasts in sackcloth (3:7–8): Keil says the custom of cattle sharing the mourning “is attested by Herodotus (9:24) as an Asiatic custom”; Correa reads it as a parable's touch. Give the Herodotus point as Keil's (read Herodotus only if a readable copy is found).
- **“Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown” (3:4).** Jonah's whole recorded sermon, with no call to repent in it. The verb “overthrown” is *hāpak* (`hebrew.mjs jonah 3:4`: H2015). A lead from memory: it is the word used of Sodom and Gomorrah, and it can also mean “turned.” Check the lexicon page before using it; if it holds, it is a Language note, since the prophecy then came true in a way Jonah did not mean. The site's note on `[[Isa. 38:5]]` already has Nineveh's sentence revised: link.
- **Did God repent? (3:9–10; 4:2).** The King James has God “repent of the evil.” The Joseph Smith Translation makes the Ninevites the ones who repent (3:9, 3:10, above), and the Student Manual's whole note on 3:10 (9-12, “Does God Need to Repent?”) is the JST text. The Hebrew verb is *nāḥam* (`hebrew.mjs jonah 3:10`), which the Church's footnote at 4:2 renders “relentest,” adding that “Jonah knew that God could revoke the calamity decreed, but expected He would do so even without the repentance of the people.” `[[D&C 56:4]]` (the footnote) is the Lord's own statement that He commands and revokes. **The site's reading:** the threat was conditional and the Ninevites met the condition; the Lord's “repenting” is His relenting, not regret for a wrong. The note at 3:10 gives the JST, the footnote and D&C 56:4, and links the site's earlier notes on the same verb (`[[Joel 2:13]]`, `[[Amos 7:3]]`, `[[Ex. 32:14]]`, `[[Gen. 6:6]]`) rather than repeating them. 4:2 points back, and adds only what is new there: Jonah quotes the Lord's own attributes as his grievance.
- **Why Jonah fled, and his anger (1:3; 4:1–4).** The text gives the reason only at 4:2: “for I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful.” The Student Manual (9-1): not cowardice but “resentment against the Lord for giving the hated enemy a chance to repent”; Van Dyke and Ogden and Elder Uceda add fear of the Assyrians. **Proposed:** the text's reason, at 4:2, is the book's; fear and national enmity are sources' explanations, given as such. The chapters may say that Jonah's own prophecy of Israel's restored border (2 Kgs. 14:25) and Assyria's threat to Israel make his resentment understandable, from Van Dyke and Ogden or the Student Manual, without excusing it. “Doest thou well to be angry?” (4:4, 9): the Lord asks and Jonah answers; the note says what the question does.
- **The gourd, the worm and the east wind (4:5–11).** “Gourd” is *qîqāyôn*; the Church's footnote reads “OR castor bean plant,” and Davies (from BDB) “more correctly translated as castor bean”: one sentence at 4:6, from the footnote and the lexicon page. The booth (4:5; footnote “OR shelter”). *Scripture Helps*: the withered gourd “exposed his misplaced priorities”; “the Lord reminded Jonah that He cared for the people of Nineveh, even if Jonah did not.” The east wind: the Student Manual says it blew “off the hot, dry Arabian Desert”; KnoWhy #560 says biblical writers used the east wind “as a sign of divine judgment” even of regions where, as at Nineveh, it “would typically bring refreshing rains.” Give the KnoWhy's point as its own.
- **The 120,000 who “cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand” (4:11).** Two readings in the Church's sources: the Bible Dictionary speaks of God “pitying the little children of Nineveh”; the Student Manual (9-13), that they were “ignorant of the saving gospel principles.” Scott: “whether accountable or considered innocent.” **Proposed:** give the phrase, both readings as readings, and what the verse plainly says: the Lord's care extends to a whole city and its animals (“and also much cattle”; Van Dyke and Ogden: “the spiritual and temporal welfare of all God's creations matter”). Don't compute Nineveh's population from the figure.
- **The ending.** The book ends on the Lord's question and Jonah's silence. The open ending is the reader's to answer; chapter 4's thread says so in a statement, not a question to the reader.
- **The book's message: mercy to outsiders and missionary work.** The Bible Dictionary: “The writer is opposing a narrowmindedness that would confine the love of God to a single nation”; “even in the gentile world the minds of men are conscious of sin and prepared to acknowledge that Jehovah is God.” The Guide: “Jehovah reigns everywhere and does not confine His love to a single nation or people.” Keil, as the Student Manual prints him (9-3), reads Jonah as representing Israel “in its pride at its own election,” and Van Dyke and Ogden as “in a sense, the whole Israelite people, who were trying to flee from their appointed mission”: give these as readings, once, in chapter 4's setting or thread. Missionary work: the Church's footnotes at 1:2 and 3:2 (“TG Missionary Work”); Scott sees “all of the worldwide missionary work among Gentile nations” foreshadowed; *Come, Follow Me* (2026) sets Jonah beside the sons of Mosiah. **The Assyrians are described as the book describes them, and as the sources do, never as a people beyond God's reach**: that is the book's point.
- **Jonah as a type of Christ.** The Saviour's sign is the Church's reading and is given as such (Bible Dictionary; Guide; *Scripture Helps* paragraph 30). Beyond it, Scott lists many parallels (asleep in the storm; lots cast; “take me up”; the weeds as the crown of thorns; the east as the direction of judgment), and says “No type or symbol is a perfect match.” **Proposed:** each chapter's `christ` section uses at most one or two of Scott's parallels, the ones the text itself invites (Jonah's offering himself to save the sailors, 1:12, which Elder Holland calls “Christlike indeed”; the descent and rising of chapter 2), as Scott's; the weeds and the crown of thorns, the direction east, and the numbers three and forty as symbols are not used. Elder Holland's caution belongs beside the type: “As a symbol of the Savior's sacrificial death and glorious Resurrection, Jonah may be flawed.” Chapter 2's `christ` is the sign of Jonah; chapter 4's is the Lord's mercy to those Jonah would have left to die.
