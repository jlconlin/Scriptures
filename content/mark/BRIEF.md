# Mark brief

Book-level decisions for the Gospel of Mark. The site-wide standard is in `STANDARDS.md`. **What Mark shares with Matthew, Luke and John is in `content/volumes/new-testament/GOSPELS.md`; read it first**: every chapter answers its own reader's questions, how a story's research is shared between the Gospels, Talmage's place, Ellicott as the outside commentary of last resort, the Greek, the themes and the images. This file holds what is Mark's own. Matthew's brief is the model for its form, and much of what it settles (the Pharisees and “the Jews,” divorce, Peter and the keys, the Transfiguration) holds here unchanged.

**Status (2026-11-21):** drafted from a research survey made with the research scripts; each source named below was opened then, and a claim about what it says comes from that reading. **The author chose this book** (the Gospels in canonical order, `GOSPELS.md`) **and has not read this brief.** Where a section says “Proposed,” that is the drafter's decision, not a fact from a source, and the author can overturn it.

Related files: `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` at the top of the repository (“The Gospels” among them); `content/volumes/new-testament/GOSPELS.md`, `stories.yaml` and `stories/`; `UNANSWERED.md` in this folder once there is something to put in it. Theme candidates are not collected book by book for the Gospels (`GOSPELS.md`).

## What a chapter is for here

The author's purpose for the Gospels: “the purpose is to anticipate questions a reader might have, and answer those questions” (`GOSPELS.md`). Read the chapter, write down what a careful reader would stop and ask, and answer that from sources read in the session (`STANDARDS.md` §3). A note exists because a reader would ask; a source's having something to say is not a reason for a note. A question no source answers goes in the writer's report and then `UNANSWERED.md`.

**Begin with the chapter's stories.** Run `node scripts/stories.mjs mark <n>` and, for each story told in another Gospel, `node scripts/stories.mjs <id> --text`, and follow `GOSPELS.md`, “How a story's research is shared.” **Mark is the first Gospel written after Matthew, so nearly every story already has a research file: read it before you research,** reopen with `source.mjs` any finding you use (it goes into your own ledger), and correct a finding you find wrong or thin, with your chapter in its `by`. Your job in the file is the `open` list: Matthew's writers left there the questions a reader of Mark will ask (over a hundred of them, by `grep -A12 '^open:' content/volumes/new-testament/stories/*.yaml | grep 'Mark'`), and those are Mark's first questions. Add to `findings` what you read for Mark that a writer of Luke will need.

**What is Mark's own to research** (checked with `stories.mjs` for all sixteen chapters):

- **No research file yet; you write it:** prayer in a solitary place ([[Mark 1:35–39]], told also in Luke 4:42–44); the widow's mites ([[Mark 12:41–44]], Luke 21:1–4); the road to Emmaus as Mark's longer ending tells it ([[Mark 16:12–13]], Luke 24:13–35).
- **Told only in Mark, so the chapter's ledger is its research:** the blind man at Bethsaida, healed in two stages ([[Mark 8:22–26]]).
- **Mark's own passages inside shared stories** (inside stories whose files Matthew's writers made, from Matthew's side): the seed growing secretly ([[Mark 4:26–29]]); the kinsmen who said “He is beside himself” ([[Mark 3:21]]); “the sabbath was made for man” ([[Mark 2:27]]); “salted with fire” ([[Mark 9:49]]); the young man who fled naked ([[Mark 14:51–52]]); the women's question about the stone ([[Mark 16:3]]); Pilate's question to the centurion ([[Mark 15:44–45]]). Smith lists these as material unique to Mark (`smith-marks-unique-contribution` [26]–[47]).

Questions a reader of Mark asks again and again (a start, not a limit):

- *What does this Aramaic word mean, and why did Mark keep it?* (Boanerges, Talitha cumi, Corban, Ephphatha, Abba, Eloi; Smith [10] and notes 2–3 list them.) See “Mark's Aramaic, his Latin words, and his explanations.”
- *Why does Jesus tell him to tell no one?* See “The commands to silence.”
- *Why do the disciples not understand?* Mark shows the Twelve failing again and again (Smith [53]–[57]); see the conventions.
- *Why is Mark's account longer, or different, from Matthew's?* Mark tells fewer stories but tells each at greater length (Smith [11]; Huntsman `huntsman-petrine-mark` [78]). What Mark has that Matthew lacks is stated from the verses; what it means only from a source read (`GOSPELS.md`).
- *What did Joseph Smith change here?* See “The Joseph Smith Translation in Mark.”
- *What does this word mean?* The footnotes gloss many words from the Greek (“GR”): at 1:43 “straitly charged him” is “warned him sternly”; at 5:30 “virtue” is “power, strength”; at 6:37 “pennyworth” is denarii, “a workman's daily wage”; at 10:52 “made thee whole” is “saved, preserved, healed thee.” A note does not repeat a footnote for its own sake (`STANDARDS.md` §1); it goes further only where the reader's question needs more.

**Where answers come from** is `STANDARDS.md` §1's order, with Talmage after the Latter-day Saint studies and Ellicott, the *Expositor's Greek Testament*, Meyer, the *Cambridge Bible* and Edersheim last (`GOSPELS.md`). **Mark is well served by Latter-day Saint studies, and above all by Julie M. Smith** (an RSC article, a BYU Studies article and an *Interpreter* article are readable; her commentary is not, below), Eric Huntsman and Gaye Strathearn. **Don't always go to the same source first** (`GOSPELS.md`), and that includes Smith: no single secondary source shapes a chapter (`STANDARDS.md` §4 rule 11). The entries quoted in this brief are leads for the questions they are quoted under.

## Divisions

Mark's own shape is geographic and turns on Peter's confession. Huntsman: “a clear dividing point in the Gospel occurs with Peter's declaration at Caesarea Philippi” ([[Mark 8:27–30]]), after which the text shifts from the doer of mighty deeds to the suffering Son of Man who three times predicts His Passion (8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34) (`huntsman-petrine-mark` [72]); the Gospel “represents a geographic, not a strictly chronological, progression, following Jesus in a path from Galilee to Jerusalem” ([74]). Easton gives the usual outline: a prologue (1:1–13 or 15), the ministry in and around Galilee (1:14–8:26), the journey to Jerusalem (8:27–10:52), and Jerusalem (11:1–16:8) (`easton-marcan-christology` [34]); Huntsman's “drama” is the same three acts ([73]). Mark arranges much of the Galilean part by kind of material, controversies, parables, miracles (Smith [15]), and has two discourses, the parables of chapter 4 and the Mount of Olives in chapter 13 (`strathearn-olivet-mark` [12]). The journey is framed by two blind men healed (8:22–26; 10:46–52), the example `GOSPELS.md` gives of an arc that is Mark's own; Keller likens Peter, who knew Jesus was the Messiah but not yet that the Messiah must suffer and die, to the blind man who after the first touch saw only partly (`keller-mark-luke` [20]). The Bible Dictionary gives no outline of Mark.

Because a division holds whole chapters, chapter 8 (which ends the Galilean ministry and holds the turn) goes with the journey it opens. **Proposed divisions** (keys used by no other book; checked against every `book.yaml`, Matthew's and Isaiah's among them):

| Key | Chapters | Division | Blurb |
|---|---|---|---|
| `markbegin` | 1–3 | The Beginning of the Gospel | John prepares the way, the Father names His Son, and Jesus teaches with authority in Galilee, forgives sins, meets His first opponents and chooses the Twelve. |
| `markseed` | 4–5 | Parables and Mighty Works by the Sea | The parables of the seed, then a storm stilled, a legion cast out, a woman healed and a girl raised. |
| `markbread` | 6–7 | The Loaves and the Hardened Hearts | Rejected at Nazareth, Jesus sends out the Twelve, feeds five thousand and walks on the sea, sets aside the traditions of the elders and heals among the Gentiles, while His disciples' hearts are hardened. |
| `markway` | 8–10 | On the Way: The Son of Man Must Suffer | Between two blind men healed, Peter confesses the Christ and Jesus three times foretells His death, teaching what it costs to follow Him. |
| `marktemple` | 11–13 | Jerusalem and the Temple | Jesus enters the city, cleanses the temple, answers His questioners, praises a widow's gift and foretells the temple's fall. |
| `markcross` | 14–16 | The Cross and the Empty Tomb | Anointed for His burial, Jesus gives the sacrament, prays in Gethsemane, is denied, condemned and crucified, and is risen. |

A chapter's sections follow its stories (`node scripts/stories.mjs mark <n>`).

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks were copied from the page named. The rest is the drafter's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **Long chapters: save in pieces.** Matthew's writers of long chapters (5, 10, 17, 18, 24, 26, 27) were cut off with “Output blocked by content filtering policy” before the chapter file was saved; a writer told to save in pieces finished at the first try (`GOSPELS.md`, “State”). **Every writer of Mark 1, 5, 6, 9, 10, 12, 14 and 15 (43 verses or more; 14 has 72) saves the chapter as a small skeleton first (the YAML frame, `setting`, section titles, no notes), then adds two or three notes at a time by Edit.** The coordinator gives this in the workflow's `focus` for those chapters; any writer may do it for any chapter.
- **Mark's Aramaic, his Latin words, and his explanations.** Mark keeps Aramaic words and translates them (5:41; 7:34; 15:34), explains Jewish customs (7:3–4) and uses Latin terms, twice explaining a Greek word by a Latin one (“a farthing,” *quadrans*, [[Mark 12:42]]; “Praetorium,” [[Mark 15:16]]) (`strathearn-gospels` [18]–[19] and note 20); Huntsman adds *legio* at 5:9 (`huntsman-petrine-mark` [12]); the Bible Dictionary calls such explanations “necessary procedure for non-Jewish readers” (`bd-gospels` [3]). **Proposed:** the note says what the word means (Thayer, the footnote); *why* Mark kept it is said only from a source, and Smith says the theories cannot be verified (Smith [10]). Where Mark has already explained a custom or a word, the note answers what his explanation leaves open. Huntsman thinks Mark's account of the washings and of Corban “broad, perhaps even a bit inaccurate” ([11]); a note on 7:3–4 explains the practice from a source on it and does not say in the site's voice that Mark erred.
- **The disciples' failures.** “Not just discipleship, but failed discipleship, is a core theme in Mark's Gospel” (Smith [52]–[53]); yet the young man at the tomb sends word to “his disciples and Peter” (16:7), and “it is assumed that Peter's discipleship will continue” (Smith [57]; `huntsman-easter-sunday` [34]). **Proposed:** notes describe the failures as the verses do, without scorn; where the reader would ask what became of them, the answer is Peter's later ministry and his restoring the priesthood (`bd-peter` [3]–[4]). The `christ` section applies their failure and Jesus's patience to the disciple now.
- **Jesus's humanity in Mark.** Mark is “more comfortable with stories that show the ‘human’ side of Jesus” (Smith [48]): anger and grief (3:5), “he could there do no mighty work” (6:5), the healing in two stages (8:23–25), “Why callest thou me good?” (10:18), Gethsemane and the cry from the cross; Smith: “it is theologically important to maintain that Jesus had both divine and human aspects during mortality” ([48]). **Proposed:** a note explains such a verse plainly and does not soften it; Restoration scripture comes in where it answers the verse's question (He “received not of the fulness at first, but continued from grace to grace,” [[D&C 93:13]]), and the Joseph Smith Translation where it changes the verse.
- **Titles of Jesus, and Mark's sandwiches.** Smith ([72]–[83]), Easton and Rappleye read Mark's Christology through his titles and his story: “Son of God” at 1:1, 1:11, from unclean spirits (3:11; 5:7) and from the centurion (15:39); “Son of Man” only from Jesus; “Christ” at 8:29 and 14:61–62. Mark often sets one story inside another so that each explains the other, as Jairus's daughter around the woman with the issue of blood (Smith [17], [61]). **Proposed:** a note on a title says what it means where it stands, from a source; a note on a sandwich says so where the reader would ask why the stories interrupt each other. The narrative readings (the anointing as defining “the Christ,” Smith [77], [87]; the centurion, Smith [44]; the pairing's meaning) are their authors', attributed.
- **The text: King James and the Greek.** The text is the King James Version, whose Greek is the Textus Receptus (`greek.mjs mark <c>:<v>`; lexicon `thayer`). Modern editions differ from it in places a reader may ask about: “the Son of God” in 1:1 (below), “in the prophets” at 1:2 (the SBL text reads “in Isaiah the prophet”), the second half of 9:49 (Smith [29]), and the longer ending (below). **Proposed:** a difference is mentioned only where the reader of the King James verse would ask, read from the SBL text on Bible Hub (`greek-nt`; `biblehub.com/texts/mark/<c>-<v>.htm` sets the editions side by side), and stated as what the manuscripts show, never as a claim that the King James verse is not scripture.
- **Mark's own wording is the text.** A note quotes the verse it sits on; Matthew's or Luke's wording comes in only where the difference is the question (`GOSPELS.md`). Mark's present tenses and his “straightway” (Greek *euthys*, 41 times, Smith [13]) are not each a note.
- **Dates and places.** The author's chronology is the order the site follows (`GOSPELS.md`); Mark's order is “not, indeed, in order” by Papias's account (below), and Huntsman reads Mark as geographic rather than strictly chronological ([74]). A year is given only from a source read, as that source's.
- **The Pharisees, the scribes and the chief priests.** Matthew's convention holds: name the group the verse names, and never let a note's wording pass from a group in a scene to a people in every age. In Mark's trial before Pilate, “the chief priests moved the people” ([[Mark 15:11]]); the note says who did what, as the verse does.
- **Women in Mark.** Smith reads Mark's women as its best disciples, from Peter's wife's mother who “ministered” (1:31) to the women at the cross and the tomb (Smith [59]–[65]). Her readings are leads; a claim such as women's presence at the Last Supper ([64]) is hers, attributed, never stated as fact.
- **Talmage.** *Jesus the Christ* is opened last, for what the other sources have not answered, and cited for what it adds (above). Find the chapter for a passage on its contents page; `talmage-jesus-christ-<chapter>` keys exist for most chapters (8–11, 14, 15, 17–24, 26, 27, 29–35, 37).

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each. The writer reads the source again before writing the note and quotes from the page.

- **Who Mark was, and the Peter tradition.** The Bible Dictionary: Mark, “also called John,” son of the Mary of Acts 12:12, Paul and Barnabas's companion who left them at Perga, later with Paul at Rome and with Peter “at Babylon (probably Rome)”; his Gospel “was possibly written under the direction of Peter” (`bd-mark`; `bd-peter` [2]). The Gospel is anonymous; its title appeared later in the second century (`huntsman-petrine-mark` [10], [13]). Papias, quoted by Eusebius, reports an elder's words that “Mark became Peter's interpreter … and wrote accurately all that he remembered, not, indeed, in order” (Huntsman [15]; `judd-who-wrote-gospels` [12]); Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria and Origen repeat the link (Huntsman [17]–[21]), and Huntsman judges it “early, probably reliable” ([23]). Mark names Peter 25 times, more often for its length than any Gospel ([27]). Huntsman: “Latter-day Saints do not need to assume that restoration scripture or theology directly supports traditional authorship” ([25]). **Proposed:** the setting of chapter 1 says once what the Bible Dictionary says, adds the Papias tradition with its source, and does not argue the identification. The title footnote reads “JST entitles this book ‘The Testimony of St. Mark,’” while Jackson (`jackson-jst-new-testament` [14]) and Huntsman ([25]) say the manuscripts named only Matthew and John testimonies: a note may give the footnote's reading and builds nothing on it.
- **Markan priority and source theories, for a Latter-day Saint reader.** “It is commonly accepted in biblical scholarship that Mark was written first and Matthew and Luke used Mark as one of their sources” (`farnes-synoptic-gospels` [45]); so what is unique to Mark “might tell us more about Matthew and Luke than it does about Mark” (Smith [26]). Elder Bruce R. McConkie allowed that “Mark was written first” as something “some scholars speculate” (quoted by Judd [17]). Farnes: the question “is, of itself, of no significant theological importance,” but a hypothetical “Q” “should be treated cautiously” ([49]–[50]); Judd: the Gospels “are inspired, even if they used oral or previously written sources” ([44]). Most scholars date Mark AD 65–74 (Farnes [30]); Huntsman between Peter's death (about 64) and 69 ([12]); for Latter-day Saints, “who accept Jesus's prophetic ability,” a Gospel's knowing of the temple's fall does not prove it was written after (Farnes [10]). **Proposed:** a note may say, attributed, that most scholars think Mark was written first, where the reader's question needs it. No note builds on Q, says what Matthew or Luke “did to” Mark in the site's voice, or dates Mark by treating Mark 13 as written after the event. Why another Gospel lacks a detail is said only from a source, as its reading.
- **The commands to silence (“the messianic secret”).** Jesus silences unclean spirits (1:25, 34; 3:11–12), tells the healed to tell no one (1:44; 5:43; 7:36; 8:26) and charges the disciples to tell no man He is the Christ (8:30; 9:9). Smith: Wrede's theory that the secret was invented later “fell out of favor in the 1970s”; “there is no current scholarly consensus”; the commands are often broken, and Mark says so ([67]–[68]). Keller: Jesus did not want demonic testimony; the spreading news drove Him into the desert (1:45); and the people, Peter included, expected a Messiah who worked miracles and would cast out the Romans, not one who must die (`keller-mark-luke` [24]–[25]). Smith: the requests most likely reflect His desire that before His death people not be “misled about what it means to be a Messiah” ([70]). Rappleye reads “Son” as a sacred name, received at the baptism, hidden from the profane and revealed at the cross (`rappleye-messianic-sacred`, p. 1). Some Latter-day Saint writers compare the secrecy to temple worship; Smith calls the analogy “admittedly not perfect” ([69]–[70]). **Proposed:** each command is explained where it stands, from its context and these readings (Keller's and Smith's first), attributed; Wrede's theory is not offered as a reading of a verse, and the temple analogy is not made in the site's voice. The exceptions are questions too: the healed man of Gadara is told to tell his friends (5:19).
- **“The Son of God” in 1:1.** The Textus Receptus behind the King James has the words; the SBL text, Westcott and Hort and Tischendorf omit them; the Nestle text of 1904 has them, and the NA27 variants bracket them (Bible Hub, `texts/mark/1-1.htm`). No Latter-day Saint study read for this brief discusses the reading. Easton reads the line as the Gospel's title, “the only place where the narrator pronounces a direct, generalized assessment of Jesus” (`easton-marcan-christology` [16]); the Father (1:11) and the centurion (15:39) say the same within the story (Smith [76]). **Proposed:** the note on 1:1 explains the title and what it promises for the book; if it mentions the manuscripts, one sentence says some early ones lack the words, and that Mark calls Jesus the Son of God throughout, so nothing hangs on the line.
- **The young man who fled naked ([[Mark 14:51–52]]).** Only Mark tells it (`bd-gospels` [4]). The Joseph Smith Translation calls him “a disciple” (JST Mark 14:57, the footnote at 14:51). Smith: “probably the most enigmatic episode in the entire Gospel of Mark”; “there is no indication as to who this young man is,” though he may be the “young man” at the tomb (16:5), since Mark uses *neaniskos* only of these two ([43] and note 36). Huntsman and Hatch read him as “perhaps serving as a type for all his followers,” who gives up even his clothes to abandon Jesus (`huntsman-thursday` [339]), and report Smith's fuller reading: *sindōn*, his linen, is the word for Jesus's shroud, and the young man in white at the tomb is the deserter redeemed (`huntsman-easter-sunday` [32]–[34]). No source read names him as Mark himself. **Proposed:** the note says what the verses say, that the Joseph Smith Translation calls him a disciple, that no one knows who he was, and gives the reading of him as a type of the disciples who fled, attributed; the link to 16:5 is made in chapter 16 as Smith's reading. The tradition that he was Mark is mentioned only from a page that states it.
- **The ending of Mark ([[Mark 16:9–20]]).** The evidence: verses 9–20 are missing in Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus (fourth century) and in many early versions; Eusebius and Jerome knew copies without them; some manuscripts have a short ending, one an expansion, some both; Irenaeus quoted 16:19, so the longer ending existed by the second century; it has “excellent support from Greek witnesses, although not from all the earliest” (`wayment-endings-mark` [9]–[16]). Wayment: the original ending “has almost certainly been lost” ([9]), the vocabulary is not Mark's ([20]), yet the evidence “is hardly definitive” and the King James text is “a viable and defensible option” ([22]). Huntsman and Hatch: the verses “appear to be later scribal interpolation” (`huntsman-easter-sunday` [26]). Jeff Lindsay argues against the scholars' consensus (`lindsay-longer-ending-1` [41]–[58]); the KnoWhy calls authenticity “a defensible position,” and notes that even scholars who reject it think it “likely stands as an early, independent witness of the resurrection” (`sc-knowhy-522` [8]–[9]). **The Church's position, as its own texts show it:** its edition prints 16:9–20 with a chapter heading that summarizes it and footnotes that join it to modern revelation; Joseph Smith revised 16:3–6 (two angels at the tomb) and left 9–20 standing; and the Lord's words in Restoration scripture repeat the commission and the signs, to the Nephite disciples ([[Morm. 9:22–25]]) and to the elders of this dispensation ([[D&C 68:8–10]]; [[D&C 84:64–72]], “as I said unto mine apostles”). The KnoWhy: Moroni's source “was not the Gospel of Mark,” so those words do not depend on the long ending ([12]). *Scripture Helps* says the Church “accepts the message of Mark 16 as inspired scripture,” but its endnotes cite works not online; it is a finder only. **Proposed:** chapter 16's notes treat verses 9–20 as scripture, as the Church's edition and Restoration scripture do. Its `setting` (or the note at 16:9) says once, plainly, that the oldest manuscripts end at verse 8 (Wayment), that Latter-day Saint scholars differ on whether Mark wrote what follows (Wayment, Huntsman and Hatch; Lindsay, the KnoWhy), and that the Lord taught the same commission and signs in the Book of Mormon and the Doctrine and Covenants, whoever first wrote them down. It does not call the verses spurious or settle their authorship in the site's voice. The signs (16:17–18) are read with D&C 84:73 (“they shall not boast themselves of these things”) and D&C 63:9–12 (“signs follow those that believe”) where the question needs it. This is a sensitive question the standard lets the drafter settle; the author may wish to see the note.
- **Mark 13 and Joseph Smith—Matthew.** The footnote at 13:1: “The text of JST Mark 13 is the same as JST Matthew 24. See Pearl of Great Price, JS—M.” Joseph Smith and Sidney Rigdon “simply copied the revised text of Matthew 24 into the place of Mark 13,” probably near December 1831; later revisions mean “the texts are not quite identical now” (`jackson-jst-new-testament` [42]–[43]). Mark's discourse has its own shape: both of the disciples' questions concern the temple (13:4); the answer falls into four sections, each opened by “take heed” (vv. 5, 9, 23, 33); it lacks the love of many waxing cold, the eagles and the sign of Noah (`strathearn-olivet-mark` [8], [14], [16]–[17]); only Mark has the porter told to watch (13:34–35). Strathearn reads it as written for Saints facing persecution in Nero's Rome ([10]). **Proposed:** as in Matthew 24, the `setting` says in two or three sentences that the Lord gave a fuller and reordered text through Joseph Smith, which stands in place of Mark 13 in his translation, and links it; a note on a verse Mark shares with Joseph Smith—Matthew answers Mark's reader briefly and links that note (`occurrences.mjs joseph-smith-matthew "<phrase>" --site`). What is Mark's own (13:4, “take heed,” 13:9–13, “let him that readeth understand,” the porter) is written in full. **[[Mark 13:32]]** (“neither the Son, but the Father”) is set beside [[JS—M 1:40]] (“but my Father only”) and [[D&C 49:7]], and the note says no more about the limits of the Son's knowledge than a source read says.
- **The cry from the cross ([[Mark 15:34]]).** Mark gives only this saying of Jesus on the cross, in Aramaic and translated. Smith reads the baptism and the cross as bookends, the voice from heaven quoting a psalm to Jesus and Jesus quoting a psalm to God (`smith-narrative-atonement-mark` [23]); the bystanders mistake “Eloi” for Elijah ([24]). **Proposed:** the note explains the words and their psalm ([[Ps. 22:1]]), and what the Father's withdrawal meant from a prophet's or apostle's teaching read on the page; it does not speculate beyond it. Matthew 27's note on the same cry is read first, so the two agree.
- **The hard sayings Mark shares.** Divorce ([[Mark 10:2–12]]) follows Matthew's approach (Elder Oaks's teaching as the Church's present position, quoted briefly; care for readers who have been divorced); Mark alone has a wife putting away her husband (10:12), an open question in the story file. The Syrophenician woman and “the children's bread” ([[Mark 7:27]]): the Joseph Smith Translation reads “children of the kingdom first.” “Purging all meats” ([[Mark 7:19]]) is read by some as Mark's comment that Jesus “declared all foods clean” (Smith [38]); the note explains the King James words from the Greek and gives that reading as a reading; the Joseph Smith Translation of 7:15 adds “which is food.” Abiathar at [[Mark 2:26]], where 1 Samuel 21 names Ahimelech, is answered only from a source read.

## The Joseph Smith Translation in Mark

- **How it was done.** For Matthew 1 through John 5 Joseph Smith dictated the whole text, so Matthew, Mark and Luke have “many more small, clarifying, textual changes” than the rest of the New Testament (`jackson-jst-new-testament` [11]–[12]). The verse divisions are his own, paragraph-length: Mark 9:16–27 was one verse, JST Mark 9:5 ([27]). Mark 13 was copied from his Matthew 24 (above). Wayment, reported by Jackson, suggests a few changes drew on Adam Clarke's commentary, perhaps the insertion of “the son of the living God” at Mark 8:29 ([25]); say that only from the page, and never argue from the Greek that a change is or is not what Mark wrote (the rule of Joseph Smith—Matthew's brief).
- **What the reader already has.** The appendix has entries for chapters 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, 14 and 16 (`jst-mark-<n>`, `…/scriptures/jst/jst-mark/<n>?lang=eng`; all read for this brief): the Son of Man made the Sabbath (2:26–27); the sin against the Holy Ghost (3:21–25); “by the prophets whom ye have rejected” (7:10–12); losing one's life, and no part in “that resurrection” for those ashamed of Him (8:37–38, 42–43); John the Baptist with Moses on the mount (9:3); the offending hand, foot and eye as a brother or a leader (9:40–48); the sacrament “in remembrance of my body” (14:20–26); the disciples in Gethsemane “wondering if this be the Messiah” (14:36–38); two angels at the tomb (16:3–6). The footnotes give more readings in chapters 1, 4, 6–12, 14 and 15, among them the Spirit taking Jesus into the wilderness (1:12), “This he said, rebuking Peter” (10:31) and “I am, even as thou sayest” (15:2).
- **The Elias of the Transfiguration (JST Mark 9:3).** “Elias with Moses, or in other words, John the Baptist and Moses.” The Bible Dictionary: “The curious wording of JST Mark 9:3 does not imply that the Elias at the Transfiguration was John the Baptist, but that in addition to Elijah the prophet, John the Baptist was present” (`bd-elias` [1]). **Proposed:** chapter 9's note follows the Bible Dictionary and links Matthew 17's notes on the keys given on the mount.
- **How to use it.** Judiciously, where the change answers the reader's question (`STANDARDS.md` §1). It is “not the official Bible” of the Church but “an invaluable aid to biblical interpretation” (`bd-jst`). Most changes are small and unprinted; a writer who needs one quotes the manuscript (New Testament Revision 1 and 2 in the Joseph Smith Papers, `jsp-nt1`, `jsp-nt2` as Matthew's brief proposes) or a transcription read on a page.
