Kings gives the fall of Judah a cause in two sentences, and the second ends on a verb. The end came “for the sins of Manasseh, according to all that he did; And also for the innocent blood that he shed: for he filled Jerusalem with innocent blood; which the LORD would not pardon” ([[2 Kgs. 24:3–4]]). “Pardon” is *sālaḥ*, a verb the lexicon says is used “always of God.”[@bdb] Kings has used it before. Solomon prayed it five times at the dedication of the temple, in the refrain “hear thou in heaven … and when thou hearest, forgive” ([[1 Kgs. 8:30]]), the last time for captives in the land of their enemies: “forgive thy people that have sinned against thee” ([[1 Kgs. 8:50]]). Naaman twice asked that the LORD pardon him for bowing in the house of Rimmon ([[2 Kgs. 5:18]]). It is one verb every time,[@oshb-wlc] and 24:4 is the one place in the two books where it has “not” in front of it.

What the LORD would not pardon is named: innocent blood. That is this essay's subject, and it is narrower than blood. Blood occurs thirteen times in 2 Kings, and some of those do not belong here. The Moabites saw water “as red as blood” and said “This is blood” when it was not ([[2 Kgs. 3:22–23]]); Ahaz sprinkled “the blood of his peace offerings” on the altar he had copied from Damascus ([[2 Kgs. 16:13]]; [[2 Kgs. 16:15]]), the blood of sacrifice, not of a killing; and Jezebel's blood on the wall ([[2 Kgs. 9:33]]) is a stain the story leaves, not a claim anyone makes. The rest, every one, is blood the LORD says He has seen, or will avenge, or would not pardon: the blood of the prophets and of Naboth (9:7, 26), and the innocent blood of Manasseh's Jerusalem (21:16; 24:4).[@oshb-wlc]

Read in order, those passages show a LORD who does not write innocent blood off. He sees it. He requites it. Even the blood of the requital, Hosea says, He will visit on the house that shed it. Then a king fills a city with it, the book says the LORD would not pardon it, and the city is emptied. The New Testament then names one more innocent blood, and names it, in the Greek, in the words Kings uses for Manasseh's victims. No Church source I have read sets 2 Kings 24:4 beside the Savior; the step is mine, and I will argue it from the words. What it shows, I think, is what pardon costs. The LORD of Kings is, in Latter-day Saint understanding, the premortal Jesus Christ,[@bd-jehovah] who said of Himself, “I am he that gave the law” ([[3 Ne. 15:5]]). The LORD who would not pardon innocent blood never changed His mind about innocent blood. He shed His own, and by it He pardons.

## I have seen the blood of Naboth

The plainest statement of how the LORD treats blood in this book is a sentence Jehu quotes. Joram, Ahab's son, has just died with an arrow through his heart, and Jehu tells his captain to throw the body into Naboth's field, because of a word spoken when the two of them rode behind Ahab: “Surely I have seen yesterday the blood of Naboth, and the blood of his sons, saith the LORD; and I will requite thee in this plat, saith the LORD” ([[2 Kgs. 9:26]]). Two things are said there. The LORD saw the blood, and the LORD will pay it back.

The word for blood in that sentence is plural, “bloods,” a form the lexicon files under blood shed by violence, placing this verse beside Genesis 4, where Abel's blood cries from the ground for vengeance.[@bdb] The idea is older than Kings. The law of Moses makes shed blood a stain on the land itself: “the land cannot be cleansed of the blood that is shed therein, but by the blood of him that shed it” ([[Num. 35:33]]). “Cleansed” there is *kipper*, the verb of atonement,[@oshb-wlc] and the essay [Between the Dead and the Living](/numbers/themes/between-the-dead-and-the-living/) follows that verse to Amulek. Innocent blood does not evaporate. It lies on the ground until someone answers for it, and the LORD keeps the account.

Naboth's story shows how He kept it. Elijah's word in the vineyard was blood for blood: “In the place where dogs licked the blood of Naboth shall dogs lick thy blood, even thine” ([[1 Kgs. 21:19]]). Then Ahab humbled himself, and the LORD saw that too, and the answer was a deferral: “I will not bring the evil in his days: but in his son's days will I bring the evil upon his house” ([[1 Kgs. 21:29]]). The son is Joram, and Naboth's field is where he lands. Ahab's sackcloth moved the requital a generation; it did not dissolve it.

Jehu's own commission names other blood the LORD had kept: “that I may avenge the blood of my servants the prophets, and the blood of all the servants of the LORD, at the hand of Jezebel” ([[2 Kgs. 9:7]]), the prophets Jezebel “cut off” in Elijah's day ([[1 Kgs. 18:4]]). Years have passed. The LORD has not forgotten whose blood it was.

## The blood of Jezreel

Jehu does what he was anointed to do, and when Ahab's house is gone the LORD's own verdict on the work is given: “thou hast done well in executing that which is right in mine eyes, and hast done unto the house of Ahab according to all that was in mine heart” ([[2 Kgs. 10:30]]). The next verse sets the limit, that Jehu “took no heed to walk in the law of the LORD God of Israel with all his heart” ([[2 Kgs. 10:31]]). The Church's manual reads the two verses together: the house of Omri had earned its destruction and Jehu was the means, “but that does not mean the Lord was pleased with his brutal methods or his wickedness.”[@ot-manual-6] I pass no judgment of my own on Jehu here.

Hosea returns to this blood. The prophet's first son is to be called Jezreel, “for yet a little while, and I will avenge the blood of Jezreel upon the house of Jehu” ([[Hos. 1:4]]). Jezreel is where Joram fell and where Jezebel was thrown down; the blood of Jezreel is the blood Jehu shed there in requiting Naboth's. The verb is not the word for vengeance but the word for visiting a sin upon someone, the verb of “visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children,” and the lexicon lists Hosea 1:4 with Exodus 20:5.[@bdb] Hosea does not say why Jehu's house should answer for it. One Latter-day Saint reading is that the blood “would now be avenged upon his dynasty, whose kings were wicked like their ancestor”;[@jackson-hosea-marriage] Keil separates the act from the man: a thing commanded by God becomes a crime in the one who does it when he “abuses the divine command, and makes it the mere cloak for the lusts of his own evil heart,” and Keil finds that motive in the historian's verdict at 10:29 and 31.[@keil-hosea] The note “Why Jehu's house answers for Jezreel” on [Hosea 1](/hosea/1/) sets these out.

For this essay the two verses together show something narrower than any explanation of them, and the text carries it. The LORD's sight of blood does not stop at the blood He commanded to be avenged. The law held the avenger guiltless, “he shall not be guilty of blood” ([[Num. 35:27]]), and Jehu's reward was real: four generations on the throne, and Kings records the promise kept ([[2 Kgs. 15:12]]). Yet the same LORD who said “thou hast done well” knew whose hand shed the blood at Jezreel, and Hosea says He would visit it. In this book no blood shed in Israel falls outside His seeing, not even the blood He sent a man to shed.

## Innocent blood

The phrase that titles this essay does not appear until Manasseh. “Moreover Manasseh shed innocent blood very much, till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another; beside his sin wherewith he made Judah to sin” ([[2 Kgs. 21:16]]). The Hebrew is *dām nāqî*, and the adjective means free from guilt, clean, innocent; the lexicon notes that the phrase “innocent blood” is “first used in D,” that is, in Deuteronomy, and lists this verse and 24:4 among its uses.[@bdb] In 2 Kings those are the only two.[@oshb-wlc] The phrase is a legal one. When a body is found in a field and no one knows who killed him, the elders of the nearest city break a heifer's neck, wash their hands over it and pray: “Be merciful, O LORD, unto thy people Israel, whom thou hast redeemed, and lay not innocent blood unto thy people of Israel's charge. And the blood shall be forgiven them” ([[Deut. 21:8]]). “Forgiven” there is *kipper* again, covered;[@oshb-wlc] the lexicon reads the clause “and the blood shall be covered for them,” and adds, of the LORD covering what men cannot, that God “in his sovereignty may himself provide an atonement or covering for men and their sins which could not be provided by men.”[@bdb]

Kings does not identify the innocent, and the proposals include “all the righteous men” and the prophets, in Josephus as the Student Manual quotes him;[@ot-manual-19] “possibly those who protested his idolatrous policies,” in one Latter-day Saint reading;[@vandyke-ogden-twelve] “perhaps specifically human sacrifice,” in another;[@seamons-hilton-blood-2022] Isaiah, sawn asunder, by what the Bible Dictionary calls tradition.[@bd-isaiah] The verse's own emphasis is quantity: “very much,” and a city filled “from one end to another,” in the Hebrew from mouth to mouth.[@oshb-wlc]

One fact about this blood needs stating before the sentence on it: how Manasseh's reign ends. Kings closes it with the formula it uses of a king who died on his throne: “And Manasseh slept with his fathers, and was buried in the garden of his own house” ([[2 Kgs. 21:18]]). By the law of Numbers the land's cleansing for shed blood was the blood of the one who shed it, and Kings records no requital of the man himself. Chronicles tells what Kings leaves out, that in affliction Manasseh “humbled himself greatly before the God of his fathers, And prayed unto him: and he was entreated of him, and heard his supplication” ([[2 Chr. 33:12–13]]); the note “The repentance Kings leaves out” on [chapter 21](/2-kings/21/) says what can be said of the silence. So the LORD heard Manasseh. The blood was still in the land.

## Which the LORD would not pardon

Josiah tests whether a king's return to the LORD would avert the judgment on Judah. No king before him “turned to the LORD with all his heart, and with all his soul, and with all his might, according to all the law of Moses; neither after him arose there any like him” ([[2 Kgs. 23:25]]), and the next word is “Notwithstanding.” The LORD “turned not from the fierceness of his great wrath … because of all the provocations that Manasseh had provoked him withal” ([[2 Kgs. 23:26]]). One Latter-day Saint reading of the history's logic is that Manasseh's wickedness was so great that even Josiah's righteousness could not make up for it;[@sc-knowhy-861] another is that Manasseh's reversal of his father's reform brought Judah's former sins back, and that Josiah's reform, for all its reach, was outward and did not last.[@satterfield-divine-justification] The first explains it by the king, the second by the people. Neither mentions the blood, and the book does.

Notice first that Josiah himself was heard. Huldah's answer kept the city and the king apart, and to the king the LORD said: “Because thine heart was tender, and thou hast humbled thyself before the LORD … I also have heard thee, saith the LORD” ([[2 Kgs. 22:19]]). “Heard” is *šāmaʿ*, the verb of Solomon's refrain.[@oshb-wlc] Solomon had joined two verbs in that refrain, “when thou hearest, forgive,” and the essay [A Hearing Heart](/1-kings/themes/hearing-heart/) follows the first of them through 1 Kings. Here the two come apart. The king is heard and gathered to his grave in peace; the wrath against the city “shall not be quenched” ([[2 Kgs. 22:17]]). Hearing a penitent and pardoning a land full of blood are not, in these chapters, the same act.

Then the sentence. “Surely at the commandment of the LORD came this upon Judah, to remove them out of his sight, for the sins of Manasseh, according to all that he did; And also for the innocent blood that he shed: for he filled Jerusalem with innocent blood; which the LORD would not pardon” ([[2 Kgs. 24:3–4]]). The Hebrew of the last clause is “and the LORD was not willing to pardon,” the verb of consent with “not” before it,[@oshb-wlc] and the verb of pardon stands without an object, as it does in Solomon's “when thou hearest, forgive.”[@bdb] Keil reads it with Jeremiah's oracle, in which even Moses and Samuel could not turn the LORD toward this people ([[Jer. 15:1]]): the LORD would not forgive “because the measure of the sins was full.”[@keil-kings]

What the sentence is about must be said exactly, because it is easy to read it as more than it is. It is a sentence on a land and a people: “to remove them out of his sight.” Kings does not say what it meant for any one soul of that generation, and this essay does not ask. Its subject is why the LORD would not pardon a city full of innocent blood as a city, and the law has already answered. The law gave a land two ways to be cleared of innocent blood: the blood of the shedder, and, for blood whose shedder no one knew, the heifer in the valley and the elders' prayer. Neither reaches a city its own king has filled from one end to the other: the shedder was the king, and Kings records no requital of him; and the heifer answered one body in a field whose killer no one knew, not a city of dead whose killer everyone knew. What the law gave instead was its warning: “Defile not therefore the land which ye shall inhabit, wherein I dwell: for I the LORD dwell among the children of Israel” ([[Num. 35:34]]). “I will remove Judah also out of my sight, as I have removed Israel, and will cast off this city Jerusalem which I have chosen, and the house of which I said, My name shall be there” ([[2 Kgs. 23:27]]). That the land was emptied for blood neither of the law's remedies reached is my reading of these verses together; it is what the law leaves when both of its remedies are out of reach.

One more word, from the Greek, before leaving Kings. The Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Old Testament that the New Testament most often quotes,[@bd-septuagint] read here in the Greek printed beside Brenton's translation, renders the last clause of 24:4 “and the Lord would not pardon,” and its verb is *hilaskomai* in the passive: *ouk ēthelēse Kyrios hilasthēnai*.[@lxx-rahlfs] In biblical Greek that passive means to become propitious, to be placated or appeased,[@thayer] so the Lord was not willing to be propitiated. The Hebrew it renders is *sālaḥ*, pardon.[@oshb-wlc] I will come back to it.

## From the blood of righteous Abel

If the principle of Kings were an Old Testament severity that the gospel set aside, the essay would end here. It is not. Jesus affirmed it, in Jerusalem, in the last week of His life. To the scribes and Pharisees who built the tombs of the prophets He said: “That upon you may come all the righteous blood shed upon the earth, from the blood of righteous Abel unto the blood of Zacharias son of Barachias, whom ye slew between the temple and the altar. Verily I say unto you, All these things shall come upon this generation” ([[Matt. 23:35–36]]).

That is the LORD of 2 Kings 9 speaking; Talmage reads the sentence as “Jehovah Himself” replying to the men before Him.[@talmage-jesus-christ-31] Righteous blood, from the first murder on, is seen, and it is required of a generation. Luke's form of the saying has the very word: the blood shed from the foundation of the world is to be “required of this generation” ([[Luke 11:50–51]]). The Zacharias meant is, in the opinion of most Bible scholars as Talmage reports it, Zechariah son of Jehoiada, stoned in the temple court in the reign of Joash.[@talmage-jesus-christ-31] Chronicles gives the dying man's words, and they are the words of this essay: “The LORD look upon it, and require it” ([[2 Chr. 24:22]]). Abel's blood, which the lexicon set beside Naboth's, is where the Savior begins the count.

So the Savior did not abolish the account. He read it aloud. Within days, the crowd before Pilate would say of His own blood, “His blood be on us, and on our children” ([[Matt. 27:25]]).

## The innocent blood

Judas brought the silver back. “I have sinned,” he said, “in that I have betrayed the innocent blood” ([[Matt. 27:4]]). In Matthew's Greek the two words are *haima athōon*.[@greek-nt] In the Septuagint of Kings, Manasseh “shed innocent blood,” *haima athōon*, and filled Jerusalem with it, and the LORD would not pardon,[@lxx-rahlfs] the same two words. They are not the law's usual words, so Judas is not reaching for a stock phrase: at the cities of refuge and at the heifer the Septuagint has another adjective, *haima anaition* ([[Deut. 19:10]]; [[Deut. 21:8]]), and *athōon* stands in the law only in the curse on him who takes a reward “to slay an innocent person” ([[Deut. 27:25]]).[@lxx-rahlfs] It is the phrase of Jonathan pleading for David and of Jeremiah warning his judges,[@lxx-rahlfs] and it is the phrase of Kings' sentence. The Church's footnotes, which send the “blood” of 2 Kings 21:16 and the “blood” of Matthew 27:4 to one entry of the Topical Guide, “Blood, Shedding of,”[@lds-scriptures] put the two verses in the same drawer. No source I have found remarks on the match.

What Judas named, a servant of the Lord in our day has named from the pulpit of general conference. “By the shedding of His innocent blood,” Elder Hamula taught, “Jesus Christ satisfied the demands of justice for every sin and transgression.”[@hamula-sacrament-2014]

Here is the step that is mine. In Kings, the LORD would not pardon innocent blood. In the gospel, innocent blood is that by which He pardons. The phrase is the same and the direction is reversed, and the reversal is not because the principle changed; the Savior had just affirmed it. It is because this innocent blood is His own. Amulek reasons from the law that “there is not any man that can sacrifice his own blood which will atone for the sins of another,” that “the law requireth the life of him who hath murdered,” and therefore that “there can be nothing which is short of an infinite atonement which will suffice for the sins of the world” ([[Alma 34:11–12]]). The blood of the Son of God is that atonement: “that great and last sacrifice will be the Son of God, yea, infinite and eternal” ([[Alma 34:14]]).

Hebrews says the same thing in the image this essay began with. Abel's blood cried from the ground, for vengeance in the lexicon's reading,[@bdb] and the Savior said that the blood from Abel's on would be required of a generation. But believers, the epistle says, “are come … to Jesus the mediator of the new covenant, and to the blood of sprinkling, that speaketh better things than that of Abel” ([[Heb. 12:24]]). Abel's blood spoke, and the Savior read it as a demand. His own blood speaks too, and it says something better. Joseph Smith taught why the first sacrifices were acceptable at all: “It must be shedding the blood of the Only Begotten to atone for man, for this was the plan of redemption, and without the shedding of blood was no remission.”[@nt-manual-hebrews-7-13]

And the Greek verb comes back. The Septuagint said the Lord was not willing to be propitiated, *hilasthēnai*, for Judah's blood. Hebrews says the Son was made like His brethren “that he might be a merciful and faithful high priest in things pertaining to God, to make reconciliation for the sins of the people” ([[Heb. 2:17]]), and “to make reconciliation” is *hilaskesthai*, the same verb with the roles reversed:[@greek-nt] at 24:4 the Lord is the one not propitiated, and in Hebrews He is the high priest who makes the propitiation. The Church's footnote at “reconciliation” sends the reader to the Redeemer.[@lds-scriptures] For the sins of the people: the sentence of 24:4 was on a people. I set the two verses beside each other as my own reading. The Lord who would not be propitiated for Judah's blood is the One who, as high priest, makes propitiation for the sins of the people, by innocent blood of His own.

Let me be careful about what this does and does not claim. It does not claim that Manasseh's blood was pardoned after all, or that the cross undid the exile. Kings' sentence stood; Jerusalem fell; the book ends in Babylon. What the Savior's blood answers is not that history, and not the blood Manasseh shed. What it answers is what the sources name: sin, and the sinner who repents. The Guide to the Scriptures defines atonement as “to suffer the penalty for an act of sin, thereby removing the effects of sin from the repentant sinner,”[@gs-atone-atonement] and says of His blood that it “cleanses the repentant from sin.”[@gs-blood] Pardon in the gospel is not the LORD overlooking sin, any more than in Kings He overlooked blood. The demands of justice for the repentant sinner's sins are satisfied, Elder Hamula says, and what satisfies them is innocent blood.[@hamula-sacrament-2014]

## In remembrance of the blood

King Benjamin's people, when they saw themselves as they were, cried out with one voice: “O have mercy, and apply the atoning blood of Christ that we may receive forgiveness of our sins” ([[Mosiah 4:2]]). The thing asked for is forgiveness; the means named is blood. That is the gospel's answer to 24:4 in a single prayer, and it is the prayer a disciple is taught to pray.

The sacrament keeps it before us every week. The prayer over the cup asks that we drink “in remembrance of the blood of thy Son, which was shed for them” ([[D&C 20:79]]). Elder Hamula said what the cup teaches: how we may be made clean, by innocent blood shed, offered to those who “have faith in Him sufficient to repent.”[@hamula-sacrament-2014] The conditions are real. The Bible Dictionary says the Atonement is “conditional … so far as each person's individual sins are concerned,” and reaches each “to the degree that he has faith in Jesus Christ, repents of his sins, and obeys the gospel.”[@bd-atonement] A disciple who has read Kings knows why there is a cup. The LORD does not write innocent blood off, and the pardon He offers is not a writing-off either; Elder McConkie's testimony was that “salvation comes in and through his atoning blood and in no other way.”[@mcconkie-gethsemane-1985]

So a reader who has felt the weight of “which the LORD would not pardon” can feel the weight of the sacrament cup. The forgiveness received there is not a lighter thing than the sentence on Judah; it is justice satisfied, for the repentant, by innocent blood. The lexicon said at the heifer prayer that God may Himself provide “an atonement or covering for men and their sins which could not be provided by men”;[@bdb] that the covering He provided is the blood of the cup is my reading, and it rests on the Church's teaching that His blood cleanses the repentant.[@gs-blood] It is innocent blood, named so by the man who sold it and by a servant of the Lord in our day, and on the reading argued here it is the one innocent blood that speaks pardon instead of demanding it. Hebrews says where the disciple stands: “ye are come … to the blood of sprinkling” ([[Heb. 12:22–24]]). Kings shows what it cost to be able to come there.
