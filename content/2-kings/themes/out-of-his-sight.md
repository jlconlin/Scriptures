Twice the book of Kings passes the same sentence, once on each kingdom. Of Israel the narrator says that “the LORD was very angry with Israel, and removed them out of his sight” ([[2 Kgs. 17:18]]). Of Judah the LORD says it Himself: “I will remove Judah also out of my sight, as I have removed Israel” ([[2 Kgs. 23:27]]). “Sight” is the King James translators' word. The Hebrew noun is *pānîm*, face, and the Greek translation known as the Septuagint kept the sense: removed “from his presence.”[@oshb-wlc][@lxx-rahlfs] The same noun lies under “presence” at 13:23 and 24:20, and under “before” wherever a prophet or a king in this book comes before the LORD.[@oshb-wlc] One word has been rendered three ways, and the thread it makes through the book is hard to see in English.

The site has followed this word through one family already ([From Hiding from God to Meeting Him](/genesis/themes/hiding-to-meeting/)); this essay follows it through a nation, not to how the face was first lost but to how a whole people was kept before it, and then was not.

Here is what I will argue. Kings tells the end of both kingdoms as a removal from the LORD's face, in the very idiom the Book of Mormon uses for the Fall of Adam and calls spiritual death. The terms for staying before that face were the nation's own walking before it. Kings never records that the nation met them, and every reprieve it does record rests on something other than the nation's merit: the covenant with the fathers, David, the LORD's own name, His compassion on their affliction, the prayers of kings. The book ends with a son of David's fallen house taken out of prison to eat bread “continually before” a king, kept, by the book's own earlier word, for David's sake. Hebrews names the one for whose sake the face finally stays open: Christ has entered heaven “to appear in the presence of God for us.” The steps from Kings' removal to the Book of Mormon's spiritual death, and from the table in Babylon to that verse, are mine.

## Before whom I stand

The book's first use of the word for the LORD's face is on a prophet's lips. Three kings have marched into the desert and run out of water, and Elisha tells the king of Israel: “As the LORD of hosts liveth, before whom I stand, surely, were it not that I regard the presence of Jehoshaphat the king of Judah, I would not look toward thee” ([[2 Kgs. 3:14]]). “Before whom” and “the presence of” are the same noun in that one verse, the LORD's face and Jehoshaphat's.[@oshb-wlc] Elisha says it again to Naaman, refusing his silver: “As the LORD liveth, before whom I stand, I will receive none” ([[2 Kgs. 5:16]]). Elijah had sworn by the same words to Ahab ([[1 Kgs. 17:1]]).

The lexicon treats “stand before” as an idiom with a meaning of its own: to attend upon someone, to be his servant, and it lists these four oaths under that heading.[@bdb] The picture behind the preposition is a court: *pānîm* literally means face, and “when entering the court of a king, one came before his face—that is, came into his presence”; the same was true of the temple.[@lane-presence-panim] The prophet's oath says where he stands and whose servant he is, and from that place he speaks for the LORD to kings.

Elisha gives the reason he will speak at all: “were it not that I regard the presence of Jehoshaphat.” The valley fills with water for the sake of another king's face. The same construction, standing before the LORD, has a second heading in the lexicon, “for intercession,” and the verse it puts first is Jeremiah's: “Though Moses and Samuel stood before me, yet my mind could not be toward this people” ([[Jer. 15:1]]).[@bdb] To stand before the face, there, is to plead for others.

## As yet

Chapter 13 turns from the prophet who serves the LORD to a king who petitions Him. Jehoahaz has done evil and Syria has crushed his army, “and Jehoahaz besought the LORD, and the LORD hearkened unto him: for he saw the oppression of Israel” ([[2 Kgs. 13:4]]). The King James hides the noun. The Hebrew says that he entreated *the face of* the LORD; the verb with that object means to “mollify, appease, entreat the favour of,” literally to “make the face of any one sweet or pleasant.”[@bdb][@oshb-wlc] A king the book has just condemned ([[2 Kgs. 13:2]]) turns to the face, and is heard, and “the LORD gave Israel a saviour” ([[2 Kgs. 13:5]]). The next generation is spared the same way: “the LORD saw the affliction of Israel, that it was very bitter … and the LORD said not that he would blot out the name of Israel from under heaven: but he saved them by the hand of Jeroboam” ([[2 Kgs. 14:26–27]]). What moves the LORD in the first is a king's entreaty and the people's oppression, and in the second their affliction alone; in neither is it anything the people have kept.

Then the narrator steps back and gives the reason the kingdom still exists at all. “And the LORD was gracious unto them, and had compassion on them, and had respect unto them, because of his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and would not destroy them, neither cast he them from his presence as yet” ([[2 Kgs. 13:23]]). “Presence” is *pānāyw*, His face, the word of chapter 17, and “as yet” is “until now.”[@oshb-wlc] Three verbs of mercy are followed by their cause, the covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob: a promise to men long dead, not anything Israel had done. Israel is before the face because of a covenant made with its fathers.

Kings gives the same kind of reason for Judah. The LORD “would not destroy Judah for David his servant's sake, as he promised him to give him alway a light, and to his children” ([[2 Kgs. 8:19]]); He will defend Jerusalem against Sennacherib “for mine own sake, and for my servant David's sake” ([[2 Kgs. 19:34]]), and says so again to the sick Hezekiah ([[2 Kgs. 20:6]]). The preposition in all three is *lĕmaʿan*, and the lexicon files these verses together: for the sake of David's memory and the promises given to him, and “for my own sake, i.e. to vindicate my name.”[@bdb][@oshb-wlc]

What the nation's own standing before the face would have required, the book had stated at the start, in the same noun. David's charge to Solomon carries the LORD's condition: “If thy children take heed to their way, to walk before me in truth with all their heart and with all their soul, there shall not fail thee … a man on the throne of Israel” ([[1 Kgs. 2:4]]; Solomon prays it back, [[1 Kgs. 8:25]]), and at the temple the LORD puts it to him with its other side: “if thou wilt walk before me, as David thy father walked,” the throne stands, but if they turn and serve other gods, “this house, which I have hallowed for my name, will I cast out of my sight” ([[1 Kgs. 9:4–7]]). “Walk before me” is *lĕpānay*, before my face; “out of my sight” is the phrase of 23:27.[@oshb-wlc] The same conditional promise, in the same idiom, was given to Nephi: “inasmuch as ye will not keep my commandments ye shall be cut off from my presence.”[@bowen-cut-off]

The condition did not lapse. It is repeated for Judah under Manasseh, “only if they will observe to do according to all that I have commanded them” ([[2 Kgs. 21:8]]), and chapter 17 says the LORD “testified against Israel, and against Judah, by all the prophets” to turn and keep them ([[2 Kgs. 17:13]]).

Those were the terms, and Kings never records that the nation met them. Every reprieve it does record rests on something else. That is the fact the rest of this essay turns on.

## Out of his sight

The sentence on Israel, when it comes, is pronounced three times in six verses, and all three use the face. The LORD “removed them out of his sight” ([[2 Kgs. 17:18]]); He afflicted them “until he had cast them out of his sight” ([[2 Kgs. 17:20]]); “Until the LORD removed Israel out of his sight, as he had said by all his servants the prophets” ([[2 Kgs. 17:23]]).[@oshb-wlc] Keil glosses each of them with the noun: He “removed them from His face,” He “thrust them from His face,” and he distinguishes the “rejected” of verse 20 as the chastisement meant to turn them, from the casting out of the face, which is exile.[@keil-kings]

The book's verdicts on its kings, right or evil “in the sight of the LORD,” use another word, *ʿênê*, eyes; the sentence uses *pānîm*, face.[@oshb-wlc] Israel was not removed from the eyes that had watched it; it was removed from the face a prophet had stood before.

Verse 23 adds two clauses. The removal came “as he had said by all his servants the prophets,” and it holds “unto this day.” What that became is known only in part: those taken into Assyria in large numbers “eventually became lost to history,” and “there is much we do not know about the fate of the lost tribes of Israel.”[@scripture-helps-2-kings-16-25] “Where they went is not known,” and the Lord has not revealed it.[@ot-manual-kings-d] (The note on [chapter 17](/2-kings/17/) carries the Church's teaching on their scattering and return.)

## Wept before me

Judah's last century is told with the same noun, and in Judah the face is still open. Hezekiah takes Sennacherib's letter into the temple and “spread it before the LORD,” and “prayed before the LORD” ([[2 Kgs. 19:14–15]]). On what should have been his deathbed he turns his face to the wall and says, “remember now how I have walked before thee in truth” ([[2 Kgs. 20:3]]), the condition of 1 Kings 2:4 in a king's mouth. To Josiah the LORD says, through Huldah, “thou hast humbled thyself before the LORD … and hast rent thy clothes, and wept before me; I also have heard thee” ([[2 Kgs. 22:19]]). Josiah then “made a covenant before the LORD” ([[2 Kgs. 23:3]]). Every “before” is *pānîm*.[@oshb-wlc]

Each of those men is heard, and the text says so in as many words. “That which thou hast prayed to me against Sennacherib king of Assyria I have heard” ([[2 Kgs. 19:20]]), and the city is defended for David's sake ([[2 Kgs. 19:34]]). “I have heard thy prayer, I have seen thy tears: behold, I will heal thee,” and fifteen years are added to Hezekiah's life ([[2 Kgs. 20:5–6]]). Josiah, because his heart was tender, will be gathered to his grave in peace ([[2 Kgs. 22:20]]). A king who comes before the face in this book is heard there. Some of what he is given is his own, years and a peaceful grave; some is the city's, and the city is spared for David's sake. Where the nation is reprieved, then, a king's plea and David's promise are the grounds, not the nation's walking.

Then it passes the sentence anyway. “Notwithstanding the LORD turned not from the fierceness of his great wrath … And the LORD said, I will remove Judah also out of my sight, as I have removed Israel” ([[2 Kgs. 23:26–27]]). The word is *pānay*, my face, and this time the LORD says it in the first person.[@oshb-wlc] The narrator repeats it twice more: the end came “at the commandment of the LORD … to remove them out of his sight, for the sins of Manasseh” ([[2 Kgs. 24:3]]), and Zedekiah rebelled only after He “had cast them out from his presence” ([[2 Kgs. 24:20]]), the same noun again.[@oshb-wlc]

So the book keeps two things apart, and I think on purpose. A man before the face is heard; Hezekiah and Josiah were, and were given a city defended, years added, a grave in peace. The nation's standing before the face was on other terms, its own walking before Him, which it had not kept; what had held it there was David's sake and the pleading of prophets and kings. In chapter 23 the book gives no reason for a further reprieve, and the sentence falls, within a generation of its best king's prayer being heard. That the reason stops is what the text shows; what it means is my reading.

## Continually before him

The book does not end with the sentence. In the thirty-seventh year of Jehoiachin's captivity, a new king of Babylon “did lift up the head of Jehoiachin king of Judah out of prison,” spoke kindly to him, set his throne above the other captive kings', “and changed his prison garments: and he did eat bread continually before him all the days of his life” ([[2 Kgs. 25:27–29]]). “Before him” is *lĕpānāyw*, the everyday “before,” used of people before people in nineteen verses of 2 Kings, so the preposition cannot carry a connection by itself; what carries it is the scene, and the adverb *tāmîd*, continually, without interruption.[@oshb-wlc][@bdb] This is the last time the book uses its word for the face, and the face is a Babylonian's.

The Church's sources give the scene as history: Jehoiachin released and “kindly treated by his Babylonian overlords.”[@ot-manual-19] The narrator says nothing of Evil-merodach's motive, and Keil says that no safe conjecture can be made.[@keil-kings] But Keil does not leave the scene without a ground: its “higher ground” lay “in the gracious decree of God, that the seed of David, though severely chastised for its apostasy from the Lord, should not be utterly rejected,” and he takes it as “a comforting sign to the whole of the captive people” that their banishment would end.[@keil-kings] That is an older commentator's reading, and no Latter-day Saint source I have found reads the ending as a figure of anything. The ground, though, is the book's own: “for David his servant's sake, as he promised him to give him alway a light, and to his children” ([[2 Kgs. 8:19]]). What follows is mine.

I have met these words before, at the start of David's reign. David asks whether anyone is left of Saul's house, “that I may shew him kindness for Jonathan's sake” ([[2 Sam. 9:1]]), and again, “that I may shew the kindness of God unto him” ([[2 Sam. 9:3]]). The one who is left is lame, in a stranger's house in Lo-debar. David says to him: “Fear not: for I will surely shew thee kindness for Jonathan thy father's sake, and will restore thee all the land of Saul thy father; and thou shalt eat bread at my table continually” ([[2 Sam. 9:7]]). The chapter says it three times, “continually,” *tāmîd*, the word of 25:29,[@oshb-wlc] and ends with the two facts side by side: “he did eat continually at the king's table; and was lame on both his feet” ([[2 Sam. 9:13]]). Keil's one cross-reference at 25:29 is to this chapter,[@keil-kings] and the site's note on [2 Samuel 9](/2-samuel/9/) already sets Jehoiachin beside Mephibosheth for the honor.

The two scenes share a shape. A royal house has fallen; one of its sons is alive, and helpless; a king brings him from where he was, changes his condition, and feeds him before his face for life. And in both the keeping is for someone else's sake. Samuel says it twice, for Jonathan's, and calls it the kindness of God. Kings says it earlier and lets the scene stand without repeating it: David's house is kept, for David's sake, by promise. (Samuel's “for … sake” is *baʿăbûr*, not Kings' *lĕmaʿan*;[@oshb-wlc] I rest nothing on the preposition.)

A book in which no nation stayed before the LORD's face on its own terms closes on a son of David fed before a face, continually, by a promise made to his father. Where the line goes from there Matthew says: this Jehoiachin is the “Jechonias” through whom he traces David's line to “Jesus, who is called Christ” ([[Matt. 1:12]]; [[Matt. 1:16]]). So the house kept for David's sake carried, in the end, the Savior. On my reading the earlier keepings, Israel's for the fathers, Judah's for David, Mephibosheth's for Jonathan, point the same way: each was for someone's sake, and this house was kept to bring the one Hebrews says now stands before the face for everyone. That reading is mine, and two other books let me make it.

## Cut off from the presence

The first is the Book of Mormon, which uses Kings' idiom for something larger than a nation. “Because man became fallen they were cut off from the presence of the Lord” ([[2 Ne. 9:6]]). Alma says the same and names it: “the fall had brought upon all mankind a spiritual death as well as a temporal, that is, they were cut off from the presence of the Lord” ([[Alma 42:9]]). Samuel the Lamanite: “all mankind, by the fall of Adam being cut off from the presence of the Lord, are considered as dead” ([[Hel. 14:16]]). Spiritual death is “separation from God and His influences,” and mortals of evil works “are spiritually dead while still alive on earth.”[@gs-death-spiritual]

That the Book of Mormon's “presence” translates the same Hebrew word as Kings' “sight” is a scholar's argument, not mine. Matthew Bowen argues that Alma's phrase rests on the Hebrew *pānîm*, face, and that Alma uses the word's two senses, the face of the earth and the face of the Lord, “to emphasize the relational nature of the physical and spiritual death that resulted from the Fall”; without the Atonement, he writes, “being ‘cut off’ from the Lord's ‘face’” would have been permanent.[@bowen-cut-off] Kings makes the connection from the other end of the Old Testament, at a different scale: the same idiom and the same kind of separation, happening to a whole people, slowly, in a book written by someone who had watched it. Removed from His face is “separation from God” when it is a nation's, told as history. The word is both books'; the comparison of the two stories is mine.

The exile in Kings is then not first the loss of a land, though it is that too. It is the loss of a face, and the land was where the face was sought: “The men of Israel were commanded to come before the face of the Lord three times a year.”[@lane-presence-panim] Jehoiachin eating before a Babylonian's face in Babylon is what the removal looks like once it has been carried out: still before a face, and not the one the nation was made to come before.

## For us

The second book is Hebrews, which says what Christ is doing now in the vocabulary of 2 Kings. “For Christ is not entered into the holy places made with hands, which are the figures of the true; but into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God for us” ([[Heb. 9:24]]). The Greek under “presence” is *prosōpon*, face, and the lexicon reads the phrase as “to appear before the face of God, spoken of Christ, the eternal priest, who has entered into the heavenly sanctuary.”[@thayer] For us: that is the sake.

Set the verse at the end of the row. Elisha stood before the face, and a king got water for another king's sake. The LORD kept Israel before His face for the fathers' sake and Judah for David's, and heard kings who entreated it. Mephibosheth ate before a king's face for Jonathan's. Jehoiachin ate before a king's face as the kept seed of David. And Christ appears before the face of God for us. Each link is the text's own words; the row is my arrangement.

The Book of Mormon then puts the answer in the first person, and the voice is the voice of 23:27. To the brother of Jared the Lord says: “Because thou knowest these things ye are redeemed from the fall; therefore ye are brought back into my presence; therefore I show myself unto you,” and then, “Behold, I am Jesus Christ” ([[Ether 3:13–14]]). *My* presence, as Kings had *my* sight. The one who speaks in Kings is Jehovah, and “Jehovah is the premortal Jesus Christ,” the Bible Dictionary says, citing the Kirtland vision.[@bd-jehovah] So the voice that said “I will remove Judah also out of my sight” and the voice that says “ye are brought back into my presence” are one voice. The Topical Guide sets Ether 3:13 with Samuel's “that thereby men may be brought into the presence of the Lord” ([[Hel. 14:15]]) under “God, Presence of,”[@tg-god-presence-of] and the Genesis page follows that pairing to its end.

Christ also promised to show Himself to the people chapter 17 describes as removed. “I go unto the Father, and also to show myself unto the lost tribes of Israel, for they are not lost unto the Father, for he knoweth whither he hath taken them” ([[3 Ne. 17:4]]); the Church's study helps on chapter 17 quote those words of these exiles.[@scripture-helps-2-kings-16-25] Chapter 17 said the LORD removed them; the Savior says the Father “hath taken them.” The people the LORD “removed out of his sight” are the people the risen Lord says He will put His face before. Nothing in that sentence says where they are, and neither do I.

The Restoration finished the thought in a house. On 3 April 1836 two men “saw the Lord standing upon the breastwork of the pulpit, before us,” and He said, “I am he who liveth, I am he who was slain; I am your advocate with the Father” ([[D&C 110:2–4]]). After that vision closed, Moses “committed unto us the keys of the gathering of Israel from the four parts of the earth, and the leading of the ten tribes from the land of the north” ([[D&C 110:11]]); the Church's study helps send a reader of 2 Kings 17 to that verse.[@scripture-helps-2-kings-16-25]

## The disciple

Lehi says the rule of this book as a doctrine, and I read his words as the sentence Kings was writing toward: “there is no flesh that can dwell in the presence of God, save it be through the merits, and mercy, and grace of the Holy Messiah” ([[2 Ne. 2:8]]). Jennifer Lane quotes that verse and concludes that “we can have no access to his presence on our own”; “access to the presence of the Lord is made possible only in and through Christ's Atonement.”[@lane-presence-panim] No people stayed before the face on its own merits, and no disciple does. A person who has sinned stands where Judah stood, under a sentence of separation. The way back begins with the one who stands before the face for him. Through His Atonement, “by obedience to the principles and ordinances of the gospel,” a man or woman can “overcome spiritual death.”[@gs-death-spiritual] Repentance reverses the sentence; it reverses it because of Him.

The face is open to the one who comes. Hezekiah spread his letter before the LORD and Josiah wept before Him, and the LORD said “I also have heard thee.” The Lord's own promise keeps Kings' noun: whoever forsakes sins and comes to Him and keeps His commandments “shall see my face and know that I am” ([[D&C 93:1]]). The coming is asked of the disciple; the access rests on the Son.

What the end of the book pictures is given by name in the Book of Mormon. Helaman closes a letter to Moroni: “may the Lord our God, who has redeemed us and made us free, keep you continually in his presence” ([[Alma 58:41]]), a presence Lane reads as something enjoyed in this life, not only after it.[@lane-presence-panim] Redeemed, made free, kept continually before a face: it is Jehoiachin's last verse, with the face the nation was made for. Christ is in the presence of God for us, and because He is, those removed from the face can be brought back into it, all of them by His resurrection; and the one who comes to Him and keeps His commandments may be kept there, as Helaman prayed, continually, all the days of his life.
