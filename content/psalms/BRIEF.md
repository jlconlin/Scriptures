## What the author has decided

Nothing for Psalms in particular yet. The author's standing rules are in `STANDARDS.md` §1 and `AUTHORING.md` and are not repeated here. How three of them land on this book:

- **Newest trumps oldest.** The newest is *Scripture Helps: Old Testament* (2025), three chapters that between them treat about seventy psalms. The Student Manual's one chapter on the whole book is older, and it defends David's authorship of the Psalter against “modern critics” in words *Scripture Helps* no longer uses (“attributed to,” “credited with”). Follow *Scripture Helps*.
- **Don't replicate the Gospel Library.** *Scripture Helps* prints a table of eight psalm verses beside their New Testament fulfilments, and answers some two dozen questions verse by verse (selah, the sons of Korah, Asaph, “ye are gods,” the two Lords of Psalm 110, “perfect hatred”). A note does not repeat one of those answers; it goes where the help stops.
- **Quote the Church's material sparingly.** President Nelson on *hesed* is printed in *Scripture Helps* at Psalm 50:5 and will tempt every psalm that has “lovingkindness” or “mercy.” It is quoted once on the site, at the first psalm where the word carries the note (Proposed: 51:1, or 136 if the author would rather), from the talk's own page; elsewhere, link.

## What a chapter is for here

A psalm is not a chapter of a longer argument. Each is a whole poem with its own speaker, occasion, and shape, and most are short: a reader can hold all of Psalm 23 in mind at once. So the reader's questions are a poem's questions. *Who is speaking, and to whom? What has happened to him? Where does the psalm turn, and why? What is this image? Why does he ask God to break his enemies' teeth? Did David write it? Is this about Christ, and how do we know?*

**The thread is the psalm's movement.** Most psalms turn: from complaint to trust, from a call to praise to the reasons for it, from the king's danger to his victory. The `thread` says where the turn is and what makes it. A lament that ends in praise without saying what changed (Psalm 13, Psalm 22 at verse 21) is the question, not a problem to smooth over.

**Say what the psalm says of its own speaker before what it foretells.** Psalm 22 is first the prayer of a man surrounded and wasting, and then the psalm the Savior prayed on the cross and the evangelists saw fulfilled in detail. Give the prayer, then the fulfilment, and keep them apart (“Messianic psalms,” below).

**Shared things are explained once.** A hundred and fifty poems share a small stock of words: selah, the title terms, *hesed*, Sheol, Zion and the holy hill, the horn, the right hand, the wicked and the righteous, the anointed. Each is explained once, at the place this brief names, and every other psalm points there with a `[[reference]]`. This matters more here than in any book so far: without it the same note will be written forty times. `check-content psalms --repeats` after each batch shows what crept in.

**Short psalms get short chapters.** Psalm 117 has two verses, 131 and 133 and 134 three each. `setting` may fall under the checker's 120 words for these, and the warning is ignored; two or three notes is a full chapter. Don't pad. Psalm 119 (176 verses) is the other extreme: one section to a stanza of eight verses, and notes on what a reader asks (the eight words for the law, the acrostic, the lines everyone knows), not on every stanza.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, which psalms share lines, how a modern translation prints a verse, which later scripture takes up which line) is the coordinator's direction of where to look, some of it from memory. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **The titles.** Most psalms have a title above verse 1 (“A Psalm of David, when he fled from Absalom his son”). In the Hebrew it is part of the text, usually verse 1, so **Hebrew verse numbers in those psalms run one ahead of the King James** (two ahead in a few); `scripts/hebrew.mjs psalms 3:1` prints the title's words first and gives both numbers. Commentaries may follow the Hebrew; cite the King James verse. The Gospel Library prints the title under the chapter heading; **`data/kjv/psalms.json` does not have the titles, and the site does not show them** (Ps2 in `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`). Until that is settled a note cannot hang on a title's words; what a title says goes in `setting`.
- **What a title is worth.** The Bible Dictionary: “it is open to question whether these are as old as the words to which they are attached,” and “it has been concluded that the Hebrew titles are sometimes inaccurate.” *Scripture Helps* says psalms are “attributed to” David and that Asaph “is credited with” his. The New Testament and the Savior quote particular psalms as David's (Psalm 110 in `[[Matt. 22:43]]`; Psalm 16 in `[[Acts 2:25]]`; Psalm 2 in `[[Acts 4:25]]`). **Proposed:** a chapter says “the title ascribes it to David” or “a psalm of David by its title” and takes the occasion a title gives (Absalom, Doeg, the cave, Bathsheba) as the setting the psalm asks to be read in, with the narrative chapter cited from 1 or 2 Samuel. Where the New Testament names David, the chapter says David without hedging. No chapter argues for or against a title, and none dates a psalm by its language or theology except from a source read, as that source's.
- **`when`.** Proposed: from the title where there is one (“By its title, David's, when he fled from Absalom”); “Undated” otherwise, with the little the psalm itself shows (137 is by the rivers of Babylon; 126 looks back on the return). KnoWhy #867 and *Scripture Helps* both say the book was gathered over a long time and likely put together after the exile; that is said once, on the book page and in Psalm 1's setting.
- **The title terms.** The Bible Dictionary's entry “Psalms” explains them in one paragraph (Neginoth, Nehiloth, Alamoth, Sheminith, Maschil, Michtam, Shiggaion, Gittith, the tune names); the Student Manual (28-4) has a longer list. **There is no Bible Dictionary entry “Maschil” or “Michtam”** (those addresses return the index); there are entries “Selah,” “Hallel,” “Degrees, songs of,” “Asaph,” “Korah,” “David,” “Music.” Each term is explained where it first appears, in `setting`, in a sentence, and never again: “To the chief Musician” and Neginoth at Psalm 4, Nehiloth at 5, Sheminith at 6, Shiggaion at 7, Gittith at 8, Michtam at 16, Maschil at 32, the sons of Korah at 42, Alamoth at 46, Asaph at 50, the songs of degrees at 120. Most are uncertain, and the chapter says so in the Bible Dictionary's own hedges (“seem to imply,” “may refer”).
- **Selah.** Explained once, at 3:2, its first use: the meaning is uncertain (*Scripture Helps*, at Psalm 3:2; the Bible Dictionary, “Selah”; Hopkin and Hopkin say nearly all scholars take it as an instruction to the performers). No later psalm has a note on it.
- **Numbering in the Greek.** The Septuagint counts most psalms one lower than the Hebrew and English (Psalm 23 is its 22). Brenton's translation on Bible Hub is arranged by the English number with the Greek in parentheses (`biblehub.com/sep/psalms/23.htm` opens “1(22:1)”). When citing the Septuagint, give the English number and say “the Greek” without the other number unless a source cited uses it.
- **Kinds of psalm.** Scholars sort the psalms by form: laments of one person or of the people, hymns of praise, thanksgivings, royal psalms, wisdom psalms. KnoWhy #438 and Larsen's *Old Testament Minute* use the terms. They help a reader see why a psalm is built as it is (a lament's address, complaint, plea, and vow of praise), so `thread` may use one, defined in a clause. Don't open a chapter by classifying it, and don't say “scholars classify.”
- **Poetry.** Hebrew verse says a thing twice: the second half of a line echoes, sharpens, or answers the first. That is explained once, at Psalm 1 or 2 (`ot-manual-g`, the Student Manual's enrichment section on Hebrew literary styles, is in `sources.yaml`; look first for a BYU or Scripture Central treatment). Afterward a note points out parallelism only where it settles what a line means (Psalm 8:4's “man” and “son of man”; 22:16–20), and a `structure` note is written only when the shape is the message (`STANDARDS.md` §7). Acrostics (a lead: Psalms 9–10, 25, 34, 37, 111, 112, 119, 145) are said in a sentence in `setting`; *Scripture Helps* explains 119's.
- **Names of God.** “LORD” in capitals is the divine name; “Lord” is *adonai*; “God” is *Elohim* or *El*. Psalm 110:1 has the first two side by side, and *Scripture Helps* explains them there. Copy any phrase with a name from `data/kjv/psalms.json`: the edition's capitals were checked verse by verse. Books Two and Three use “God” far more than “LORD” (a lead: Psalm 14 and Psalm 53 are one psalm with the names changed); say it where a reader would notice, from a source read.
- **Psalms that repeat.** Leads, each to be confirmed in the text before it is said: Psalm 18 is David's song in 2 Samuel 22; 14 and 53 are nearly one psalm; 70 is 40:13–17; 108 joins the ends of 57 and 60; parts of 96, 105 and 106 are sung in 1 Chronicles 16; 9 and 10, and 42 and 43, may each be one poem. The second chapter says so in a sentence, links to the first, and notes only what differs.
- **Other books on the site.** Isaiah, Jeremiah, Lamentations, Ezekiel, Daniel, Hosea, Joel, Malachi and Genesis are written. Link where a psalm shares a line or an image with one of them (the shepherd of Psalm 23 and `[[Ezek. 34:11]]`; Psalm 137 and Lamentations; Leviathan in 74 and 104 and `[[Isa. 27:1]]`; “The LORD reigneth” and Isaiah 52:7), and don't say who borrowed from whom except as a source read says.
- **The Book of Mormon.** Nephi and Jacob use the Psalms more than anyone else in the book: Hilton counts the allusions and finds sixty-three percent in those two; Scripture Central has KnoWhys on Jacob's use of Psalm 95 (#62), Nephi's of Psalm 24 (#51), Lehi's of Psalm 51 (#325), Zenos's of Psalm 46 (#313), laments (#438), temple psalms (#437), and “Nephi's psalm” (#30). A note may say that a Nephite prophet takes up the line, from the KnoWhy or Hilton, where it shows how the psalm was read. **No comparison panel**, and nothing is written for a Book of Mormon chapter (`AUTHORING.md`: no more Book of Mormon content for now).
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** Joseph Fielding McConkie's essay counts changes in nearly two hundred verses of Psalms, most of them small, and almost two dozen major ones, more than in any other book of the Writings; David LeFevre's chapter dates the work to early 1833 and reads the changes beside the Prophet's own circumstances. The Church's edition carries few of them: the saved footnotes show a Joseph Smith Translation reading in eleven psalms only (11, 14, 15, 19, 24, 30, 32, 50, 109, 138, 141). A change that is not in the footnotes (Psalm 46's, which *Scripture Helps* quotes in part) is used only from a page that prints it: *Scripture Helps*, McConkie, or LeFevre. Use a change where it answers a reader's question (Psalm 14:1, where the fool's saying becomes a denial that anyone has seen God, has a BYU Studies article); a change the footnote already shows is not a note by itself.
- **Hymns.** Many hymns come from psalms, and the hymnbook has a scripture index. That is reception history (`STANDARDS.md` §1, “Not a compendium”): leave it out, except a line in `explore` where a psalm is sung by the Saints nearly as written (Psalm 23; Psalm 100).
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports what runs across the book; the coordinator adds it to `THEME-CANDIDATES.md`. No theme page is written until the author chooses it.

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter; don't re-explain what another chapter explains, point with a `[[reference]]`; a Witness of Christ does not open by saying what the psalm lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer, since the site shows it alone as the note's card; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; “In plain words” adds nothing to the verse, and with poetry the risk is the opposite one too: don't flatten an image into its moral; Hebrew only where it changes what the reader understands; don't join two verses yourself and call it the text's meaning; no personal application, which in the Psalms means no “when we feel as David felt.”

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that a hundred and fifty chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-10.

- **Messianic psalms: prophecy, or prayer later fulfilled?** Hopkin and Hopkin put the two views plainly: that an inspired David “foretold precisely and clearly of Jesus Christ,” and that the psalms were written “to reflect the yearnings, experiences, and understanding of Israelites in their time” and were seen afterward to fit the Savior's life; and they answer, “For Latter-day Saints, these two viewpoints need not be seen as mutually exclusive.” *Scripture Helps*: such psalms “prophetically refer to the life and mission of Jesus Christ.” The Bible Dictionary: “No book of the Old Testament is more Christian in its inner sense.” **Proposed:** the chapter gives the psalm as its speaker's prayer or the king's song first, then what the New Testament does with it, quoting the New Testament verse; where the Savior or an apostle says the psalm spoke of Him (Psalm 110, Psalm 16, Psalm 22, Psalm 118:22, Psalm 2), the chapter says so as scripture's own reading and does not hedge it. A psalm no scripture or Church source reads of Christ gets a short `christ` section resting on what the psalm says of the Lord, who is Jehovah, and no typology of the writer's own. The psalms the endnotes of *Scripture Helps* call royal (2, 18, 45, 72, 89, 110, 132) are about Israel's king first; say what was asked of and promised to the king, then where the New Testament carries it.
- **Psalm 22.** Shon Hopkin's article in *BYU Studies* is the Latter-day Saint study, and it calls Psalm 22 and Isaiah 53 “the twin pillars of Old Testament prophecy regarding Christ.” Isaiah 53 is on the site: link. **“They pierced my hands and my feet” (22:16)**: the Hebrew text as printed reads differently (“like a lion”), the Septuagint has “pierced,” and a Dead Sea scroll bears on it; KnoWhy #641 and Hopkin's earlier article (“The Psalm 22:16 Controversy,” *BYU Studies* 44, no. 3) lay it out. Give the evidence in the order text, Greek, scroll, and the conclusion as theirs. What the Savior's cry of verse 1 meant (whether the Father withdrew) has been taught by apostles; quote one, from the talk's own page, and don't adjudicate beyond it.
- **David.** The psalms of David's trouble are read beside his story, including its worst part. Psalm 51's title places it after Nathan came to him. *Scripture Helps* there: David “could be forgiven of his sins, except for the murder of Uriah.” `[[D&C 132:39]]` says he “hath fallen from his exaltation,” and Peter that his soul was not left in hell reads Psalm 16 of Christ and of David's own hope (`[[Acts 2:27–31]]`; *Scripture Helps* at 16:10). The chapter on 51 says this once, from those sources, and the other penitential psalms link to it. Elsewhere David is the psalmist the titles and the New Testament present, and a chapter does not keep reminding the reader of Bathsheba.
- **The cursing psalms** (a lead: 35, 58, 69, 83, 109, 137:8–9, 139:19–22, and lines in many others). A reader stops at every one. *Scripture Helps* answers at 139:19–22 in three sentences: the words “may have been used to express unwavering loyalty to God,” and “we should keep in mind the higher law taught by Jesus Christ,” quoting the Sermon on the Mount. The Student Manual (28-5) prints a longer answer from a handbook: the psalmist is a plaintiff before a judge, and “Vengeance is always seen as God's province, and his alone.” **Proposed:** one full note, at the first psalm where a curse is the question (Psalm 5:10 or 35, whichever is written first in the batch plan; later ones point to it), saying three things from sources read: the psalmist asks God to act and does not act himself; the prayer is against those who attack the helpless or God's people, in the terms of the covenant's own curses; and the Savior's command to love enemies is the higher law. Then each later psalm notes only what is its own (who the enemy is; that Peter applies 69:25 and 109:8 to Judas in `[[Acts 1:20]]`; that 137's last verse answers what Babylon did, described and not quoted). Never explain a curse away as “only poetry,” and never apply one.
- **“Ye are gods” (82:6) and the council of gods.** *Scripture Helps*: the psalm opens with God “standing among a group of heavenly beings. This setting, sometimes called the divine council, is described in several places throughout the Old Testament,” and yet “God appears to be addressing those He had appointed as judges but who had judged unjustly,” so that the phrase “likely refers to” their delegated authority; the Savior cites the verse in `[[John 10:34]]`; and “Modern prophets have referenced Psalm 82:6 when teaching that we are the children of God.” Latter-day Saint scholars have argued at length that the “gods” are heavenly beings (the FARMS Review exchange with Michael Heiser is on Scripture Central's archive; Daniel McClellan's article in *Interpreter* could not be opened). **Proposed:** follow *Scripture Helps*, which holds both the council and the judges; give the heavenly-beings reading as one that Latter-day Saint scholars have defended, from a page read; quote the Savior. The same care at 8:5, where *Scripture Helps* says “angels” translates *Elohim* and that “a little lower than God” is more accurate, and at 89:5–7, 95:3, 97:7 and 138:1.
- **Sheol and what the psalmist knew of life after death.** “In death there is no remembrance of thee” (6:5), “Shall the dust praise thee?” (30:9), “the dead praise not the LORD” (115:17), beside 16:10, 49:15 and 73:24. *Scripture Helps* (at 16:10): “hell” is *sheol*, “the place where all people go after death, regardless of their moral character,” and “generally viewed as inescapable.” Explain *sheol* once, at 6:5; say what each psalm says without correcting it from later revelation in the psalmist's mouth, and then what the Restoration adds (the spirit world: the Guide to the Scriptures or a talk read), as addition.
- **“In sin did my mother conceive me” (51:5).** *Scripture Helps* answers it with Brigham Young: not original sin. It sits beside the verse, so the chapter does not repeat it; if a note is written it is on what the Hebrew parallelism is doing in the line.
- **The temple in the Psalms.** The richest Latter-day Saint writing on the book: Skinner on seeing God in His temple; Hopkin and Hopkin on psalms sung by Levites during the ordinances; KnoWhy #437 (“temple” appears in Psalms more than in any Old Testament book but 2 Chronicles); Parry's proposal that “generation” in 24:6 could be read as a circle of worshippers; KnoWhy #642 and Matthew Brown on the king grasping God's hand (73:23); Larsen's *Old Testament Minute*, which reads Psalm 1's tree as the tree of life by the temple and the blessed man as the king of Psalm 2. Much of this is reconstruction: what was said or done at the gate, in a procession, at an enthronement. **Proposed:** what the psalm itself says about the house, the hill, the gates, the altar, and the face of God is stated plainly; a reconstruction of ritual is given as “one reading” or “has been proposed,” once, with its source in the note's `sources`; and no note draws a line from a psalm to the modern temple endowment. Psalm 24:3–4 is the anchor (*Scripture Helps* has a section; Elder Bednar's “Clean Hands and a Pure Heart” is in its Learn More): the full note on ascending the hill of the Lord is there, and 15, 26, 27, 43, 84, 118:19–20 and 120–134 point to it.
- **Psalm 110.** Two Lords (verse 1) and a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek (verse 4); *Scripture Helps* treats both, with `[[Alma 13:1–19]]` and D&C 107 behind the second. The most quoted psalm in the New Testament (a lead; say it only from a source read). Melchizedek is on the site at `[[Gen. 14:18]]`: link. The note's own work is the Savior's question in `[[Matt. 22:41–46]]` and how Hebrews 5–7 argues from verse 4.
- **Creation and the sea monster** (74:13–14; 89:9–10; 104:26). KnoWhy #866 is on Leviathan and on creation told as the Lord's victory over the sea. Isaiah 27:1 and 51:9 on the site touch it; link, and give the KnoWhy's reading as its reading. These verses are poetry about the Creator's power, and no chapter sets them against Genesis 1.
- **Hesed.** “Lovingkindness” and “mercy” in the King James (Psalm 136 has it in every verse). *Scripture Helps* at 26:1–3, 50:5, 51:1 and 103:17–18 explains it as covenantal love, with President Nelson. One full note (see “Quote the Church's material sparingly,” above), with the Hebrew from `hebrew.mjs` and a lexicon page; `[[Hosea 6:4]]` on the site has a note on the word: read it first and agree with it.
- **Psalm 137:9.** Described, not quoted (`STANDARDS.md` §3). It is the cursing-psalms question at its hardest; see above, and `scripture-helps-violence` (the appendix on violence in the Old Testament, in `sources.yaml`).
- **Psalm 45, a royal wedding song** quoted of the Son in `[[Heb. 1:8–9]]`. “Thy throne, O God” is addressed to the king in the psalm; say what the line is in the psalm and then what Hebrews does. Whether it bears on the Savior's marriage is not raised.
- **Psalm 2:7, “this day have I begotten thee.”** KnoWhy #865 is on it (and #655 on the baptism). The king's adoption at his enthronement, and then `[[Acts 13:33]]` and `[[Heb. 1:5]]`. Don't build a doctrine of when the Son was begotten from the psalm.
