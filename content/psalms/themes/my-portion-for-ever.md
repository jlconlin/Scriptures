Psalm 1 opens the book of Psalms with a promise. The man whose delight is in the law of the LORD “shall be like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that bringeth forth his fruit in his season; his leaf also shall not wither; and whatsoever he doeth shall prosper” (1:3). The Cambridge Bible names two psalms where that confidence is tested: “contrast with its simple confidence the questionings of 37 and 73, in which the problem of the prosperity of the wicked is treated as a trial of faith.”[@cambridge-psalms]

That pairing is the commentaries’ own. I read the two psalms as answering each other through shared words. Psalm 37 tells a man who sees the wicked prosper not to envy them, and it promises him an end: “the end of that man is peace” (37:37). Psalm 73 is the testimony of a man who did envy them, and the word for what he saw and envied is that same word for peace. In the sanctuary he is given Psalm 37’s other word, their “end” (73:17). Then he names what he has, in a word Psalm 37 never uses: “God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever” (73:26). What he names is what the priests were given in place of land, when the LORD said to Aaron, “I am thy part” (Num. 18:20). The man who envied the wicked their peace ends with the portion that was never land at all. The Savior later repeated Psalm 37’s promise as a blessing, and latter-day revelation sets that inheritance within His redemption.

No source I have read says the Psalter arranges Psalms 1, 37, 73 and 92 to be read in that order, and I do not claim it. The joins are in the words.

<h2 id="whatsoever-he-doeth">“Whatsoever he doeth shall prosper”</h2>

Psalm 1 sets two ways side by side. The blessed man’s “delight is in the law of the LORD; and in his law doth he meditate day and night” (1:2), and the next verse gives his reward, first as a tree and then plainly: “whatsoever he doeth shall prosper.” The verb is *ṣālaḥ*, whose literal meaning is “to carry through to a successful result.”[@oshb-wlc][@cambridge-psalms] The tree and its channels are discussed in the note on [Psalm 1:3](/psalms/1/).

The psalm does not leave the wicked out. “The ungodly are not so: but are like the chaff which the wind driveth away,” and it closes, “For the LORD knoweth the way of the righteous: but the way of the ungodly shall perish” (1:4, 6). So the end of the wicked is already in Psalm 1. What the psalm does not mention is a season in between, when the wicked man is the one who prospers and the righteous man has to watch. The Cambridge Bible calls it “an appropriate prologue to the Psalter, which records the manifold experiences of the godly.”[@cambridge-psalms] The promise comes first; the experiences come after.

<h2 id="fret-not">“Fret not thyself because of him who prospereth”</h2>

Psalm 37 is written for the man who has seen that season. It opens, “Fret not thyself because of evildoers, neither be thou envious against the workers of iniquity” (37:1), and a few lines later names what he has seen: “fret not thyself because of him who prospereth in his way, because of the man who bringeth wicked devices to pass” (37:7). “Prospereth” is Psalm 1’s verb, and the lexicon files the two verses together: “Psalm 37:7, so probably Psalm 1:3.”[@oshb-wlc][@bdb] The word Psalm 1 promised to the righteous man is here the wicked man’s.

Psalm 37 answers with patience and a promise. “Rest in the LORD, and wait patiently for him” (37:7); “those that wait upon the LORD, they shall inherit the earth” (37:9); “the meek shall inherit the earth; and shall delight themselves in the abundance of peace” (37:11). For now it allows that the righteous may have less: “A little that a righteous man hath is better than the riches of many wicked” (37:16). Nor is the promise only land. The LORD is the psalm’s subject throughout, “the salvation of the righteous is of the LORD” (37:39), and it already says “for ever”: “The LORD knoweth the days of the upright: and their inheritance shall be for ever” (37:18). Delitzsch judges that this everlasting inheritance “is not as yet thought of as extending into the future world, as in the New Testament.”[@delitzsch-psalms]

Then it turns to how things end. “Mark the perfect man, and behold the upright: for the end of that man is peace. But the transgressors shall be destroyed together: the end of the wicked shall be cut off” (37:37–38). “End” is *ʾaḥărît*, what comes after; the Church’s footnote at verse 38 gives “HEB future.”[@oshb-wlc][@lds-scriptures] “Peace” is *šālôm*.[@oshb-wlc] One line holds both words. As the King James Version reads it, the upright man’s future is peace; the lexicon files the word here under “posterity,” “possibly not more than ‘a future’,”[@bdb] and a modern reading has “posterity for the peaceable” (see the note on [Psalm 37:37](/psalms/37/)). Either way Psalm 37 joins peace to the righteous man, and it promised the meek “the abundance of peace” in so many words (37:11). The Pulpit Commentary, at the psalm’s first verse, already reaches forward to Psalm 73: faith in God’s justice “will enable us calmly to await ‘the end’ (Psalm 73:17).”[@pulpit-psalms] The notes on [Psalm 37](/psalms/37/) take up the verb “fret” and the senses of “end.”

<h2 id="envious">“I was envious at the foolish”</h2>

Psalm 73 begins by confessing the envy Psalm 37 forbids. “But as for me, my feet were almost gone; my steps had well nigh slipped. For I was envious at the foolish, when I saw the prosperity of the wicked” (73:2–3). “Envious” is the verb of 37:1, “neither be thou envious,” and Delitzsch and the Cambridge Bible both point back to that verse.[@oshb-wlc][@delitzsch-psalms][@cambridge-psalms]

“Prosperity” here is not Psalm 1’s verb. It is *šālôm*: “Lit. the peace.”[@oshb-wlc][@cambridge-psalms] When Psalm 73 later says the ungodly “prosper in the world” (73:12), the Hebrew is another word again, and the psalm never uses Psalm 1’s verb at all.[@oshb-wlc]

Read together, the two psalms meet on that word for peace. Psalm 37 promised the meek “the abundance of peace” (37:11) and told its hearer to look at the upright man, “for the end of that man is peace.” The man of Psalm 73 looked and saw peace, but in the hands of the wicked, and now. What Psalm 37 promised as the end of the righteous he saw as the present of the wicked, and the sight nearly took his feet. I take his trouble to be more than that the wicked do well. The promise itself seems to have gone to the wrong people. A Church article on the psalm names his fault differently: Asaph felt “like the wicked were always prospering,” and “his problem was his inclination to see things from a temporal and temporary perspective.”[@lds-bible-verses-strength]

He says what it cost him: “Verily I have cleansed my heart in vain, and washed my hands in innocency. For all the day long have I been plagued, and chastened every morning” (73:13–14). The complaint is old. Job asks, “Wherefore do the wicked live, become old, yea, are mighty in power?” (Job 21:7). Jeremiah asks, with Psalm 1’s verb, “Wherefore doth the way of the wicked prosper?” (Jer. 12:1).[@oshb-wlc] Malachi’s people say, “It is vain to serve God” (Mal. 3:14). Psalm 49 takes up the question as well, and the commentaries read it with 37 and 73;[@ellicott-psalms][@delitzsch-psalms] it has a page of its own, [Who Can Ransom a Life?](/psalms/themes/who-can-ransom/).

He could not think his way out of it. “When I thought to know this, it was too painful for me; Until I went into the sanctuary of God; then understood I their end” (73:16–17). What he understood there is given in one word, and it is Psalm 37’s. “Their end” is *ʾaḥărît*, the word of “the end of that man is peace” and “the end of the wicked shall be cut off.”[@oshb-wlc] The lexicon gives it here the sense “final lot,” and files Psalm 37’s two under “posterity” or “a future.”[@bdb]

Psalm 37 had put the two words in one line, the end and the peace. Psalm 73 has them apart. Its speaker saw the peace first (73:3) and understood the end only in the sanctuary (73:17), and once he understood the end of the wicked, their peace lost its hold on him: “How are they brought into desolation, as in a moment!” (73:19). Readings differ on whether that end comes in this life or afterward. Ellicott thinks of consequences, “but still consequences in this world,”[@ellicott-psalms] and the Church’s heading to the psalm says the wicked “will be consumed with terrors hereafter.”[@lds-scriptures] The notes on [Psalm 73](/psalms/73/) discuss the peace of the wicked and the sanctuary.

<h2 id="my-portion">“My portion for ever”</h2>

By verse 20 Psalm 73 has reached Psalm 37’s answer: the wicked come to ruin. It does not stop there. “Nevertheless I am continually with thee: thou hast holden me by my right hand. Thou shalt guide me with thy counsel, and afterward receive me to glory. Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon earth that I desire beside thee. My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever” (73:23–26).

Verse 24 is read two ways. The Church’s heading takes it of the life to come, “Those who trust in the Lord will be received up unto glory”;[@lds-scriptures] the Cambridge Bible calls its meaning “much disputed” and reads “glory” as honour within this life.[@cambridge-psalms] The note on [73:24](/psalms/73/) sets out the readings.

Verse 26 is said of a failing body, not of lost wealth. “Strength” is literally “rock,” and the Cambridge Bible reads the verse “Though bodily and mental powers fail”: God is “the possession which cannot be taken from him,” and the speaker will no longer “envy those ‘whose portion in life is of the world’ (Psalm 17:14).”[@cambridge-psalms] Delitzsch paraphrases: “Though his outward and inward man perish, nevertheless God remains ever the rock of his heart.”[@delitzsch-psalms] The speaker does not say his flesh will be spared. He says what would remain if it failed.

“Portion” is *ḥēleq*, “a portion assigned, whether of land or property or food.”[@cambridge-psalms] Psalm 37 never uses it; its words for what the righteous receive are “inheritance” and “the land” (37:18, 29).[@oshb-wlc] Numbers 18:20 uses the nouns for portion and inheritance of the LORD. He said to Aaron, “Thou shalt have no inheritance in their land, neither shalt thou have any part among them: I am thy part and thine inheritance among the children of Israel” (Num. 18:20): “part” is *ḥēleq*, and “inheritance” is the noun of Psalm 37:18.[@oshb-wlc] What set the priest apart was not the word for his share but what the share was: no land, and the LORD. The lexicon files the two verses under one sense, the LORD as “possession of his servants: of priests … Numbers 18:20 … in spiritual sense Psalm 73:26.”[@bdb] The Cambridge Bible says at Psalm 16:5 that the language “reminds us of the Levites, who had no portion or inheritance, but Jehovah was their portion,” and that spiritually He was the portion “of individual Israelites (Psalm 73:26 …).”[@cambridge-psalms] The title gives Psalm 73 to Asaph, “A cymbal-playing Levite.”[@bd-asaph] The note on [Psalm 73:26](/psalms/73/), “A Levite’s portion,” follows the word.

This is where I take the two psalms to arrive. Psalm 37 promised the righteous the land and peace. The speaker of Psalm 73, given Psalm 37’s word “end” in the sanctuary, does not close by claiming Psalm 37’s land. He names God as his portion, as the LORD was the portion of the tribe that received no land. The man who envied the wicked their peace ends with the portion that was never land. Psalm 37 said the upright’s inheritance “shall be for ever” (37:18); Psalm 73’s “for ever” is said of God, his portion. The everlasting thing in Psalm 73 is not a possession that lasts but the One possessed.

Psalm 92 gives the righteous a place to stand. It grants that “the wicked spring as the grass,” and adds, “it is that they shall be destroyed for ever” (92:7). Then: “The righteous shall flourish like the palm tree: he shall grow like a cedar in Lebanon. Those that be planted in the house of the LORD shall flourish in the courts of our God” (92:12–13). “Planted” is the verb of Psalm 1’s tree; in the Psalter that verb occurs only at 1:3 and 92:13.[@oshb-wlc] Psalm 92 sets the righteous in the LORD’s house, which Delitzsch calls “His holy Temple.”[@delitzsch-psalms] The Cambridge Bible’s introduction to Psalm 92 joins it to the other two: “The problems which perplexed the authors of Psalms 37, 73 will ultimately receive a triumphant solution, of which an earnest has been already experienced.”[@cambridge-psalms] The note on [Psalm 92:12](/psalms/92/) follows the two kinds of growth.

<h2 id="blessed-are-the-meek">“Blessed are the meek”</h2>

The Savior took Psalm 37’s promise into His own sermon: “Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth” (Matt. 5:5). He said it again to the Nephites (3 Ne. 12:5). “When Jesus promised that the meek would inherit the earth, He was quoting from Psalm 37:11,” and “To ‘inherit the earth’ (Matthew 5:5) means to inherit the celestial kingdom.”[@nt-student-manual-3]

Latter-day revelation places the inheritance within the Redemption. “Through the redemption which is made for you is brought to pass the resurrection from the dead,” and “the redemption of the soul is through him that quickeneth all things, in whose bosom it is decreed that the poor and the meek of the earth shall inherit it” (D&C 88:14, 17). The Doctrine and Covenants Student Manual says of the passage, “To inherit the earth means to inherit the celestial kingdom.”[@dc-student-manual-2017-88] The promise of Psalm 37 is kept through the Redeemer, in a celestial earth.

Psalm 73 found something Psalm 37 did not name. Elder D. Todd Christofferson, in a talk that does not cite the psalm, says what the Father offers: “What our Heavenly Father offers us is Himself and His Son, a close and enduring relationship with Them through the grace and mediation of Jesus Christ, our Redeemer.”[@christofferson-relationship-god] I read the two together: the portion Asaph claims at the psalm’s close, God Himself, is what the Father offers through His Son. Asaph claimed it though his flesh and heart should fail, and the talk goes on to quote Paul, who links suffering with the promise: “joint-heirs with Christ; if so be that we suffer with him, that we may be also glorified together” (Rom. 8:17).[@christofferson-relationship-god]

The disciple who keeps the commandments and sees others prosper without them stands where Psalm 73 begins, and the psalm does not tell him the sight is false. Elder Christofferson says that “not every blessing predicated on obedience to law is shaped, designed, and timed according to our expectations,” and that “The atoning power of Jesus Christ … ensures that God can and will fulfill His promises.”[@christofferson-relationship-god] President Spencer W. Kimball, as the Student Manual prints him, answered a woman who brought him the question, “Yours is an ancient question. Job and Jeremiah made the same complaint,” and then, “for many rewards you need not wait until the judgment day.”[@ot-manual-34] The meek are promised the earth through the Redeemer’s work. What Psalm 73 adds is a portion its speaker names now, and would name though his flesh and his heart failed: God Himself, “my portion for ever.”
