Several psalms hold to one promise: a king on David’s throne, for ever. Read in order, the book goes from the sworn promise to the fallen crown and unanswered question of Psalm 89, then to the LORD’s own reign, and then to the oath and the crown again. The New Testament says the oath was kept in Jesus. This page follows that reading order; it does not say which psalm was written or placed to answer which. Where it sets two passages side by side without a citation, the comparison is its own.

## The king and the oath

The LORD sets His king on Zion and calls him Son ([[Ps. 2:6–7]]; [Psalm 2](/psalms/2/)). Psalm 72 prays for what a king is for: “He shall judge the poor of the people” ([[Ps. 72:4]]), and his name is to last “as long as the sun” ([[Ps. 72:17]]). Its king, Solomon, is “a type of the Messiah.”[@lds-scriptures] See [Psalm 72](/psalms/72/).

Psalm 89 gives the promise as covenant and oath: “I have sworn unto David my servant, Thy seed will I establish for ever, and build up thy throne to all generations” ([[Ps. 89:3–4]]). “My covenant will I not break… Once have I sworn by my holiness that I will not lie unto David” ([[Ps. 89:34–35]]), though his sons could be punished ([[Ps. 89:30–33]]). See [Psalm 89](/psalms/89/).

<h2 id="cast-down">The crown on the ground</h2>

Then the same words turn: “thou hast been wroth with thine anointed” ([[Ps. 89:38]]); “Thou hast made void the covenant of thy servant: thou hast profaned his crown by casting it to the ground” ([[Ps. 89:39]]). In Hebrew, “thine anointed” is the same word as “his anointed” in [[Ps. 2:2]], and “My covenant will I not break” (89:34) has the same verb as “profaned” (89:39).[@oshb-wlc]

The singer asks: “How long, LORD?” ([[Ps. 89:46]]), and “Lord, where are thy former lovingkindnesses, which thou swarest unto David in thy truth?” ([[Ps. 89:49]]). The psalm’s own last words are reproach ([[Ps. 89:50–51]]); the blessing of verse 52 closes the third of the Psalms’ five books[@ot-manual-psalms] (see [Psalm 150](/psalms/150/)). Inside the psalm the question is not answered.

## The LORD reigneth

In the next book “The LORD reigneth” comes four times ([[Ps. 93:1]]; [[Ps. 96:10]]; [[Ps. 97:1]]; [[Ps. 99:1]]), with “Thy throne is established of old” ([[Ps. 93:2]]). “Righteousness and judgment are the habitation of his throne” ([[Ps. 97:2]]) is the same Hebrew phrase as [[Ps. 89:14]], “Justice and judgment are the habitation of thy throne.”[@oshb-wlc] Read after Psalm 89, where David’s throne was cast down, these psalms speak of a throne that is the LORD’s own.[@lds-scriptures] Many psalms of this fourth book “describe the millennial day when Israel will find rest under the Messiah’s reign.”[@scripture-helps-ps-1]

## The oath again

Psalm 132 has the oath as well, with Psalm 89’s words “sworn,” “David” and “throne”: “The LORD hath sworn in truth unto David… Of the fruit of thy body will I set upon thy throne,” with a condition for the children ([[Ps. 132:11–12]]; [Psalm 132](/psalms/132/)). Then: “upon himself shall his crown flourish” ([[Ps. 132:17–18]]). The Hebrew word for “crown” in that line occurs in Psalms only there and at [[Ps. 89:39]].[@oshb-wlc] Here the crown cast to the ground is set beside the crown that flourishes.

## The throne given

Peter uses the oath’s words: David knew “that God had sworn with an oath to him, that of the fruit of his loins, according to the flesh, he would raise up Christ to sit on his throne,” and “being a prophet… spake of the resurrection of Christ” ([[Acts 2:30–31]]). He cites [[Ps. 110:1]] ([[Acts 2:34–35]]; [Psalm 110](/psalms/110/)) and ends: “God hath made that same Jesus, whom ye have crucified, both Lord and Christ” ([[Acts 2:36]]). Of the child Jesus, the angel says the Lord God “shall give unto him the throne of his father David” ([[Luke 1:32–33]]). Zacharias says God “hath raised up an horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David” ([[Luke 1:69]]); on one older reading, Zacharias had [[Ps. 132:17]] in mind.[@cambridge-psalms]

None of these New Testament passages quotes the lament of Psalm 89:38–51. One older reading of Psalm 89 as a whole holds that “the Incarnation was the true solution of the Psalmist’s perplexity.”[@cambridge-psalms] Israel’s kings did not do all that a king was meant to do; Jesus would not fail[@sc-knowhy-865] (see [Psalm 2](/psalms/2/)).
