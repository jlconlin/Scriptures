Psalm 89 holds both the oath to David and the complaint that it has failed; one Latter-day Saint study calls it “the most extensive biblical exposition of the Davidic covenant.”[@taggart-sworn-david] In its first half the LORD speaks: “I have sworn unto David my servant, Thy seed will I establish for ever, and build up thy throne to all generations” (89:3–4). In its second half the singer speaks back to Him: “thou hast profaned his crown by casting it to the ground” (89:39), and then asks, “Lord, where are thy former lovingkindnesses, which thou swarest unto David in thy truth?” (89:49). The psalm does not answer. Its last verse, “Blessed be the LORD for evermore. Amen, and Amen” (89:52), is the blessing that closes the third of the five books of Psalms, not the poem’s reply.[@ot-manual-psalms]

I read the rest of the book as answering that question in Psalm 89’s own words, but of another throne and of a crown that flourishes. Book Four says “The LORD reigneth” and “Thy throne is established of old” (93:1–2), and repeats of the LORD’s throne the line Psalm 89 had sung of it (97:2; 89:14).[@cambridge-psalms] Psalm 132 recalls the oath to David and ends, “upon himself shall his crown flourish” (132:18), with the only other use in the Psalms of the Hebrew word for the crown of 89:39.[@oshb-wlc] One older reading names what the book leaves undone: the psalms of the LORD’s own reign and the psalms of His Anointed are two series that “converge in the Old Testament, but do not meet.”[@delitzsch-psalms] The same reading goes on: the history that fulfils them makes clear that the coming of the Anointed and the coming of the LORD “is one and the same.”[@delitzsch-psalms] The New Testament tells that history of Jesus, whom God raised up “to sit on his throne” (Acts 2:30).

No source I have read says that Book Four or Psalm 132 was written or placed to answer Psalm 89, and the commentaries disagree about their dates; one places Psalm 132 before Psalm 89.[@delitzsch-psalms] What follows is the book’s reading order, and the reading of it as an answer is mine. What it shows of the Savior’s work is that the oath was kept when the throne was given to One who is both the LORD who swore it and the son of David whom God raised from the dead (Acts 13:34). One Latter-day Saint study of the covenant says the first half plainly: Jesus “is simultaneously the promisor and the fulfillment of the promise.”[@taggart-sworn-david]

<h2 id="sworn">“I have sworn unto David my servant”</h2>

The oath is the promise Nathan carried to David: “I will set up thy seed after thee … If he commit iniquity, I will chasten him with the rod of men … But my mercy shall not depart away from him … thy throne shall be established for ever” (2 Sam. 7:12, 14–16). The Church’s edition sets Luke 1:33 beside that last line.[@lds-scriptures] Psalm 89 calls the promise a covenant and an oath and gives it at length in verses 19–37 (see “Nathan’s promise, called a covenant and an oath” on [Psalm 89:3](/psalms/89/)).

As the psalm states it, the oath has two parts, and it keeps them apart. The sons can fail: “If his children forsake my law … Then will I visit their transgression with the rod” (89:30, 32). The promise cannot: “My covenant will I not break, nor alter the thing that is gone out of my lips. Once have I sworn by my holiness that I will not lie unto David. His seed shall endure for ever, and his throne as the sun before me” (89:34–36). The “if” governs the chastening of the sons, not the throne (see “The sons may be punished; the oath stands” on [Psalm 89:30](/psalms/89/)). The covenant was “an unconditional promise to the house of David that a descendant from his seed would rule forever.”[@seely-messiah]

That is what makes the second half so hard. An oath that allowed for punished sons could absorb a bad reign; it could not absorb a fallen throne.

<h2 id="cast-down">“Thou hast profaned his crown”</h2>

The psalm turns at verse 38: “But thou hast cast off and abhorred, thou hast been wroth with thine anointed.” One older reading hears in its first words, “And Thou - the same who hast promised and affirmed this with an oath.”[@delitzsch-psalms]

The complaint is made in the oath’s own words. “Thou hast made void the covenant of thy servant: thou hast profaned his crown by casting it to the ground” (89:39). “Profaned” translates the verb used in “My covenant will I not break” (89:34).[@oshb-wlc] What God swore not to do to the covenant, the singer says He has done to the crown, and the throne that was to be “as the sun” (89:36) is “cast … down to the ground” (89:44). (The other echoes are set out in “The complaint in the oath’s own words” on [Psalm 89:39](/psalms/89/).)

Why should a crown be profaned? The word is *nēzer*, which means consecration and then the crown that marks it, “the crown or diadem of the high priest (Exodus 29:6) or the king (2 Samuel 1:10), as the mark of consecration to their office.”[@cambridge-psalms] The lexicon lists this verse and 132:18 together, of the crown as a “symbol of royal power.”[@bdb] The crown was holy, and to cast it down was to desecrate it: God “has therefore made the sacred thing vile by casting it to the ground.”[@delitzsch-psalms]

Then comes the question of verse 49, with “swarest” the verb of “I have sworn” in verses 3 and 35, in the same stem.[@oshb-wlc] The psalm’s own last words are about enemies who “have reproached the footsteps of thine anointed” (89:51). Verse 52, the formula that ends Book Three, has even so been heard as “a subtle gesture of hope.”[@taggart-sworn-david]

Whom the singer means, and when, is not settled. One reading sets the psalm in the exile, while Jehoiachin was “a dishonoured captive in Babylon”;[@cambridge-psalms] another in the reign of Rehoboam, after Shishak of Egypt “spoiled the Temple and Palace”;[@delitzsch-psalms] a third finds that “the time of the persecution of Antiochus Epiphanes suits best all the conditions presented by the psalm.”[@ellicott-psalms] (The first two are weighed in “Which king?” on [Psalm 89:45](/psalms/89/).)

The Church’s edition points from verse 39 to David’s own fall. It ties “casting” to the revelation that David, for “the case of Uriah and his wife,” has “fallen from his exaltation” (D&C 132:39).[@lds-scriptures] On this reading, David’s failure “resulted in a loss of blessings,” yet the narrative “asserts that God never abandoned David or the covenantal promise.”[@taggart-sworn-david] The psalm leaves its question open; the scripture set beside it speaks of David’s failure, not the oath’s.

<h2 id="the-lord-reigneth">“The LORD reigneth”</h2>

The next book of the Psalms speaks of a throne that was never cast down. “The LORD reigneth, he is clothed with majesty … Thy throne is established of old: thou art from everlasting” (93:1–2). The cry returns in 96:10, 97:1 and 99:1.

Psalm 97 goes on, “righteousness and judgment are the habitation of his throne” (97:2). Psalm 89 had said to the LORD, “Justice and judgment are the habitation of thy throne” (89:14). In Hebrew the two lines are the same four words but for “thy” and “his,”[@oshb-wlc] and the line in Psalm 97 is “taken from Psalm 89:14.”[@cambridge-psalms] I add one observation of my own: “It shall be established for ever as the moon” (89:37), said of David’s throne,[@cambridge-psalms] and “Thy throne is established” (93:2) are one Hebrew verb in the same stem.[@oshb-wlc]

One older reading takes these psalms as thanksgiving for the return from Babylon, after the LORD “seemed to have abdicated His throne”;[@cambridge-psalms] another allows that the writer “may be anticipating the final establishment of the reign of Messiah.”[@pulpit-psalms] The Church’s reading looks forward: its heading for Psalm 97 is “The Lord reigns in millennial glory,”[@lds-scriptures] and many of Psalms 90–106 “describe the millennial day when Israel will find rest under the Messiah’s reign.”[@scripture-helps-ps-1] The Church’s edition sets beside “he is clothed with majesty” (93:1) the Son of Man who comes “clothed in the brightness of his glory” (D&C 65:5), and beside “the LORD” of 96:13 the Topical Guide’s “Jesus Christ, Jehovah.”[@lds-scriptures]

Read after Psalm 89, these psalms sing again, after the lament, what the singer had sung before it. The throne cast to the ground was David’s; the throne “established of old” is the LORD’s. The later psalms echo Psalm 89’s wording about both thrones. That is the first half of the answer, and only half. Psalm 93 says nothing of David, and “The LORD reigneth” does not say what has become of the anointed whose footsteps were reproached. That is the gap the two series leave.

<h2 id="crown-flourish">“Upon himself shall his crown flourish”</h2>

Psalm 132 brings David back. The people pray, “For thy servant David’s sake turn not away the face of thine anointed” (132:10), and the answer is the oath: “The LORD hath sworn in truth unto David; he will not turn from it; Of the fruit of thy body will I set upon thy throne” (132:11). The verb is the one of 89:3, 35 and 49, in the same stem.[@oshb-wlc] One older reading sets this oath beside Psalm 89’s.[@pulpit-psalms]

Psalm 132 “resembles Psalms 89, with which it should be carefully compared.”[@cambridge-psalms] On that reading Psalm 132 is a psalm of hope from after the exile, while Psalm 89 comes from the exile “without one ray of hope.”[@cambridge-psalms] Another reading sets Psalm 132 in a time “in which the Davidic throne was still standing.”[@delitzsch-psalms]

The condition is still there: “If thy children will keep my covenant and my testimony that I shall teach them, their children shall also sit upon thy throne for evermore” (132:12). It was a condition of the line of kings continuing, not of the throne lasting for ever.[@delitzsch-psalms] (See “What the ‘if’ governs” on [Psalm 132:12](/psalms/132/).)

Then the LORD promises more than was asked: “There will I make the horn of David to bud: I have ordained a lamp for mine anointed” (132:17). “To bud” is related to *ṣemaḥ*, the Branch, a “title of the Messianic king” in Jeremiah and Zechariah[@cambridge-psalms] (see “The horn buds as the Branch grows” on [Psalm 132:17](/psalms/132/)). “Zacharias appears to have had this passage in mind” when he blessed God for “an horn of salvation” (Luke 1:69).[@cambridge-psalms]

And “upon himself shall his crown flourish” (132:18). The crown is *nēzer* again, and nowhere else in the Psalms.[@oshb-wlc] It is “used here as in Psalm 89:39,” and “flourish” is related to *ṣîṣ*, the plate of gold “bearing the inscription ‘Holiness to Jehovah’” on the high priest’s turban, so that the line seems “to suggest that David’s representative will have high-priestly as well as royal dignity.”[@cambridge-psalms] Another reading: “the crown consecrated by promise, which the seed of David wears, shall blossom like an unfading wreath.”[@delitzsch-psalms]

Read together, the two verses show the same consecrated crown twice. In 89:39 it is profaned and on the ground; in 132:18 it is on the head of David’s heir, and it flourishes. Psalm 132 does not say who that heir is, or how a line of sons who could be cut off would wear a crown that does not fade. It says only that the LORD “will not turn from it.”

<h2 id="christ-on-his-throne">“He would raise up Christ to sit on his throne”</h2>

The New Testament names the heir. At Pentecost Peter began from David’s grave and went on: “being a prophet, and knowing that God had sworn with an oath to him, that of the fruit of his loins, according to the flesh, he would raise up Christ to sit on his throne; He seeing this before spake of the resurrection of Christ” (Acts 2:29–31). Peter’s words take up the oath of 132:11.[@pulpit-psalms] He ends, “God hath made that same Jesus, whom ye have crucified, both Lord and Christ” (Acts 2:36). Beside “David” in Acts 2:34 the Church’s edition again sets D&C 132:39.[@lds-scriptures] David fell, and the oath sworn to him was kept.

The other witnesses agree. Gabriel told Mary that “the Lord God shall give unto him the throne of his father David … and of his kingdom there shall be no end” (Luke 1:32–33). Paul at Antioch: “Of this man’s seed hath God according to his promise raised unto Israel a Saviour, Jesus” (Acts 13:23), and the raising of Jesus from the dead is the giving of “the sure mercies of David” (Acts 13:34). “Mercies” there is Isaiah’s word (Isa. 55:3), the plural of *ḥesed*, the same word as the “lovingkindnesses” the singer asked for in 89:49.[@oshb-wlc] John greets the churches from “Jesus Christ, who is the faithful witness, and the first begotten of the dead, and the prince of the kings of the earth” (Rev. 1:5), in language borrowed from 89:27 and 37 (see [Psalm 89:37](/psalms/89/)).[@cambridge-psalms] None of these quotes the lament of 89:38–51. They take up the oath, and Peter, Paul and John set its keeping in the Resurrection.

The Church’s edition heads both Psalm 89 and Psalm 132 “A messianic psalm,” and Psalm 132 “Of the fruit of David’s loins will the Lord set One upon His throne”; it ties “establish” in 89:4 and “fruit” in 132:11 to the Topical Guide’s “Jesus Christ, Davidic Descent of.”[@lds-scriptures] The royal psalms picture “an ideal divine king priest judge never fully realized in any actual king of Israel. Only the Messiah combines these roles.”[@ot-manual-psalms] One older reading holds that “the Incarnation was the true solution of the Psalmist’s perplexity.”[@cambridge-psalms]

Here the psalms of the LORD’s reign and of His Anointed meet, as Delitzsch’s reading of Psalm 93 said they would.[@delitzsch-psalms] The conclusion I draw from the arc is about the crown. Book Four gave the LORD’s throne and Psalm 132 David’s flourishing crown. The New Testament gives David’s throne to Jesus, and in one Latter-day Saint reading He is also the LORD who swore the oath (on the LORD of the Psalms, see [Psalm 110:1](/psalms/110/)), “the one who covenanted to establish David’s seed and the one whose grace makes that establishment possible.”[@taggart-sworn-david] So the crown cast to the ground and the crown that flourishes are one crown. David’s sons lost it, as the “if” of 132:12 allowed. The LORD who reigns received it Himself, as David’s son according to the flesh, given the throne of His father David (Luke 1:32), and wears it as the first begotten of the dead. The singer asked where the lovingkindnesses sworn to David had gone; they came in the Lord Himself, born of David’s line and raised from the dead.

<h2 id="sure-mercies">“The sure mercies of David”</h2>

Psalm 89 is the prayer of someone holding a promise that looks void, and it sets God’s own words against what it sees. Its closing blessing, though no part of the original psalm, has been called “an expression of the faith which can bless God even when the visible signs of His love are withdrawn.”[@cambridge-psalms]

The promise reaches past David. Isaiah offers it to whoever will come: “Incline your ear, and come unto me: hear, and your soul shall live; and I will make an everlasting covenant with you, even the sure mercies of David” (Isa. 55:3). Paul says the promise made to the fathers “God hath fulfilled the same unto us their children, in that he hath raised up Jesus again” (Acts 13:32–33).

The human side stays conditional. David himself “paid, and is paying, a heavy price for his disobedience,” and his life “illustrates the need for all persons to endure in righteousness to the end.”[@bd-david] God “always honors human agency, but those who repent find the mercy and divine help that are always available.”[@taggart-sworn-david] See [Psalm 51:1](/psalms/51/) on *ḥesed* and those who stray. The oath does not fail, and a disciple keeps a place in it by enduring. A promise of God can look as lost as David’s crown in 89:39, and the LORD who swore that oath kept it Himself.
