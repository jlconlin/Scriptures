The book of Psalms opens with a man who loves the law. “Blessed is the man… his delight is in the law of the LORD; and in his law doth he meditate day and night” ([[Ps. 1:1–2]]). The psalm ends by dividing the world in two: “the LORD knoweth the way of the righteous: but the way of the ungodly shall perish” ([[Ps. 1:6]]). A hundred and eighteen psalms later the book's longest poem takes up the first psalm's subject and holds it for 176 verses. “O how love I thy law! it is my meditation all the day” ([[Ps. 119:97]]). Few in scripture say as much, or as often, about loving God's commandments as the singer of Psalm 119. And this is his last line:

> I have gone astray like a lost sheep; seek thy servant; for I do not forget thy commandments. ([[Ps. 119:176]])

“Lost” is the word Psalm 1 used for the way of the ungodly. The Hebrew verb is the same, *ʾābad*, to perish or be lost[@oshb-wlc], and in the Psalms it is said most often of the wicked, of enemies, of “they that are far from thee” ([[Ps. 73:27]]).[@oshb-wlc] Here the man who loves the law says it of himself. He asks God to seek him, and in the whole Psalter this is the only verse where God is asked to seek, *bāqaš*, a person.[@oshb-wlc] And in his final clause he keeps his loyalty: “for I do not forget thy commandments.”

That verse is the subject of this essay, and my argument runs in three steps. First, Psalms 19 and 119, having praised the law, turn to the law-lover's own need, and Psalm 119 ends in the sharpest form of that need. Second, the Psalter has already said who answers it: the law “restores the soul” in Psalm 19, and the shepherd “restores my soul” in Psalm 23, the same two Hebrew words, and only a shepherd can seek what is lost. Third, scripture names the shepherd. The Lord promises through Ezekiel, in the two words of the psalmist's prayer, “I will seek that which was lost”; Jesus says He came to do it, in the words of the psalm's own last verse; and the risen Lord tells the Nephites, “I am he that gave the law.” The giver of the law he loved is the Lord he asked to seek him. Some of the joins between these passages are in the commentaries and some are mine, and I say which as they come.

## The way of the ungodly shall perish

Psalm 1 is the book's door. Its blessed man does not walk with the ungodly, delights in the law, meditates in it day and night, and is “like a tree planted by the rivers of water” ([[Ps. 1:3]]); the wicked are chaff. Who the man is has been read more than one way. Scripture Central's commentary takes the tree of verse 3 as the Messiah[@ot-minute-psalms-1-20], and the site's notes on [Psalm 1](/psalms/1/) set out the case for the king. For this essay what matters is what the psalm says of the two ways, and what word it uses. The LORD “knoweth” the way of the righteous, a knowing that “involves approval, care, guidance.”[@cambridge-psalms] The way of the ungodly “shall perish,” *tōʾbēd*.[@oshb-wlc]

Follow that verb through the book. It is what God does to the wicked and what happens to His enemies: “the wicked shall perish” ([[Ps. 37:20]]); “so let the wicked perish at the presence of God” ([[Ps. 68:2]]); “thou hast destroyed all them that go a whoring from thee” ([[Ps. 73:27]]). Where the verb describes the speaker himself, it concerns danger from enemies or affliction, not sin. “I am forgotten as a dead man out of mind: I am like a broken vessel,” says one, where “broken” is this verb, a perishing vessel ([[Ps. 31:12]]).[@oshb-wlc] “Unless thy law had been my delights, I should then have perished in mine affliction,” says Psalm 119 in its ninety-second verse ([[Ps. 119:92]]), and three verses on, “The wicked have waited for me to destroy me” ([[Ps. 119:95]]).[@oshb-wlc] And in its last: “like a lost sheep,” the one place where what kind of perishing is meant is left open. The Cambridge commentary glosses the word: the lost sheep is one “strayed and in danger of perishing.”[@cambridge-psalms] So the word that closed Psalm 1 on the ungodly is the word the law-lover ends on. The comparison between the two verses is mine; I have found no commentary that sets them side by side. But a reader who begins at Psalm 1 and reads to the end of Psalm 119 may hear the echo.

## The law restores the soul, and then the plea

Between the two stands Psalm 19, the other great psalm of the law, and it shows in fourteen verses the movement that Psalm 119 makes in 176. Its second half praises the law under six names, and the first thing it says of it is this: “The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul” ([[Ps. 19:7]]). The site's note on that verse explains that “converting” is not a change of religion. The Hebrew is the common verb for turning back, in a causative form, with “soul” as its object: the law restores the soul, brings a life back.[@oshb-wlc] “Restoring, bringing back, i.e., imparting newness of life, quickening the soul,” Delitzsch writes, and in the same breath sends the reader to Psalm 23:3.[@delitzsch-psalms] Hold that cross-reference; it is the hinge of this essay.

Then the praise turns. “Who can understand his errors? cleanse thou me from secret faults. Keep back thy servant also from presumptuous sins” ([[Ps. 19:12–13]]). The law has been praised as perfect, and the psalmist's next thought is of himself. Scripture Central's commentary says it well: his reflections on the law's perfection “cause him to turn his thoughts inward as he considers how his own life measures up to the standard of that law and pleads with the Lord to help him do better.”[@ot-minute-psalms-1-20] Delitzsch calls the law “a mirror of self-knowledge” and says the prayer is for forgiveness of the sins of infirmity “to which, even the pardoned one succumbs.”[@delitzsch-psalms] The site's note on [Psalm 19:12](/psalms/19/) has the rest. The point for us is the shape: the praise of the law does not end in the law. It ends in a servant asking to be cleansed and kept.

Psalm 119 has the same shape, drawn out. Its singer says what the law has done for him in words Psalm 19 would own: “Thy word hath quickened me” ([[Ps. 119:50]]); “Unless thy law had been my delights, I should then have perished” ([[Ps. 119:92]]). He is a man under attack for his loyalty, as the site's note on [Psalm 119](/psalms/119/) shows: princes speak against him, the proud dig pits for him, he is “small and despised.” And he keeps asserting, against all of it, that he has held fast. “Yet I erred not from thy precepts” ([[Ps. 119:110]]). Sixty-six verses later: “I have gone astray.” The two verbs are one Hebrew verb, *tāʿâ*[@oshb-wlc], and the Greek translators used one Greek verb for both.[@lxx-rahlfs] The Cambridge commentary notices and does not flinch: “after all his professions of fidelity and constancy, even including an explicit declaration that in spite of intimidation he had not gone astray from God's commandments (Psalm 119:110), the Psalmist concludes with a confession of weakness and failure, actual or possible… The confession of failure is not inconsistent with the profession of devotion.”[@cambridge-psalms] It adds that Psalm 19:12–14 “may have been in the Psalmist's mind.”[@cambridge-psalms]

Two cautions, both from the sources. Delitzsch observes that the Hebrew accents may read the line as a condition, “If I have gone astray, seek Thou like a lost sheep Thy servant,” and on that reading “his going astray is no apostasy; his home, to which he longs to return when he has been betrayed into by-ways, is beside the Lord.”[@delitzsch-psalms] The Cambridge commentary, having given the confession reading, prefers another as “more in accordance with the general spirit of the Psalm”: the psalmist “is describing his outward circumstances rather than his spiritual state,” a wanderer “like a sheep that has been separated from the flock” and “exposed to constant dangers.”[@cambridge-psalms] That fits the man the psalm shows, hunted by princes, and it fits the word *ʾābad*, which is about perishing as much as about straying. The psalm does not settle the question, and I will not. What no reading can remove is the plea itself: a man who loves the law has asked God to come and find him.

He asks it with a word the Psalter uses often and never like this. “Seek,” *bāqaš*, occurs twenty-seven times in the book. Enemies seek the psalmist's life; the faithful seek the LORD and His face; the wicked seek to slay the righteous.[@oshb-wlc] Only here is the LORD the one asked to seek a man. And the man does not stop being who he was. The Cambridge commentary sends the reader from this verse to Jeremiah's strayed sheep,[@cambridge-psalms] and Jeremiah's is the one other verse in scripture that puts “lost sheep” and “forget” in the same breath: “My people hath been lost sheep: their shepherds have caused them to go astray… they have forgotten their restingplace” ([[Jer. 50:6]]). The contrast is what shows what Psalm 119 is doing, and the contrast is mine. The sheep of Psalm 119 is lost but has not forgotten the commandments. “For I do not forget thy commandments” is the reason he gives God to come.

## He restoreth my soul

Now the hinge. Four psalms after Psalm 19 the Psalter says of the LORD what Psalm 19 said of the law. “The LORD is my shepherd… He restoreth my soul” ([[Ps. 23:1]]; [[Ps. 23:3]]). The Hebrew of “restoreth my soul” is the verb and the noun of “converting the soul” in Psalm 19:7, in a different stem of the same verb[@oshb-wlc], and both older commentaries cross-reference the two verses: Delitzsch at Psalm 19, the Cambridge Bible in both directions, “restoring the soul… (cp. Psalm 23:3)” and “He restoreth my soul… Cp. Psalm 19:7, note.”[@delitzsch-psalms][@cambridge-psalms] The Greek translators used one verb for both.[@lxx-rahlfs] Six verses in the Psalms have the two words together, and in these two the subject is what matters: in Psalm 19 the law restores; in Psalm 23 the shepherd does.[@oshb-wlc]

A law can show the way, lead a man in it, and even bring a soul back to itself, as Psalm 19 says it does. What it cannot do is go out after a sheep. That is the office of a shepherd, and Psalm 119:176 asks for a shepherd's act. The law-lover's last prayer is not for more law; he has that, and says so. It is for the one of whom Psalm 23 speaks, the LORD who restores and leads and is “with me” in the valley ([[Ps. 23:4]]). The reading that puts these three psalms together is mine. The commentaries join 19:7 to 23:3 as a matter of wording; what I am adding is that the lost sheep of 119:176 is asking for the shepherd of Psalm 23, and that the Psalter has placed the answer before the question.

## I will seek that which was lost

The shepherd has a name in the prophets, and he makes the psalmist's prayer his own promise. Ezekiel's chapter against the shepherds of Israel charges them that “neither have ye sought that which was lost” ([[Ezek. 34:4]]), and the LORD answers, “Behold, I, even I, will both search my sheep, and seek them out… I will seek that which was lost” ([[Ezek. 34:11]]; [[Ezek. 34:16]]). “Seek” and “lost” in verse 16 are *bāqaš* and *ʾābad*, the two words of Psalm 119:176[@oshb-wlc], and the Cambridge commentary reads the psalmist's plea as an appeal to this promise.[@cambridge-psalms] The psalmist said, seek thy servant, a lost sheep; the LORD says, I will seek the lost. He says more in the same verse: “and bring again that which was driven away,” where “bring again” is the verb of “converting the soul” in Psalm 19:7, in the same causative form.[@oshb-wlc] The shepherd does in so many words what the law was praised for doing. The site's page on [Ezekiel 34](/ezekiel/34/) and the essay [One Shepherd](/ezekiel/themes/one-shepherd/) follow the promise to “one shepherd… my servant David” ([[Ezek. 34:23]]). The Church's footnote at Ezekiel's “lost” points to Luke 15:4 and Luke 19:10.[@lds-scriptures]

“For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost” ([[Luke 19:10]]). In Greek that is *zētēsai… to apolōlos*.[@greek-nt] The Greek Old Testament has the same two words in Ezekiel's promise, *to apolōlos zētēsō*, I will seek the lost,[@lxx-rahlfs] and in the psalmist's prayer, *probaton apolōlos; zētēson ton doulon sou*, a lost sheep; seek thy servant.[@lxx-rahlfs] So the Savior's statement of why He came carries the Greek of Ezekiel's promise, and the promise was made in the words of the psalm. The Church's footnotes join Luke 19:10 to Ezekiel, not to the psalm; the last step, to Psalm 119, is mine, and the words are on the page. The parable says the same in a story, of a man with a hundred sheep who will “go after that which is lost, until he find it” ([[Luke 15:4]]). Jesus also says, “My sheep hear my voice… and they shall never perish” ([[John 10:27–28]]), where “perish” is the root of “lost” and of the Greek of Psalm 1:6.[@greek-nt] Isaiah had said of all of us, with the verb of Psalm 119:176, “All we like sheep have gone astray… and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all” ([[Isa. 53:6]])[@oshb-wlc], and the Topical Guide's entry on sheep lists Psalm 119:176 beside that verse.[@tg-sheep]

Here I should say what the Church's teachers do and do not say. The parable's sheep has mostly been read among Latter-day Saints as the inactive member or the sinner, a call to go and find them; Jenny Rytting's study of the three parables documents that tradition and recovers an older one, in which Christ is the shepherd and all of us are the sheep, a reading she finds “accords with Church teachings about the universality of Christ's Atonement.”[@rytting-lost-sheep] President Uchtdorf gave that reading from the pulpit: “Is it possible that the Savior's message was that God is fully aware of those who are lost—and that He will find them, that He will reach out to them, and that He will rescue them?” And: “It matters not how you became lost—whether because of your own poor choices or because of circumstances beyond your control.”[@uchtdorf-shoulders] The parable itself seems to say otherwise: the ninety and nine are “just persons, which need no repentance” ([[Luke 15:7]]), and on that reading the law-lover belongs with them, not with the one. Rytting's answer is the older reading's: it “erases the false distinction among those who are accountable, between those who are ‘righteous’ and those who are ‘sinners,’” for “all are in need of being found by the Shepherd.”[@rytting-lost-sheep] No Latter-day Saint source I have found comments on Psalm 119:176 itself, and none says in so many words that a disciple keeping the commandments can be the lost sheep. The psalm says it, and President Uchtdorf's sentence and Rytting's leave room for it. I claim no more than that.

## He that gave the law

There remains the question Psalm 119 raises by its very existence: what has the law to do with the shepherd? The psalmist loved the law and asked for a shepherd. Scripture's answer is that the giver of the law and the shepherd are one Lord. The site's note on [Psalm 23:1](/psalms/23/) shows that the shepherd of the Psalms is Jehovah by name and that Jesus claimed the title. And the risen Jesus, speaking to the Nephites of the law the psalmist loved, said: “Behold, I am he that gave the law, and I am he who covenanted with my people Israel; therefore, the law in me is fulfilled” ([[3 Ne. 15:5]]). Avram and Thora Shannon read the sentence as “reminding the Nephites that as the giver of the law, it is his law, and not really the law of Moses at all but rather the law of Jehovah,”[@shannon-i-am-law] and S. Kent Brown that “Moses was chiefly the transmitter of the Law… Jesus on the other hand was its giver, its author.”[@brown-old-adorns-new] In the same discourse He says, “I am the law, and the light” ([[3 Ne. 15:9]]), and then, “there shall be one fold, and one shepherd… ye are my sheep” ([[3 Ne. 15:21]]; [[3 Ne. 15:24]]). The law in 3 Nephi 15 is the law of Moses, which “hath an end” in Him; the psalmist's *torah*, his word for the law, is wider than a code, as the site's note on [Psalm 119](/psalms/119/) explains. But the giver is the same, and that is the point. The sources I have read name Him the giver of the law; none joins that title to the shepherd's. I say it, on the strength of the texts: the LORD whose law the psalmist loved for 176 verses is the LORD he asked to seek him, and in 3 Nephi 15 that LORD stands in one place and says both things of Himself.

So the psalm's last verse is not a lapse at the end of a long devotion, as if the law had failed him. Psalm 19 praised the law and then prayed, and its last word is “my redeemer” ([[Ps. 19:14]]); Psalm 119 does the same at length, and its last word is a plea to be sought. On my reading both psalms end by turning from the law to the one who gave it, and He is the shepherd.

## The disciple who has not forgotten

What this gives a disciple is a prayer, and a permission to pray it. The disciple who keeps the commandments and still finds himself lost, in sin he cannot see, or in a danger he did not choose, has a verse in scripture that says exactly that and does not apologize for it. He may say both halves. “I have gone astray like a lost sheep; seek thy servant,” and, in the same breath, “for I do not forget thy commandments.” This year's *Come, Follow Me* asks readers of Psalm 119 to watch for words like “walk,” “path,” and “wander,” and says its phrases “compare our lives to a journey back to Heavenly Father.”[@cfm-ot-2026-36] The journey's last verse is a sheep being sought.

It gives, second, the ground of the prayer. The shepherd's seeking rests on His covenant love. President Nelson, on the covenant love that *Scripture Helps* prints beside the Psalms: “Because God has *hesed* for those who have covenanted with Him, He will love them… And should they stray, He will help them find their way back to Him.”[@nelson-everlasting-covenant] “Seek thy servant” is the asking that, in President Uchtdorf's words, “is how we show Him that we want to be rescued”;[@uchtdorf-shoulders] “I do not forget thy commandments” is the covenant kept. President Holland, on the shepherd's staff of Psalm 23, calls it “the great scriptural instrument of rescue and redemption,” and says of us: “Whether it be in threatening confrontations or routine wandering, we are blessed and protected by God's vigilant care.”[@scripture-helps-ps-1]

And it gives the end of the matter. Psalm 1 said the way of the ungodly shall perish, and the law-lover feared he was perishing. The shepherd who came to seek the lost said of His sheep, “they shall never perish” ([[John 10:28]]). The one who gave the law is the one who seeks the sheep, and the sheep who has not forgotten His commandments has not been forgotten by Him. That is why Psalm 119 can end where it does. Its last words are not “lost sheep.” They are “I do not forget thy commandments,” said by a sheep that has asked its shepherd to come.
