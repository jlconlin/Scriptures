Psalm 49 asks why the singer should fear those “that trust in their wealth, and boast themselves in the multitude of their riches” (49:6), and answers with a price: “None of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him: (For the redemption of their soul is precious, and it ceaseth for ever:) That he should still live for ever, and not see corruption” (49:7–9). Then he speaks of himself: “But God will redeem my soul from the power of the grave: for he shall receive me” (49:15).

The two words of verse 7 come from the law. The commentators hear in them Exodus 21:30, the ransom for a life forfeited when an ox known to gore has killed.[@cambridge-psalms][@delitzsch-psalms] I hear more of the law than that one case. “Redeem” is the law’s verb for buying back a firstborn son at a set price, and “ransom” is its word for the half shekel every man gave “for his soul unto the LORD” when Israel was numbered.[@bdb] The psalm takes the prices the law laid on human lives, says no man can pay them to escape death, and gives the same verb to God. The Church reads the psalm by that contrast: “Men cannot be ransomed or redeemed by wealth—God alone can redeem a soul from the grave.”[@lds-scriptures]

What the psalm does not say is what God’s redemption costs. Peter says it: “ye were not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold … But with the precious blood of Christ” (1 Pet. 1:18–19). Read against the law, the psalm retains the sense of a price paid for a life. The law set that price in shekels; no man could pay it to escape death. The New Testament names the price, and the Redeemer paid it.[@curtis-redemption]

<h2 id="in-the-grave">“In the grave who shall give thee thanks?”</h2>

Most of what the psalms say about death is said in fear. “For in death there is no remembrance of thee: in the grave who shall give thee thanks?” (6:5). “The dead praise not the LORD, neither any that go down into silence” (115:17). “The grave” in 6:5 is *sheol*, “the abode of departed spirits.”[@oshb-wlc][@bd-sheol] All people were understood to go there, and it “was generally viewed as inescapable.”[@scripture-helps-ps-1] (See the note on [Psalm 6:5](/psalms/6/), and, for Psalm 88, [The Unanswered Prayer](/psalms/themes/the-unanswered-prayer/).)

Thanks for rescue from Sheol is thanks for life: “thou hast kept me alive” (30:3). Another psalm asks the question Psalm 49 takes up: “What man is he that liveth, and shall not see death? shall he deliver his soul from the hand of the grave?” (89:48). The Church’s footnote at 49:9 sends the reader to that verse,[@lds-scriptures] and 49:15 answers it in its words: “the power of the grave” is, in Hebrew, “the hand of Sheol.”[@oshb-wlc]

<h2 id="a-ransom-for-him">“Nor give to God a ransom for him”</h2>

The rich make “a god of their wealth,” Cambridge says. “But how powerless it is! It cannot deliver anyone from death.”[@cambridge-psalms] The words for their money are the law’s. An owner whose ox was known to gore forfeited his life when it killed, and the law let him pay instead:[@cambridge-psalms] “If there be laid on him a sum of money, then he shall give for the ransom of his life whatsoever is laid upon him” (Ex. 21:30). The Hebrew has *kōper*, “the price of a life, ransom,” and beside it *pidyôn napšô*, “the redemption of his soul”; Psalm 49:8 has *pidyôn napšām*, “the redemption of their soul.”[@bdb][@oshb-wlc] The psalm’s language “is borrowed from the ancient law in Exodus 21:30, where the words ransom and redemption of life (or soul) occur together.”[@cambridge-psalms] *Kōper* stands nowhere else in the Psalms.[@oshb-wlc]

The law used both words more widely. The verb *pādâ*, “redeem,” means first of all redemption “for an assessed price,” and the lexicon’s verses for that sense are the law of the firstborn.[@bdb] Every firstborn son belonged to the LORD (“it is mine,” Ex. 13:2) and was bought back: “all the firstborn of man among thy children shalt thou redeem” (13:13; again 34:20), “for the money of five shekels” (Num. 18:16). Exodus’s [The Firstborn](/exodus/themes/the-firstborn/) follows that law.

*Kōper* had a second use, and it fell on every man. “When thou takest the sum of the children of Israel after their number, then shall they give every man a ransom for his soul unto the LORD, when thou numberest them; that there be no plague among them” (Ex. 30:12). It was half a shekel for each man counted,[@bdb] and “the rich shall not give more, and the poor shall not give less” (30:15). (The note on [Exodus 30:15](/exodus/30/) takes up the flat rate.) Numbers’s [Between the Dead and the Living](/numbers/themes/between-the-dead-and-the-living/) follows the one life the law would not let be ransomed, the murderer’s (Num. 35:31).

I read verse 7 against two fixed prices the law set on an Israelite life: at a firstborn son’s birth and at a man’s numbering. Two of its phrases are the law’s own. “Can by any means redeem” is *pādōh yipdeh*, the verb doubled for emphasis, and the lexicon lists this form of the verb (the Qal infinitive) in only one other verse, the law of the firstborn: “the firstborn of man shalt thou surely redeem,” *pādōh tipdeh* (Num. 18:15).[@bdb][@oshb-wlc] And “nor give to God a ransom for him” has the words of the census law, where “they give every man a ransom for his soul unto the LORD” (Ex. 30:12). Both have “give,” “man,” and *kōper*, and in both the ransom is given to God, where at Exodus 21:30 it went to the dead man’s kin.[@oshb-wlc][@cambridge-psalms]

Read so, the verse turns the law’s price around. In the law the price was small and every man could pay it, five shekels for a son and half a shekel for himself. It bought a life the LORD had claimed, or a life kept from plague. The psalm asks the price of something else, “That he should still live for ever, and not see corruption” (49:9), and the law’s “surely redeem” becomes “none … can by any means redeem.” “The ransom of their life is costly and can never suffice.”[@esv] No man has it, for his brother or for himself. (“Corruption” there is *šaḥat*, “pit,” the “pit of Sheol”;[@bdb] a later section returns to it.)

<h2 id="death-their-shepherd">“Death shall feed on them”</h2>

The first half of the psalm ends on a refrain: “man being in honour abideth not: he is like the beasts that perish” (49:12). The second follows the rich past death: “Like sheep they are laid in the grave; death shall feed on them” (49:14). The verb is the herdsman’s, “pasture, tend,” the verb behind “my shepherd” in Psalm 23:1, and a modern translation gives the line as “death shall be their shepherd.”[@bdb][@oshb-wlc][@esv] Sheol is their fold.[@delitzsch-psalms]

Keil and Delitzsch set this shepherd against the one of Psalm 23, “whose pasture is the land of the living”[@delitzsch-psalms] (see the notes on [Psalm 49:14](/psalms/49/) and [Psalm 23](/psalms/23/)). What the rich trusted could not buy them out of Death’s flock. The second refrain changes one word, “Man that is in honour, and understandeth not” (49:20), and Keil and Delitzsch take it to mean that the gloomy prospect belongs “not to the rich man as such, but to the worldly and carnally minded rich man.”[@delitzsch-psalms] The psalm has already named them: those “that trust in their wealth” (49:6).

<h2 id="but-god">“But God will redeem my soul”</h2>

Then the psalm turns. “But God will redeem my soul from the power of the grave: for he shall receive me” (49:15). “Redeem” is the verb of verse 7 in the same form, *yipdeh*. There “a man” cannot; here “God” will.[@oshb-wlc] The lexicon files the two verses under different senses: verse 7 under redemption “from violence and death,” verse 15 under “God subject,” which it glosses “underlying thought of payment.”[@bdb] The psalm uses one word for both. (The same lexicon files the *kōper* of verse 7 with Exodus 21:30, under the price of a life, and lists the census ransom apart;[@bdb] my connection with the census differs from its grouping.) The verb the law used for an assessed price, and that the psalm denied to every man at death, is given to God with its sense of payment still in it.

The contrast between the two verses is the commentators’ and the Church’s. “The verse corresponds to Psalm 49:7-8,” Cambridge says. “While wealth is powerless to avert death, God can and will deliver His servant.”[@cambridge-psalms] The righteous are redeemed “not by themselves, but by God.”[@pulpit-psalms] *Come, Follow Me* (2026) heads its section on the psalm “Redemption comes only through Jesus Christ.”[@cfm-ot-2026-35]

How far the singer saw is read in more than one way. Cambridge holds that he looks for deliverance “from the premature and penal death of the wicked,” not for resurrection, and yet that “his words contain the germ and principle of the doctrine of the Resurrection.”[@cambridge-psalms] Ellicott sees “the dim foreshadowing of a better hope,”[@ellicott-psalms] the Pulpit Commentary a hope that the righteous will “enter upon a higher life,” held “not … as a revealed doctrine, but as a presentiment,”[@pulpit-psalms] and Keil and Delitzsch “a ‘bold flight’ of faith.”[@delitzsch-psalms] The Church reads the verse of Christ. Elder LeGrand R. Curtis Jr. taught in general conference that the redemption it promises is the Atonement of Jesus Christ,[@curtis-redemption] and the footnote at “receive” points to the Topical Guide’s “Eternal Life.”[@lds-scriptures]

In the King James rendering of Hosea, the Lord promises in His own voice, with the same verb, which the King James renders “ransom” there, and the same “hand of Sheol”: “I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death” (Hos. 13:14).[@oshb-wlc] The Church’s footnotes join that verse and Psalm 49:15 in both directions.[@lds-scriptures] Whether Hosea’s lines are a promise or a question is taken up at [Hosea 13:14](/hosea/13/).

Psalm 16 says something close of the faithful one: “For thou wilt not leave my soul in hell; neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption” (16:10). Its “corruption” is the *šaḥat* of 49:9, and the lexicon lists the two verses together under “pit of Sheol.”[@bdb][@oshb-wlc] I read the two verses together; they meet at the pit: 49:9 names what no man’s ransom can buy, not to “see corruption,” and 16:10 says God will not let His Holy One see it. Neither says how.

<h2 id="not-redeemed">“Not redeemed with corruptible things”</h2>

The apostles read Psalm 16:10 of the Resurrection, because it had not come true of David. David “is both dead and buried,” Peter said, so “he seeing this before spake of the resurrection of Christ, that his soul was not left in hell, neither his flesh did see corruption. This Jesus hath God raised up” (Acts 2:29, 31–32). Paul: David “saw corruption: But he, whom God raised again, saw no corruption” (13:36–37). The Savior did not stay in the world of spirits; God raised Him.[@scripture-helps-ps-1] (See the note on [Psalm 16:10](/psalms/16/).)

They read the psalms in Greek. The Greek of 16:10 has *diaphthora*, “decay,” the word Peter quotes,[@lxx-rahlfs][@greek-nt] and the Greek of 49:9 has *kataphthora*, from the same root; both are “corruption” in English, and the Hebrew in both is the pit.[@bdb] I trace 49:9 through 16:10 and the Greek wording to the empty tomb: what Psalm 49 says no ransom can buy, God gave His Holy One.

Peter supplies what Psalm 49 left out, the price. “Ye were not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold, from your vain conversation received by tradition from your fathers; But with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot” (1 Pet. 1:18–19). Elder Curtis set these words beside Psalm 49:15, and said that “unlike the redemptions under the law of Moses or in modern legal arrangements,” this one does not come by silver and gold.[@curtis-redemption] I set them against verses 6–8. Peter’s verb is the Greek *lytroō*,[@greek-nt] and in the Greek Psalter the apostles read it is the psalm’s verb as well: at 49:7 no man “redeems” (*lytrōsetai*) his brother, and at 49:15 God “shall redeem” (*lytrōsetai*) the singer’s soul.[@lxx-rahlfs] Peter answers the psalm in its own Greek word. The psalm says those who trust in “the multitude of their riches” cannot redeem. Peter says what the redeemed were not bought with, and what they were. “He redeemed us with His blood.”[@bd-redemption]

The Savior said He came “to give his life a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45). His word is the Greek *lytron*, “a redemption price,”[@strongs-greek] the noun of the verb the Greek Psalter uses for “redeem” at 49:7 and 49:15; the psalm’s own “ransom” is a different Greek word there,[@lxx-rahlfs] and the Church’s footnotes do not join the two verses.[@lds-scriptures] The New Testament Student Manual calls His suffering, death, and Resurrection “the price He paid to redeem all mankind.”[@nt-manual-mark-8-10] [Between the Dead and the Living](/numbers/themes/between-the-dead-and-the-living/) follows that word, and Paul’s “ransom for all” (1 Tim. 2:6), back to the Hebrew *kōper*.

One more verse answers the picture of Death’s flock. “Now the God of peace, that brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus, that great shepherd of the sheep, through the blood of the everlasting covenant” (Heb. 13:20). Hebrews does not cite the psalm, and no source I have read sets the two together. I read the one against the other. In Psalm 49 the flock is driven into Sheol with Death for its shepherd. In Hebrews the Shepherd was Himself among the dead and was brought again from them, “through the blood.”

Read whole, the arc runs from the law’s price to the Redeemer’s. Jesus Christ is the great Redeemer because He “paid the price for the sins of mankind and made possible the resurrection of all people.”[@gs-redeemer] And the question of Psalm 6, “in the grave who shall give thee thanks?”, has its answer in latter-day revelation: in the world of spirits “the saints rejoiced in their redemption, and bowed the knee and acknowledged the Son of God as their Redeemer and Deliverer from death and the chains of hell” (D&C 138:23).

<h2 id="be-not-afraid">“Be not thou afraid when one is made rich”</h2>

The psalm ends where it began, with fear, and turns it into counsel: “Be not thou afraid when one is made rich, when the glory of his house is increased; For when he dieth he shall carry nothing away” (49:16–17). Fear of the powerful can rest on the rich men’s mistake: that wealth or standing can buy what only God gives; *Come, Follow Me* names the temptation to “trust something other than God for redemption.”[@cfm-ot-2026-35]

The price also says what a soul is worth. The Church’s footnote at “soul” in verse 8 points to the Topical Guide’s “Worth of Souls,”[@lds-scriptures] and Elder Jack H. Goaslind Jr. joined the verse to that worth: to the Father “the worth of souls is great,” and “the redemption of their soul is precious,” and therefore “God sent his Son, the Savior and Redeemer, to loose the bands of death and atone for the sins of carnal, fallen men.”[@goaslind-ends-earth] No one can pay for his own soul or his brother’s; the Father gave His Son to pay for both.

“Temporal death is overcome, and all will be resurrected,” Elder Curtis taught, and Christ “paid for the sins of all mankind on condition of individual repentance.”[@curtis-redemption] Those “who have faith in Him and who repent are also redeemed from spiritual death.”[@gs-redeem] A disciple who stands at a grave, his own or a brother’s, does not stand where the psalm’s rich men stood, with a price he cannot pay. The ransom no man could give has been given. It brings every soul up from the grave, and it redeems from spiritual death those who come to Him by faith and repentance; nothing they bring could pay it.
