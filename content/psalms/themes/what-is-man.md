The psalms ask what a human being is and give two answers: glory and dominion, and a shadow and dust. Hebrews then takes the first answer and says where it can be seen. The psalms are read here in the order 8, 144, 90, 103, which is not their order in the Psalter; the order is this page’s own.

## The question, and the first answer

“When I consider thy heavens, the work of thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which thou hast ordained; What is man, that thou art mindful of him? and the son of man, that thou visitest him?” ([[Ps. 8:3–4]]). The answer: “For thou hast made him a little lower than the angels, and hast crowned him with glory and honour… thou hast put all things under his feet” ([[Ps. 8:5–6]]). In Hebrew he is made “less than the gods”[@lds-scriptures]: the word translated “angels” is Elohim,[@scripture-helps-ps-1] and “angels” first appeared in the Septuagint, the Greek version of the Psalms[@sperry-psalm-8] (see [Psalm 8](/psalms/8/)). “As children of God, we have the potential to become like Him”;[@scripture-helps-ps-1] “each has a divine nature and destiny.”[@family-proclamation] Set beside “God said, Let us make man in our image… and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea” ([[Gen. 1:26]]), verse 6 is “God’s own declaration in the Creation.”[@ot-minute-psalms-1-20]

## The question again

“LORD, what is man, that thou takest knowledge of him! or the son of man, that thou makest account of him! Man is like to vanity: his days are as a shadow that passeth away” ([[Ps. 144:3–4]]). Its Hebrew uses Psalm 8:4’s two words for man in reverse order[@oshb-wlc], and Psalm 144:3 has been read as “a variation of Psalm 8:5” [KJV 8:4][@delitzsch-psalms] (see [Psalm 144](/psalms/144/)). Job asks the same question as a complaint ([[Job 7:17]]).

“Thou turnest man to destruction; and sayest, Return, ye children of men” ([[Ps. 90:3]]); in Hebrew, “returnest man to dust.”[@lds-scriptures] The same psalm asks, “So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom” ([[Ps. 90:12]]; see [Psalm 90](/psalms/90/)). Psalm 103 sets the same dust beside a Father’s pity: “Like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him. For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust” ([[Ps. 103:13–14]]). “As for man, his days are as grass” ([[Ps. 103:15]]), “But the mercy of the LORD is from everlasting to everlasting upon them that fear him” ([[Ps. 103:17]]; see [Psalm 103](/psalms/103/)).

Moses’ “man is nothing” ([[Moses 1:10]]) stands beside God’s work and glory “to save and exalt mankind”: “compared to God, man is nothing; yet we are everything to God.”[@uchtdorf-you-matter-2011] That pairing is made without reference to the psalms.

## “We see Jesus”

Hebrews quotes the question: “But one in a certain place testified, saying, What is man, that thou art mindful of him?” ([[Heb. 2:6]]). It goes on: “But now we see not yet all things put under him. But we see Jesus, who was made a little lower than the angels for the suffering of death, crowned with glory and honour” ([[Heb. 2:8–9]]).

Hebrews takes Psalm 8:4–6 as “a messianic prophecy”: “Christ condescended from His premortal throne and came to earth so that He could experience mortality with its physical restrictions.”[@nt-manual-hebrews-1-6] Hebrews reads the psalm of Jesus as the “son of man,” while the psalm’s own “more generic anthropological reading” applies to the audience, “the ‘many sons and daughters’ of Hebrews 2:10.”[@bowen-brethren-hebrews-2] The first is what Hebrews says of Jesus; the second is what the psalm says of humankind.

## Many sons unto glory

“But now we see not yet all things put under him” ([[Heb. 2:8]]). Paul’s words: “he must reign, till he hath put all enemies under his feet. The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death” ([[1 Cor. 15:25–26]]). For the other New Testament uses of Psalm 8:6, see [Psalm 8](/psalms/8/).

Then Hebrews turns to those whom, in that reading, the psalm described: “in bringing many sons unto glory, to make the captain of their salvation perfect through sufferings… he is not ashamed to call them brethren” ([[Heb. 2:10–11]]). The epistle’s message includes Jesus’ “solidarity with humankind that grows out of his sibling relationship with them.”[@bowen-brethren-hebrews-2]
