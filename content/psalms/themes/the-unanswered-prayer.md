The psalms are not shy about asking God where He is. “How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me?” ([[Ps. 13:1]]). “Wherefore hidest thou thy face, and forgettest our affliction?” ([[Ps. 44:24]]). “Hide not thy face from me in the day when I am in trouble” ([[Ps. 102:2]]). The Hebrew behind every one of these is the same phrase, a verb meaning to hide with the noun for face[@oshb-wlc], and the lexicon files them together under what it means for God to do this: He “withdraws his favour.”[@bdb] A reader who prays the psalms learns the question quickly. The harder thing to learn is what the book does with it.

Most of these laments turn. Psalm 13 asks “how long” four times and then, with no word from heaven recorded in between, says, “But I have trusted in thy mercy” ([[Ps. 13:5]]). Rosalynde Welch, following the scholars who first sorted the psalms by kind, puts the puzzle well: the laments move from distress to petition to praise, and “the transitions between these phases usually happen with no explanation… But the rescue itself is unseen.”[@welch-psalm-102] Not every lament reaches praise; Psalm 44 ends in a plea, “redeem us for thy mercies' sake” ([[Ps. 44:26]]). But one lament does not even reach that. Psalm 88 asks Psalm 13’s question about God’s hidden face in the same Hebrew words, “why” instead of “how long”: “why hidest thou thy face from me?” ([[Ps. 88:14]]). Four verses later it ends: “Lover and friend hast thou put far from me, and mine acquaintance into darkness” ([[Ps. 88:18]]). Nothing follows.

And one lament was prayed from a cross. “About the ninth hour Jesus cried with a loud voice, saying, Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani? that is to say, My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?” ([[Matt. 27:46]]). That is the first line of Psalm 22, a psalm that begins where Psalm 88 begins, crying by day and by night to a God who does not answer, and then turns, and says of its sufferer the one thing Psalm 88 could not say: “neither hath he hid his face from him; but when he cried unto him, he heard” ([[Ps. 22:24]]).

My argument is that these three facts belong together. The hidden face is the laments' question. Psalm 88 shows that the question can go unanswered to the last word. Psalm 22 shows the question answered, in the very words of the question, for the one who prayed it. And the Gospels put Psalm 22 in the mouth of Jesus Christ. So the one who entered the unanswered prayer is the one in whom it was answered, and Psalm 22 goes on to say what its sufferer did with the answer: he declared God's name “unto my brethren.” Hebrews gives that line to Christ. One Latter-day Saint reading, through the revelation on the redemption of the dead, hears Him speak it to the faithful dead, the very company Psalm 88’s sufferer believed God could not reach. Psalm 88 stays dark on its own page. What I am claiming is that the Gospels, Hebrews, and that revelation tell us who went into that dark, and came out, and for whom.

## How long wilt thou hide thy face

Begin with the question itself. “Hide thy face” is not a figure the psalmists invented. It is the Lord's own threat in the law: “I will hide my face from them,” He says in Deuteronomy, of a day when Israel turns to other gods ([[Deut. 31:17–18]]), and in most of the places the lexicon lists outside the Psalms the cause is named, and the cause is sin.[@bdb] The one exception that sounds like the laments is Job, a man who denies the charge: “Wherefore hidest thou thy face, and holdest me for thine enemy?” ([[Job 13:24]]). The laments stand with Job. Psalm 13 names no sin. Psalm 88 names no sin, and no enemy either; the site's notes on [Psalm 88](/psalms/88/) say so. The Church's edition, for its part, sends the reader from Psalm 88:14 to David's sin with Bathsheba and his census ([[2 Sam. 11:27]]; [[1 Chr. 21:8]]),[@lds-scriptures] so one may read the psalm as a sinner's prayer; but the psalm itself confesses nothing. On my reading, the psalmists have taken the Lord's word for judgment and used it for what His silence feels like from the inside.

The Church's edition marks the question in Psalm 13 in an unusual way. At “hide” in 13:1 its footnote points not to another psalm but to Liberty Jail: “O God, where art thou? And where is the pavilion that covereth thy hiding place?” ([[D&C 121:1]]).[@lds-scriptures] Hold that footnote; it will be joined by another.

The laments that ask the question mostly turn. Psalm 13 turns at “But I” ([[Ps. 13:5]]), and the site's note on that verse points out that the turn is a statement about the speaker, not a report of anything God has done. The longer laments often turn on a word: in Psalm 22 it is the last word of verse 21, “thou hast heard me,” the same verb that was negated in verse 2, “thou hearest not.”[@oshb-wlc] Delitzsch calls it a perfect of confidence: “in the very midst of his supplication there springs up in the mind of the suppliant the assurance of being heard and answered.”[@delitzsch-psalms] Whatever happens at the turn, the psalmist does not tell us. He simply begins to praise.

## The psalm that does not turn

Psalm 88 is the lament that never gets there, and the Church's heading says what kind of psalm it is: “A prayer of one who feels forsaken and who asks whether the Lord's loving kindness will be declared in the grave.”[@lds-scriptures] Delitzsch calls it “the darkest, gloomiest, of all the plaintive Psalms,” in which “the gloom of melancholy does not brighten up to become a hope, the Psalm dies away in Job-like lamentation.”[@delitzsch-psalms] The Cambridge commentary agrees: “In other Psalms the light breaks through the clouds at last: here the gloom is deepest at the close. It is characteristic that the last word is darkness.”[@cambridge-psalms]

Two things about the psalm matter for what follows. The first is where its sufferer thinks he is. “My life draweth nigh unto the grave,” he says, using the name Sheol; “I am counted with them that go down into the pit… Thou hast laid me in the lowest pit, in darkness, in the deeps” ([[Ps. 88:3–6]]). He is not dead, but he reckons himself among the dead, “free among the dead, like the slain that lie in the grave, whom thou rememberest no more” ([[Ps. 88:5]]). And his argument with God is built on that reckoning. “Wilt thou shew wonders to the dead? shall the dead arise and praise thee?… Shall thy lovingkindness be declared in the grave? or thy faithfulness in destruction? Shall thy wonders be known in the dark? and thy righteousness in the land of forgetfulness?” ([[Ps. 88:10–12]]). Six questions, and he expects the answer no to all of them. That is why he needs rescue now: he believes a dead man cannot praise. Delitzsch, reading these verses, says plainly that this was how the Old Testament pictured what lies beyond, “uniform darkness,” and adds that “in the New Testament not merely the conception of the state after death, but this state itself, is become a different one.”[@delitzsch-psalms] Keep that sentence too.

The second thing is that he does not stop praying. “But unto thee have I cried, O LORD; and in the morning shall my prayer prevent thee” ([[Ps. 88:13]]). The verb for “cried” here is *šāwaʿ*, a cry for help[@oshb-wlc], and where a psalmist uses it of his own cry and says what followed, he was heard: “cried unto my God: he heard my voice” ([[Ps. 18:6]]); “I cried unto thee, and thou hast healed me” ([[Ps. 30:2]]). The one cry the Psalter says went unanswered is the enemies' ([[Ps. 18:41]]). Psalm 88 reports no outcome at all. He cries, and the next verse is “LORD, why castest thou off my soul? why hidest thou thy face from me?” ([[Ps. 88:14]]), and then the dark. Delitzsch writes that “with this complaint the harp falls from the poet's hands. He is silent, and waits on God, that He may solve this riddle of affliction,” and then, from outside the poem, infers an answer: “From the Book of Job we might infer that He also actually appeared to him.”[@delitzsch-psalms] That is the move this essay will make too, but with more than an inference to go on. Within the psalm, whatever God did, the psalm does not say.

I want to be careful here. No Latter-day Saint source I have read takes Psalm 88 of the Savior, and the Church's edition gives it no messianic heading, so this page does not claim it is about Him. Some of the early Christian fathers read it so, as the Cambridge commentary notes, which is why it became a Good Friday psalm.[@cambridge-psalms] I leave it where the edition leaves it: a man in the dark who confesses nothing, left there by the book. That is its value. It is the psalm for the prayer that is not answered.

## Prayed from the cross

Now Psalm 22. Its opening is Psalm 88's opening. Psalm 88 begins, “O LORD God of my salvation, I have cried day and night before thee” ([[Ps. 88:1]]); Psalm 22 begins, “My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?… O my God, I cry in the daytime, but thou hearest not; and in the night season, and am not silent” ([[Ps. 22:1–2]]). The two use different Hebrew verbs for crying, but the address and the day-and-night pairing are shared[@oshb-wlc], and so is the situation: a man praying around the clock to a God who is not answering. Through eighteen verses the sufferer stays there, surrounded, mocked, poured out like water, his body giving out. The site's notes on [Psalm 22](/psalms/22/) follow it verse by verse.

Jesus prayed its first line. Matthew and Mark both record it, in Aramaic and in translation, as the cry of the ninth hour ([[Matt. 27:46]]; [[Mark 15:34]]). The Church's heading on the psalm calls it “a messianic psalm of David” in which “the Messiah will say, My God, my God, why hast Thou forsaken me?”[@lds-scriptures], and its footnotes at Matthew's cry point two ways: at “My” to Psalm 22:1, and at “forsaken” to Liberty Jail and “O God, where art thou?”, as the footnote at Psalm 13:1 does.[@lds-scriptures] So the edition we read in has joined the two questions, the hidden face of Psalm 13 and the cry from the cross, to one latter-day prayer, and D&C 121's own footnote sends the reader back to Psalm 13.[@lds-scriptures] The connection among the three is the Church's; what I am adding is Psalm 88, which asks Psalm 13's question and gets no answer.

What the cry meant on the Savior's lips is a question the Church's leaders have answered carefully, and the note “Did the Father forsake Him?” on [Psalm 22](/psalms/22/) gathers their words. Elder Holland has testified both “that a perfect Father did not forsake His Son in that hour” and that “the Father briefly withdrew from Jesus the comfort of His Spirit, the support of His personal presence,” so that He “had to feel what it was like to die not only physically but spiritually.”[@holland-none-with-him] The New Testament Student Manual says the cry “was prefigured centuries earlier in the 22nd Psalm,” and that though the Savior had never sinned, “He experienced that separation on our behalf during the Atonement.”[@nt-student-manual-10] For this essay the point is simpler than the theology. Whatever the Father did or did not withdraw, the Son prayed the lament's question in the lament's words. He did not pray above it. He entered it.

Shon Hopkin, whose study of Psalm 22 is the Latter-day Saint treatment of the psalm, draws out what that entering did. On one side, “that cry showed that Christ had truly ‘descended below all things’ (D&C 88:6), so that he could understand and succor his people (Alma 7:12).”[@hopkin-psalm-22] On the other, because the cry is the first line of a whole psalm, Christ “was still lovingly teaching his people by communicating to them the many concepts contained in Psalm 22—including his final victory over suffering—while uttering only one short phrase.”[@hopkin-psalm-22] On that reading the first line carries the rest of the lament with it. And the rest of this lament turns.

## Neither hath he hid his face

Here is the turn, and then the verse the whole essay rests on:

> Save me from the lion's mouth: for thou hast heard me from the horns of the unicorns. I will declare thy name unto my brethren: in the midst of the congregation will I praise thee… For he hath not despised nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted; neither hath he hid his face from him; but when he cried unto him, he heard. ([[Ps. 22:21–22]]; [[Ps. 22:24]])

Read verse 24 beside the two laments. “Hid his face” is the phrase of Psalm 13:1 and Psalm 88:14, the same verb in the same stem with the same noun, and here it is negated.[@oshb-wlc] “When he cried” is *šāwaʿ*, the cry of Psalm 88:13 that got no reported answer, and here it is followed by the word Psalm 88 never reached: “he heard.”[@oshb-wlc] Delitzsch says of the verse that “the sufferer felt the wrath of God, but this has changed into a love that sends help; God did not long keep His countenance hidden, He hearkened to him.”[@delitzsch-psalms] Hopkin reads verses 23 to 25 as spoken after the trial, “declaring that God has already ‘heard’ and responded to the cry of the sufferer,” and, read of Christ, as lines that “can only refer to events subsequent to Christ's sacrifice and death on the cross.”[@hopkin-psalm-22]

The Psalter knows of hidden faces that ended. Psalm 30 says, “thou didst hide thy face, and I was troubled,” and had already said, “I cried unto thee, and thou hast healed me” ([[Ps. 30:7]]; [[Ps. 30:2]]). Psalm 31, whose fifth verse Jesus also prayed from the cross ([[Luke 23:46]]), says, “I am cut off from before thine eyes: nevertheless thou heardest the voice of my supplications when I cried unto thee” ([[Ps. 31:22]]). But only Psalm 22 answers the hidden face in the words of the question, as a thing that did not happen: “neither hath he hid his face from him.” That is the connection I am making, and I should say plainly that no commentary I have read makes it; the lexicon groups 13:1, 22:24 and 88:14 under one heading without comment[@bdb], and Hopkin, who quotes 22:24, does not mention Psalm 13 or 88. But the words are there to be checked. The question “why hidest thou thy face from me?” and the answer “neither hath he hid his face from him” are built on the same two Hebrew words, and the cry that fell silent in Psalm 88 is the cry that was heard in the lament whose first line the Savior prayed.

Hebrews says something like it of the Savior's prayers in its own language. He “offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears unto him that was able to save him from death, and was heard in that he feared” ([[Heb. 5:7]]). Delitzsch, at Psalm 22:21, reaches for that verse himself: to be answered “from the horns of the antilope is equivalent to hearing and rescuing from them,” he writes, “cf. the equally pregnant expression… Psalm 118:5, perhaps also Hebrews 5:7.”[@delitzsch-psalms] The Deuteronomy essay on this site, *Let It Suffice Thee*, has already noticed the strangeness of Hebrews' word: the cup did not pass, and Hebrews calls Him heard. Psalm 22 lets us see what the hearing looked like from the far side of the cross.

## My brethren

The answered sufferer does not keep the answer. The first thing he says after the turn is a vow to tell it: “I will declare thy name unto my brethren: in the midst of the congregation will I praise thee” ([[Ps. 22:22]]). Hebrews gives that line to Christ. The Son and “they who are sanctified are all of one: for which cause he is not ashamed to call them brethren, saying, I will declare thy name unto my brethren, in the midst of the church will I sing praise unto thee” ([[Heb. 2:11–12]]). Hopkin notes that the author of Hebrews chose to quote “from the final section of the psalm,” to “emphasize the victorious nature of Jesus's Atonement.”[@hopkin-psalm-22]

Where is this congregation? Hopkin offers a reading that only Latter-day Saints can offer. The vow “beautifully reflects Christ's important witness immediately after his death when visiting those waiting for him in the spirit world,” and he quotes President Joseph F. Smith's vision: “there were gathered together in one place an innumerable company of the spirits of the just… the Son of God appeared, declaring liberty to the captives who had been faithful; and there he preached to them the everlasting gospel” ([[D&C 138:12–19]]). In that light, he goes on, the psalm's “he heard” in verse 24 “not only refers to God hearing Christ in the midst of his affliction but also fits Joseph F. Smith's description of the congregation assembled waiting for Christ, whose cries God had also heard.”[@hopkin-psalm-22] The site's note on [Psalm 22:22](/psalms/22/) records this reading as a fit, and I take it as one.

Now set Psalm 88 beside it. Psalm 88's sufferer counted himself among the dead, and his six questions were all one question: can anything of God reach the dead? “Shall thy lovingkindness be declared in the grave?” ([[Ps. 88:11]]). He expected no. Within his psalm there is no answer, and I am not going to put one there. But the one who prayed Psalm 22's first line went, after His death, to the dead. Peter says it: He “went and preached unto the spirits in prison” ([[1 Pet. 3:19]]), and for this cause “was the gospel preached also to them that are dead” ([[1 Pet. 4:6]]). The revelation to Joseph F. Smith says it at length, and ends its account, “thus was the gospel preached to the dead” ([[D&C 138:30]]). Delitzsch said that in the New Testament the state of the dead “itself” had become different. The Restoration says how.

The Hebrew lets the two psalms speak to each other more closely than the English shows. “Declared” in Psalm 88:11, the thing the sufferer was sure could not happen in the grave, is the verb of Psalm 22:22, “I will declare thy name unto my brethren.”[@oshb-wlc] On Hopkin's reading, that declaring was done among the faithful dead. The join between Psalm 88's question and that congregation is mine, and no source I have found makes it. But the question Psalm 88 asked, whether God's lovingkindness would be declared to the dead, is the question the Savior's descent answered, and in the psalm's own verb. The Psalter had already said, in its own way, that the place was not beyond Him: “if I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there” ([[Ps. 139:8]]), where “hell” is Psalm 88's Sheol;[@oshb-wlc] the essay [Who Can Ransom?](/psalms/themes/who-can-ransom/) follows that verse.

## The disciple's Psalm 88

This gives a disciple whose prayer is not being answered three things, and the first is permission. The Psalter kept Psalm 88. It did not edit the darkness out or append a turn. Scripture Central's study of the laments observes that their fixed form was “most likely established so that once recorded, a lament could be reused by others,” so that “these ancient petitions may become the readers' own prayers to a merciful Father.”[@sc-knowhy-438] A disciple who can find no turn in his own prayer has a psalm in the canon that could not find one either, and it is addressed, from its first word, to “the LORD God of my salvation.”

The second is company. Hopkin's reading of the cry is that it “showed that Christ had truly ‘descended below all things’… so that he could understand and succor his people,” and he recalls that when Joseph Smith prayed Psalm 13's question in Liberty Jail, “O God, where art thou?”, the answer he recorded was “The Son of Man hath descended below them all. Art thou greater than he?” ([[D&C 122:8]]).[@hopkin-psalm-22] The one who hears a disciple pray Psalm 88 has prayed Psalm 22's opening Himself, which cries day and night as Psalm 88 does. Elder Holland: “because Jesus walked such a long, lonely path utterly alone, we do not have to do so… we will never be left alone nor unaided, even if sometimes we may feel that we are.”[@holland-none-with-him] Elder Hales said it of Gethsemane, where the Savior was “ultimately without the presence of His Father”: “‘in my Gethsemane’ and yours, we are not alone.”[@hales-waiting]

The third is the end of the matter, which Psalm 88 could not see and Psalm 22 did. The hidden face is not the last word about God, even when it is the last word of a prayer. “Neither hath he hid his face from him; but when he cried unto him, he heard.” Elder Holland has taught that prayers “are heard and they are answered according to His unfailing love and cosmic timetable,” even when not “the way I asked.”[@holland-hidden-fire-2024] The disciple's darkness is real, and nothing in these psalms asks anyone to pretend it is light. What they ask, with the Restoration's help, is that we know He has been in it, that He came out of it declaring the Father's name to His brethren, and that His brethren include the dead. The book of Psalms is called in Hebrew “Praises,”[@bd-psalms] and its last section, Psalms 107 to 150, “praise[s] God for delivering Israel through the Messiah.”[@scripture-helps-ps-1] It ends, “Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD” ([[Ps. 150:6]]). It gets there without going back to fix Psalm 88. On my reading, it gets there because the one who prayed Psalm 22's first line and lived its last made the praise possible for everyone who prays Psalm 88, and for everyone who has already gone down to the dust ([[Ps. 22:29]]).
