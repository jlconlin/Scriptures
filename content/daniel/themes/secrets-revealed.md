Nebuchadnezzar, on his face before a captive who has just told him his dream, says the sentence a reader remembers: “your God is a God of gods, and a Lord of kings, and a revealer of secrets, seeing thou couldest reveal this secret” ([[Dan. 2:47]]). It is easy to hear the title as Daniel’s. It is not. Everyone the book calls a revealer of secrets is God: “there is a God in heaven that revealeth secrets” ([[Dan. 2:28]]); “he that revealeth secrets maketh known to thee what shall come to pass” ([[Dan. 2:29]]); and the king’s own words, said of “your God.” The one time the verb is used of Daniel, it is in the king’s “thou couldest reveal this secret,” and the king gives the title in the same breath to Daniel’s God.[@oshb-wlc] Daniel is the man to whom the secret “was revealed… in a night vision” ([[Dan. 2:19]]), who insists it “is not revealed to me for any wisdom that I have more than any living” ([[Dan. 2:30]]), and of whom the king can only say, “no secret troubleth thee” ([[Dan. 4:9]]). The Bible Dictionary notices the care: Daniel, like Joseph, “is careful to point out that he is not able to do this by his own wisdom but because of the wisdom given him by the God of heaven.”[@bd-daniel-book]

That distinction, between the one who reveals and the one who is shown, is what this essay follows through the book, and it goes somewhere the first chapters do not prepare a reader for. In the court stories Daniel is the one who makes known. In the visions he is the one who must be made to understand. At the end he hears and says, “I understood not” ([[Dan. 12:8]]), and is told to shut up the words and seal the book. The man whom kings consulted leaves the stage not understanding when and how his last vision ends, and the understanding is promised, at the time of the end, to “the wise” ([[Dan. 12:10]]), which is the very word the book used for Daniel and his companions in its first chapter.[@oshb-wlc]

My argument is that this is not a decline but a lesson the book teaches by its shape. The revealer of secrets is never Daniel; it is the Lord. The book shows the greatest of interpreters standing at last where everyone else stands, before words he cannot open, and then it names the condition on which they will be opened: not skill, but righteousness. And the New Testament names the one who opens them. “The Revelation of Jesus Christ,” John’s book begins, and the Joseph Smith Translation makes it plain that the revelation “was given unto him of Jesus Christ.”[@nt-manual-53] In that book a sealed scroll is held up and no one in heaven or earth can open it, until the Lamb does. Daniel’s sealed book is not that scroll, and the Church’s own revelation says what that scroll is; I will keep the two apart. But the pattern is one pattern. Secrets are God’s to reveal, books are God’s to open, and the one who reveals and opens is the Lord whom Daniel called “a God in heaven,” and whom John saw as a Lamb that had been slain. The comparison across the two books is mine, and I say where the sources stop.

## He that revealeth secrets maketh known

Begin with the words of the Aramaic chapters, since that is where the title lives. “Secret,” *rāz*, occurs nine times in Daniel, eight of them in chapter 2 and once in chapter 4, and the verb “reveal,” *gālēʾ*, seven times in six verses, all in chapter 2.[@oshb-wlc] The participle, “he that revealeth,” “a revealer,” is God’s every time it is used (2:22, 28, 29, 47).[@oshb-wlc] What Daniel does is mostly a different verb. God “revealeth secrets, and maketh known to the king” ([[Dan. 2:28]]); the king asks Daniel, “Art thou able to make known unto me the dream… and the interpretation?” ([[Dan. 2:26]]); and at the end of the court stories Daniel says to Belshazzar, “I will read the writing unto the king, and make known to him the interpretation” ([[Dan. 5:17]]). Making known is the Aramaic causative of the verb to know. It is the verb the kings demand of Babylon’s wise men, who fail at it ([[Dan. 2:9]]; [[Dan. 5:8]]); the verb Daniel performs; the verb God is said to do ([[Dan. 2:28]]; [[Dan. 2:45]]); and, in chapter 7, the verb of the one who “stood by” and “made me know the interpretation of the things” ([[Dan. 7:16]]).[@oshb-wlc] The observation about the two verbs is mine; no source I have read makes it. But the text keeps them apart with care. God reveals. Daniel makes known what God revealed. He is a conduit, and before Nebuchadnezzar he says so.

The Student Manual reads the scene before Nebuchadnezzar that way: “Daniel made it clear that it was not he, nor any of the wise men or soothsayers, who was able to determine the nature of the dream and its interpretation… Daniel did not take credit to himself for what the Lord had done.”[@ot-manual-28] Richard Draper draws the contrast with the Babylonian wise men, who had confessed that their gods’ “dwelling is not with flesh”: “their own gods were distant and impersonal, while Daniel’s shows that Jehovah, even in Babylon, was immediate and personal.”[@draper-exile] And Draper reads the king’s confession for what it is and is not: “the king allowing Jehovah a place among the pantheon of Babylonian gods and even according Him the special place as revealer.”[@draper-exile] The site’s note on [Daniel 2:47](/daniel/2/) weighs the concession. For this essay the point is the title. Nebuchadnezzar, who got many things wrong, got this one right. God was the revealer.

## Made to understand

Then the book turns, and the turn is in the verbs again. Chapter 7 is the first vision that is Daniel’s own. “Unlike earlier chapters in Daniel where kings received the dreams and then Daniel interpreted them, Daniel 7 recounts the prophet’s own dream,” Jared Ludlow writes, and the one who interprets it is “one of them that stood by,” “presumably an angel.”[@ludlow-son-of-man] The interpreter now needs someone to explain his vision to him. In chapter 8 a voice commands, “Gabriel, make this man to understand the vision” ([[Dan. 8:16]]), and Gabriel says to him, “Understand, O son of man” ([[Dan. 8:17]]). In chapter 9 Gabriel “informed me” ([[Dan. 9:22]]) and says, “understand the matter, and consider the vision” ([[Dan. 9:23]]). In chapter 10 the messenger says, “I am come to make thee understand what shall befall thy people in the latter days” ([[Dan. 10:14]]). The verb “understand” is the Hebrew *bîn*, and in these chapters it is repeatedly commanded of Daniel or brought to him ([[Dan. 8:16–17]]; [[Dan. 9:22–23]]; [[Dan. 10:11]]; [[Dan. 10:14]]).[@oshb-wlc] The book had introduced him as a man to whom God gave “understanding in all visions and dreams” ([[Dan. 1:17]]), the same root.[@oshb-wlc] Daniel still understands things himself: he “understood by books” the seventy years of Jeremiah ([[Dan. 9:2]]), and of the last vision the narrator says, “he understood the thing, and had understanding of the vision” ([[Dan. 10:1]]). But the understanding now has to be brought to him, and at the end of chapter 8 it has not arrived: “I was astonished at the vision, but none understood it” ([[Dan. 8:27]]).

This is not a failing peculiar to Daniel. It is the shape of the kind of writing he is doing, the “earliest example of apocalyptic literature currently in the Bible,” as the Bible Dictionary calls it.[@bd-daniel-book] Jared Halverson, describing the genre Daniel and Revelation both typify, quotes the scholars’ definition: “The constant element is the presence of an angel who interprets the vision or serves as guide on the otherworldly journey. This figure indicates that the revelation is not intelligible without supernatural aid.”[@halverson-apocalyptic] Scripture Central sets Daniel’s angelic guides beside John’s: Daniel “was guided through his visions by angelic messengers,” and John’s vision was “sent and signified” by God “through ‘his angel.’”[@sc-knowhy-492] A vision from God is not self-interpreting. The man who could explain Nebuchadnezzar’s dream needed help to understand his own.

## I heard, but I understood not

The book ends with Daniel asking a question and not getting the answer. He hears a man clothed in linen swear how long the wonders will last, and says, “I heard, but I understood not: then said I, O my Lord, what shall be the end of these things?” ([[Dan. 12:8]]). The answer: “Go thy way, Daniel: for the words are closed up and sealed till the time of the end… none of the wicked shall understand; but the wise shall understand” ([[Dan. 12:9–10]]). He had already been told, earlier in the chapter, to “shut up the words, and seal the book, even to the time of the end” ([[Dan. 12:4]]), as he had been told at the end of chapter 8 to “shut thou up the vision” ([[Dan. 8:26]]). The site’s notes on [Daniel 12](/daniel/12/) discuss the sealing and the unexplained numbers. Here I want only the fact, its limits, and what the commentators make of it. What Daniel did not understand was the answer to “How long shall it be to the end of these wonders?” ([[Dan. 12:6]]). Keil, setting 10:1 beside 12:8, says “the non-understanding in Daniel 12:8 regards a single point, namely, the duration of the affliction.”[@keil-daniel] The Student Manual says the same of what remains unrevealed: the time periods.[@ot-manual-28]

Keil hears the angel’s “Go thy way” as a word of comfort: “Calm thyself, Daniel, if thou dost not understand these words. The prophecy shall be preserved for all times to the end of the days… though thou thyself understandest it not, yet it shall be a source of great blessing to the people of God.”[@keil-daniel] He notices that the verb of 12:10, “shall understand,” stands “in such distinct relation to the ‘I understood not’” of 12:8 “that they must be taken in the same sense in both places.”[@keil-daniel] And Keil adds that sealing is not making a thing unreadable but keeping it safe: “the book should be preserved in security against any defacement of its contents, so that it might be capable of being read at all times down to the time of the end.”[@keil-daniel] The Church’s manuals say in their own way that the sealing held. “The interpretation of the time periods mentioned in these verses has not been revealed by the Lord as yet,” the Student Manual says, and adds that “for reasons not at present known, the Lord has not revealed the key for interpreting this passage, and, until He does so, speculation and calculation are pointless.”[@ot-manual-28] The seminary manual puts it in a sentence: “much of their meaning was not revealed even to Daniel.”[@ot-seminary-daniel]

So the book that opened with a God who reveals secrets closes with a man who is told to seal words he does not understand, and told who will understand them, and when. “The wise,” *hammaśkîlîm*, “till the time of the end.” It is the word of Daniel 12:3, where “they that be wise shall shine as the brightness of the firmament,” and of 11:33, where “they that understand among the people shall instruct many,” and it is the word the book used of Daniel and his friends in its fourth verse, “skilful in all wisdom” ([[Dan. 1:4]]), the participle there and the verb in 1:17, “skill in all learning,” and again when Gabriel comes “to give thee skill” ([[Dan. 9:22]]).[@oshb-wlc] The connection is mine, and no source I have read draws it, but the word that described Daniel’s company at the start names those to whom understanding is promised at the end. What was closed to Daniel is closed “till the time of the end,” and the last verse promises him his own place in that end: “thou shalt rest, and stand in thy lot at the end of the days” ([[Dan. 12:13]]).

## The condition

The condition of understanding is moral, and the book is explicit about it. Not learning: Daniel had all of that. “None of the wicked shall understand; but the wise shall understand.” The dividing line is moral. Joseph Smith read the verse exactly so, in 1842, setting it beside Daniel’s vision of the Son of Man: “It is for us to be righteous, that we may be wise and understand; for none of the wicked shall understand; but the wise shall understand.”[@tpjs-2007-ch-21] The Church’s edition sends the reader from “understand” in 12:10 to the Savior’s words about His parables, “it is given unto you to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it is not given” ([[Matt. 13:11]]), and from “knowledge” in 1:17 to Alma: “It is given unto many to know the mysteries of God; nevertheless they are laid under a strict command that they shall not impart only according to the portion of his word which he doth grant unto the children of men, according to the heed and diligence which they give unto him” ([[Alma 12:9]]).[@lds-scriptures] The New Testament Student Manual on the parables: “Even an all-powerful God cannot give to someone who will not receive.”[@nt-student-manual-5] The Bible Dictionary’s entry on the word says that a mystery in the New Testament is “a spiritual truth that was once hidden but now is revealed,” and warns in the same breath that “there is no spiritual gain in idle speculation about things the Lord has not revealed.”[@bd-mystery]

Set those beside the Lord’s own promise in Kirtland, to which the Church’s footnote at “secrets” in Daniel 2:28 sends the reader: “to them will I reveal all mysteries, yea, all the hidden mysteries of my kingdom from days of old… For by my Spirit will I enlighten them, and by my power will I make known unto them the secrets of my will” ([[D&C 76:7]]; [[D&C 76:10]]).[@lds-scriptures] The verbs are Daniel’s: reveal, make known. The speaker is the same.

## The Revelation of Jesus Christ

Who is the God in heaven that revealeth secrets? In chapter 2 Daniel says “God of heaven” and does not name Him further, and no Church source glosses the verse itself; Draper calls the God of Daniel 2 “Jehovah,”[@draper-exile] and the Bible Dictionary says that “Jehovah is the premortal Jesus Christ.”[@bd-jehovah] The book of Daniel that “corresponds in the Old Testament to the Revelation of John in the New Testament,” as the Bible Dictionary puts it[@bd-daniel-book], ends with a sealed book; the Revelation of John opens with a name. “The Revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave unto him, to shew unto his servants things which must shortly come to pass; and he sent and signified it by his angel unto his servant John” ([[Rev. 1:1]]). The New Testament Student Manual reads the title both ways: the book “is both an unveiling of the Lord Jesus Christ and an uncovering of Jesus Christ’s authority and power,” and the Joseph Smith Translation “clarif[ies] that the book was indeed a revelation given to John by the Savior, Jesus Christ.”[@nt-manual-53] Ellicott, quoting Grotius: “John is the writer, but Jesus Christ is the author.”[@ellicott-revelation] The pattern of Daniel is here entire: a God who reveals, an angel who makes known, a servant who sees. The difference is that the revealer has a name and a face.

And then the sealed book. “I saw in the right hand of him that sat on the throne a book written within and on the backside, sealed with seven seals. And I saw a strong angel proclaiming with a loud voice, Who is worthy to open the book, and to loose the seals thereof? And no man in heaven, nor in earth, neither under the earth, was able to open the book, neither to look thereon” ([[Rev. 5:1–3]]). John weeps. Then: “the Lion of the tribe of Juda, the Root of David, hath prevailed to open the book,” and the song, “Thou art worthy to take the book, and to open the seals thereof: for thou wast slain” ([[Rev. 5:5]]; [[Rev. 5:9]]). The Lord Himself told Joseph Smith what that book is: “the revealed will, mysteries, and the works of God; the hidden things of his economy concerning this earth during the seven thousand years of its continuance” ([[D&C 77:6]]).[@lds-scriptures] It is not Daniel’s book, and I do not say it is; the Church’s footnotes join Revelation 5 to that revelation and to nothing in Daniel, and join Daniel 12:9 to the sealed records of the Restoration, not to John.[@lds-scriptures] John Tvedtnes, who lists Daniel’s words among “other sealed books to come forth in the last days,” gives John’s scroll a paragraph of its own and does not join the two.[@tvedtnes-sealed-books]

On my reading the two books share a pattern, and it is the pattern Daniel’s own book had been teaching. A book sealed by God is not opened by a man’s own wisdom, however great; it is opened by God, through whom He will, as the Lord gave Joseph Smith “the keys of the mystery of those things which have been sealed” ([[D&C 35:18]]). The Student Manual: “Jesus Christ’s role as the only person worthy to open and read the sealed book highlights His authority as the executor of God’s plan of salvation.”[@nt-manual-54] Ellicott notices what kind of worthiness it is: “It was not lack of intellectual capacity so much as the taint of moral unworthiness which hindered the reading of the book,” and the Lamb’s “worthiness has been established in conflict and temptation.”[@ellicott-revelation] Draper reads John’s “look thereon” as “not just viewing but reading, understanding, or comprehending. This he could not do… The Lamb possessed those powers. He received them, we must remember, because of the wound.”[@draper-apocalyptic] It is Daniel 12:10 heard from the other side: none of the wicked shall understand, and the one who understands and opens is the one who was slain. The connection between the two scenes is mine. But the Church’s own apparatus puts the pieces within reach: the God who reveals secrets in Daniel 2 is, in the Bible Dictionary’s reading of Jehovah, the Lord; the Revelation is His; and the sealed book of heaven is opened by Him alone.

One more verse belongs here, because Daniel 9:24 uses the same word for sealing of the Messiah’s work. The seventy weeks of Gabriel’s answer are given, among other things, “to seal up the vision and prophecy, and to anoint the most Holy” ([[Dan. 9:24]]), and the verb is the *ḥātam* of 12:4 and 12:9.[@oshb-wlc] The Student Manual reads the clause of Christ: “By completing the mission His Father sent Him to accomplish, Christ fulfilled the law and the words of the prophets concerning His coming, and thus did He ‘seal up [make sure] the vision and prophecy,’” though it adds that the covenant of 9:27 “may make problematic the explanation given above.”[@ot-manual-28] Tvedtnes reads the same clause as one of Daniel’s sealed books, shut rather than made sure.[@tvedtnes-sealed-books] The site’s note on [Daniel 9:24](/daniel/9/) has the Hebrew. On the reading of sealing as making sure, the Messiah alone seals vision and prophecy in Daniel; Daniel himself seals his book in the sense of shutting it.

## Reading a sealed book

Daniel shows what a disciple does with words sealed “till the time of the end,” and so do the Church’s teachers. He asked. He was told to go his way. He went. Keil’s word for the angel’s answer is the right one: it was meant to calm him.[@keil-daniel] The Student Manual’s counsel offers the same reassurance: where the Lord has not given the key, “speculation and calculation are pointless,” and the manual reports the Prophet Joseph Smith’s teaching that if the Lord did not give the key for interpreting a symbol, He would not hold His children responsible for it.[@ot-manual-28] A disciple is not expected to understand what God has not revealed. He is asked to be the kind of person to whom God opens things. The Guide to the Scriptures: “Mysteries of God are spiritual truths known only by revelation. God reveals His mysteries to those who are obedient to the gospel. Some of God’s mysteries are yet to be revealed.”[@gs-mysteries-of-god]

That is Daniel’s own practice, from chapter 2. He did not ask God to make him clever; he asked his friends to pray ([[Dan. 2:18]]), and when the secret came he blessed the God who gave it ([[Dan. 2:20–23]]). At the end, when the secret did not come, he did not force it. The book’s last promise to him is not understanding but rest: “thou shalt rest, and stand in thy lot at the end of the days” ([[Dan. 12:13]]). A disciple who does not understand Daniel’s numbers shares Daniel’s position, and the book has already said where that is. The wise are set against the wicked, and Joseph Smith taught that righteousness is the way to be among them. The sealed things stay sealed until the Lord opens them. And the one who holds them is known. The God in heaven that revealeth secrets did not stop revealing when Daniel stopped understanding. He came, and John saw Him take the book that no man could open, and open it.
