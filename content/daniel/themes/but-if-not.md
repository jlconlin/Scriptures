Nebuchadnezzar puts the question that the whole book of Daniel answers in two ways: “who is that God that shall deliver you out of my hands?” ([[Dan. 3:15]]). The three men answer him with his own grammar. He had said, “but if ye worship not, ye shall be cast the same hour into the midst of a burning fiery furnace”; they say, “our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods” ([[Dan. 3:17–18]]). The Aramaic “but if not” of verse 18 is the same two words as the king’s “but if… not” in verse 15.[@oshb-wlc] He had set a condition; they return one. That echo is my observation; what the commentators say is what the condition means. Ellicott: “There is not the slightest ground for supposing that they expected a miraculous deliverance. Their language implies no more than faithful obedience.”[@ellicott-daniel] The Cambridge commentary paraphrases, “But even if He cannot, or will not, do this, still we can never fall down and worship thy gods.”[@cambridge-daniel] Elder Dennis E. Simmons called the second sentence “a statement of true faith,” and President Joy D. Jones said why: “These three young men were not basing their obedience upon being delivered… Keeping our covenants is always independent of our situation.”[@simmons-but-if-not][@jones-sin-resistant] The site’s note on [Daniel 3:18](/daniel/3/) weighs the two “ifs.”

My argument is that Daniel keeps both halves of the three men’s answer, “he will deliver us” and “but if not.” The book shows what God does when He delivers, twice, and then shows faithful people whom He does not deliver, and it ends by putting the two under one promise. The faith of 3:18 is the faith the whole book asks for, because the book itself does not let the reader assume the furnace ending. I say where each step comes from; the reading of the book’s two endings as one argument, and the pairing of the Book of Mormon’s Alma 14 with it, are mine.

## He will deliver

The first ending is rescue, and the book tells it twice in nearly the same words. The three come out of the fire and the king says their God “hath sent his angel, and delivered his servants that trusted in him” ([[Dan. 3:28]]). Daniel comes out of the den and says, “My God hath sent his angel, and hath shut the lions’ mouths” ([[Dan. 6:22]]). The Aramaic verb “deliver,” *šêzib*, carries both stories: it is in the king’s taunt, in the three’s answer, in Nebuchadnezzar’s praise, in Darius’s hope and his question at the den’s mouth, and in his decree, nine times in seven verses, and nowhere else in the book.[@oshb-wlc] Darius asks the question Nebuchadnezzar asked, in the same verb, “is thy God, whom thou servest continually, able to deliver thee from the lions?” ([[Dan. 6:20]]), and the three men’s earlier answer, “is able,” answers Darius’s question too.[@oshb-wlc] The site’s notes on [Daniel 3:15](/daniel/3/) and [Daniel 6:16](/daniel/6/) follow the verb through the two chapters, and the note on [Daniel 3:25](/daniel/3/) weighs who the fourth man in the fire was.

## They shall fall

The second ending comes in the last vision, and it is told of the same kind of people. “They that understand among the people shall instruct many: yet they shall fall by the sword, and by flame, by captivity, and by spoil, many days… And some of them of understanding shall fall, to try them, and to purge, and to make them white, even to the time of the end” ([[Dan. 11:33]]; [[Dan. 11:35]]). The ones who fall are the *maskilim*, and the book gave that name first to Daniel and his friends, “skilful in all wisdom” ([[Dan. 1:4]]); the site’s note on [Daniel 11:33](/daniel/11/) traces the word.[@oshb-wlc] They fall “by flame.” No angel’s rescue is described, and no one walks out. The text does say they are “holpen with a little help” ([[Dan. 11:34]]), so the help is not nothing; but it is not the furnace. The Cambridge commentary reads these verses of the martyrs under Antiochus, and compares the three men’s courage at 3:18 with that of “the loyal Jews in the age of the Maccabees”; it cites the same chapters of the Maccabees under both verses without saying that it has done so.[@cambridge-daniel] Whatever the date of the vision, the book that told the furnace story twice tells this one too, and tells it of “they that understand.”

The two endings are not joined by a shared word in the original. The book’s first half is Aramaic and its second Hebrew; “deliver” in 12:1 is the Hebrew *mālaṭ*, not the Aramaic *šêzib*, and “flame” in 11:33 is not the “flame of the fire” of 3:22.[@oshb-wlc] The link between the rescues and the falling is in the King James English, and in the people it happens to. I say so because the argument does not rest on a word; it rests on the shape of the book.

## Delivered, and awake

The last chapter puts the two endings under one promise, and the join is not mine but Keil’s. The promise opens with an angel, Michael, “the great prince which standeth for the children of thy people,” and goes on: “at that time thy people shall be delivered, every one that shall be found written in the book. And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life” ([[Dan. 12:1–2]]). Keil reads verse 2 as the necessary sequel to the falling of 11:33–35: since many of those who understand “lose their life in the persecution, so in the promise of deliverance a disclosure of the lot awaiting those who sealed with their blood their fidelity to God was not to be avoided.”[@keil-daniel] The Hebrew binds the passages too. The three verbs of 11:35, “try… purge… make them white,” come back in 12:10, “purified, and made white, and tried,” and so does the name for those who understand, “the wise shall understand”; each of the three roots stands in Daniel only in those two verses, and the Church’s footnotes join them both ways.[@oshb-wlc][@lds-scriptures] Between them sits the promise, and Keil reads it over both groups: “a part of the people shall outlive the tribulation, but those who lose their lives in the persecution shall rise again from the dead.”[@keil-daniel] The site’s note on [Daniel 12:2](/daniel/12/) gives the Savior’s own teaching of the two resurrections, which the Church’s footnote joins to the verse.

Daniel himself is given the second ending’s promise. He is not told he will see the end; he is told, “go thou thy way till the end be: for thou shalt rest, and stand in thy lot at the end of the days” ([[Dan. 12:13]]). Keil: “thou shalt rest, namely, in the grave, and rise again.”[@keil-daniel] The man who walked out of the den is promised the grave and the resurrection, and the Church’s edition gives “rise unto your destiny” as another rendering of the last words.[@lds-scriptures] The site’s note on [Daniel 12:13](/daniel/12/) has the Hebrew.

## The same two endings elsewhere

Hebrews 11 holds the same two endings side by side. The chapter’s roll of faith says that some “stopped the mouths of lions, Quenched the violence of fire” ([[Heb. 11:33–34]]), and the Church’s footnotes send the reader to Daniel 6:22 and Daniel 3:27.[@lds-scriptures] Then the roll turns: “others were tortured, not accepting deliverance; that they might obtain a better resurrection” ([[Heb. 11:35]]), which the Joseph Smith Translation gives as “the first resurrection.”[@lds-scriptures] The commentators do not read those “others” as Daniel 11; the Cambridge commentary thinks the reference may be to the Maccabean martyrs, the scribe Eleazar and the seven brothers, and glosses the better resurrection as “an everlasting reawakening of life.”[@cambridge-hebrews] Ellicott marks the turn: “hitherto we have heard of the victories of faith in action, now it is of the triumph of faith over suffering that the writer speaks,” and adds that those who escaped the sword and those who were slain by it “alike exemplified the power of faith.”[@ellicott-hebrews] So Hebrews sets Daniel’s rescues beside martyrs who refused rescue, in two consecutive verses, and names the resurrection as the second group’s hope. Elder Simmons set the two halves beside each other too: amid the glorious outcomes of Hebrews 11 “there were always the but if nots.”[@simmons-but-if-not] That Hebrews pairs the lions and the fire with the martyrs is in its text; that it is the pattern of Daniel 3, 6 and 11–12 is my reading.

The Book of Mormon has the same pair inside one chapter, and the Church’s own helps point from Daniel 3 to it. *Come, Follow Me* asks, beside the three friends, “What if your faith doesn’t lead to the miracles you seek?” and sends the reader to Alma 14:8–13; the seminary manual asks whether the refusal to worship would “have been worth the three men’s lives if the Lord had not saved them from the flames,” and cites Alma 14:8–11 and 60:13.[@cfm-ot-2026-46][@ot-seminary-daniel] In Ammonihah the believers are burned while Alma and Amulek watch, and Alma says, “The Spirit constraineth me that I must not stretch forth mine hand; for behold the Lord receiveth them up unto himself, in glory” ([[Alma 14:11]]). The Book of Mormon Student Manual: “The Lord did not permit him to do so.”[@bom-student-manual-26] Then, in the same chapter, the prison falls and the two prophets walk out, “and they were not hurt; for the Lord had granted unto them power, according to their faith which was in Christ” ([[Alma 14:28]]). The pairing of its prison with its fire, as Daniel’s pattern, is mine. Scripture Central notes that in the Book of Mormon’s accounts of martyrdom, Abinadi, the Anti-Nephi-Lehies and Ammonihah, “it is stated or implied that Salvation would be given to those who willingly suffered martyrdom.”[@sc-knowhy-351] Moroni says it as a rule: “the Lord suffereth the righteous to be slain that his justice and judgment may come upon the wicked; therefore ye need not suppose that the righteous are lost because they are slain; but behold, they do enter into the rest of the Lord their God” ([[Alma 60:13]]).

## Faith in the Lord Jesus Christ

Both of Daniel’s endings are the Lord’s. The One who sends the angel in chapter 3 is the One who promises the awakening in chapter 12, and *Scripture Helps*, summing up Daniel 1–7, says the Lord showed Daniel in vision “the ultimate deliverance of God’s faithful people through Jesus Christ.”[@scripture-helps-dan] Alma prayed for strength “according to our faith which is in Christ, even unto deliverance” ([[Alma 14:26]]); I read the believers’ end in 14:11 as the same deliverance in its other form. Jeffrey McClellan puts the three friends’ faith in a phrase: they “leaned for repose on Jesus, not on outcomes.”[@mcclellan-thy-troubles] Elder Wickman, speaking to parents whose child was not healed, said what faith is when the “if not” comes: “Faith is, quite simply, a confidence in the Lord.”[@wickman-but-if-not] The Bible Dictionary says of the book that “a testimony of mankind’s resurrection is given in Dan. 12.”[@bd-daniel-book] In my reading that testimony is what lets “but if not” be said in faith rather than resignation. The three did not know the end of their story; the book that tells it knows the end of every faithful story, and tells it in its last chapter.

President Kimball read the three men’s answer as faithfulness without a promise of survival: “The promises of eternal life from God supersede all promises of men to greatness, comfort, immunities,” and the men were saying, “We do not have to live, but we must be true to ourselves and God.”[@ot-manual-28] Daniel’s book gives that sentence its ground. God delivered twice, and the book also promises the resurrection to those who fall. A disciple can say “but if not” for the reason the three could: the ending is His either way, and the book’s last verse promises Daniel himself that he will rest and rise.
