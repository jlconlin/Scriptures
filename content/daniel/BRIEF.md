# Daniel brief

Book-level decisions for Daniel. The site-wide standard is in `STANDARDS.md`; this file records what is particular to Daniel. Writers, checkers, and reviewers read both before starting a chapter. Jeremiah's brief (`content/jeremiah/BRIEF.md`) is the model, and its section “What a chapter is for” holds here word for word; the author's rules recorded in Genesis's brief carry over to every book. Ezekiel's brief (“Learned from the first twelve chapters”) holds here too and is repeated below in short.

**Status (2026-10-08):** drafted by the coordinator from the sources named below, each read in the session with the research scripts. The author chose the book on 2026-10-08 (“Yeah, let's go with Daniel”) and has not read this brief. The book is in preview (`status: preview` in `book.yaml`). Where a section says “Proposed,” that is the approach the chapters follow; it is a decision of the coordinator's, not a fact from a source, and the author can overturn it.

Related files: `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` at the top of the repository (what waits for the author, for every book); `THEME-CANDIDATES.md` in this folder. Genesis, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Lamentations, Ezekiel, Malachi, Moses, and Abraham are on the site.

## What a chapter is for here

Daniel is short (12 chapters, 357 verses) and its two halves ask different things of a chapter.

**Chapters 1–6** are stories most readers know from childhood. The Church's helps are full here, and the danger is repeating them. A chapter does what they don't: says when this is and which king, from the book's own dates; explains the court and its words (eunuchs, Chaldeans, pulse, satraps and presidents, the law of the Medes and Persians, the instruments of chapter 3); reads the Aramaic where it changes what a reader understands (“is gone from me,” 2:5; “like the Son of God,” 3:25; *mene, tekel, peres*); and asks the follow-up question a careful reader asks (not “who was the fourth man?” only, but “what did Nebuchadnezzar, who did not worship the Lord, mean by what he said?”). The applications to a reader's life are in *Come, Follow Me* already and are not a chapter's work beyond a sentence where a source gives one.

**Chapters 7–12** are visions, and after chapter 7 the Church's newest helps stop. The Student Manual and the chapter headings are what the Church has on 8–12; Keil and the older commentaries will be needed and may pass the usual third where they are the only answer to a real question (as the author agreed for Lamentations 1 and 5), with the reason in the writer's report. A chapter says what Daniel sees, then **what the text itself says it means** (every vision has an interpreter: 7:16–27; 8:15–26; 9:21–27; 10:14 and the whole of 11–12), then what the Restoration has added, then what remains unexplained, and it says plainly which of these it is doing. Chapter 11 is forty-five verses of kings unnamed in the text: identify them as far as a source read does, in a few notes covering runs of verses, and don't write a note a verse.

## Divisions

The Bible Dictionary divides the book in two: “Dan. 1–6 contains narratives regarding Daniel and his three companions; Dan. 7–12 contains prophetic visions seen by Daniel and reported in his own name.” The Guide to the Scriptures and *Scripture Helps* say the same. `book.yaml` has three, keeping that line and adding one of the site's own at chapter 10, since chapters 10–12 are a single vision (10:1 to 12:13). Proposed: keep them; they are only the map on the book page.

|---|---|
| 1–6 | Faithful in the Courts of Babylon (`captives`) |
| 7–9 | The Beasts, the Ancient of Days, and the Seventy Weeks (`beasts`) |
| 10–12 | The Last Vision (`sealed`) |

## Book-wide conventions

- **Say when, from the book's own dates.** From `data/kjv/daniel.json`: 1:1 (the third year of Jehoiakim); 2:1 (the second year of Nebuchadnezzar); 3 and 4 (undated, under Nebuchadnezzar); 5 (Belshazzar's last night, 5:30); 5:31 and 6 (Darius the Median); 7:1 (the first year of Belshazzar); 8:1 (the third year of Belshazzar, at Shushan); 9:1 (the first year of Darius, of the seed of the Medes); 10:1 (the third year of Cyrus, the latest date in the book); 11:1 looks back to the first year of Darius the Mede. Daniel “continued even unto the first year of king Cyrus” (1:21) and “prospered in the reign of Darius, and in the reign of Cyrus the Persian” (6:28). **The visions of 7 and 8 come before the stories of 5 and 6**; say so in the settings of 7 and 8, once each.
- **Years B.C.** *Scripture Helps*: Daniel was taken “around the beginning of the sixth century BC,” and Cyrus “conquered Babylon in 539 BC.” Its first endnote: the Bible places the first event “around 606 BC, during the third year of King Jehoiakim's reign,” while “scholars generally believe that Nebuchadnezzar captured Jerusalem in 597 BC.” The Student Manual (28-1, 28-2) says “about 605 B.C.” and ties it to Carchemish (`[[Jer. 46:2]]`). Proposed: chapter 1's setting gives the book's own date, says that the Church's helps place it about 605 or 606 B.C., and mentions the scholars' 597 in a sentence from the endnote; later chapters don't return to it. Jerusalem fell in 586 B.C. (the Bible Dictionary's date, used across the site; the Student Manual here says “about 587”). A year reached by counting is given as “about.”
- **Daniel's contemporaries.** Ezekiel's book on the site names Daniel three times (14:14, 14:20, 28:3; the notes are at `[[Ezek. 14:14]]` and `[[Ezek. 28:3]]`); Jeremiah was in Jerusalem; the Student Manual (28-2) says Daniel “lived in Jerusalem at the same time Lehi did, though there is no evidence to suggest that they knew each other.” Say it once, in chapter 1's setting. Jeremiah on the site tells Jehoiakim's reign and the sieges: link (`[[Jer. 25:1]]`, `[[Jer. 25:11–12]]`, `[[Jer. 29:10]]`, `[[Jer. 46:2]]`) rather than retell.
- **Two languages.** The Bible Dictionary: “Dan. 2:4–7:28 is written in Aramaic and the rest in Hebrew.” The King James “Syriack” (2:4) is Aramaic (the Church's footnote). Say it once, at 2:4. `scripts/hebrew.mjs daniel <c>:<v>` gives the Aramaic words too (their morphology codes begin with `A`); a note that explains a word in 2:4–7:28 calls it Aramaic, not Hebrew.
- **Verse numbers.** The Hebrew Bible numbers 4:1–3 as 3:31–33, 4:4–37 as 4:1–34, 5:31 as 6:1, and 6:1–28 as 6:2–29 (`scripts/hebrew.mjs` prints both). Commentaries may follow the Hebrew; cite the King James verse.
- **The four names.** Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah are renamed Belteshazzar, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abed-nego (1:6–7; the Church edition hyphenates Abed-nego). Explain the renaming once, at 1:7, from a source read (what each name means is given only from a lexicon or a commentary's page). Belteshazzar (Daniel) and Belshazzar (the king of chapter 5) are different names; a reader confuses them, so chapter 5 says so in a sentence.
- **Names of God.** The book says “the God of heaven,” “the most High,” “the King of heaven,” and “the living God,” mostly in the mouths of Daniel and of foreign kings; “LORD” (the divine name) comes only in chapter 9 (seven times), where “Lord” (*adonai*) stands beside it. Copy any phrase with the name from `data/kjv/daniel.json`. As in the other books, the Bible Dictionary identifies the LORD of the Old Testament as the premortal Christ; say it where it first matters and only once (chapter 9's prayer is the place).
- **What a king says is what the king says.** Nebuchadnezzar and Darius praise Daniel's God (2:47; 3:28–29; 4:34–37; 6:26–27). The Student Manual (28-18) cautions that acknowledging Daniel's gifts “was not acknowledging Jehovah as the only or even the supreme god.” Don't write a conversion the text doesn't; chapter 4 is in the king's own voice, and what became of his belief is not said.
- **Repeated things are explained once and linked.** The four kingdoms (chapter 2, the full account; chapter 7 says what is the same and what is new, and 8 names two of them itself); the kingdom that shall not be destroyed (2:44; 7:14, 27); Gabriel (8:16; then 9:21); Michael (10:13; then 10:21 and 12:1; and the Ancient of days, 7:9); “the abomination that maketh desolate” (the full note at 11:31; 8:13, 9:27, and 12:11 link to it); the sealing of the book (8:26; 12:4, 9); “the time of the end.”
- **Daniel and Joseph.** The Bible Dictionary notes the “points of resemblance” twice: both captives, both interpreters of a king's dreams, and both “careful to point out” that the wisdom was God's (`[[Gen. 41:16]]`; Dan. 2:27–30). Genesis is on the site: link at 2:28, and don't build the parallel out further than a source does.
- **Echoes in the book of Revelation** (the beasts from the sea; a time, times, and a half; the books opened; Michael). Revelation is not on the site. Name an echo where a Church footnote or a source read joins the two, in a sentence; explaining John's vision is not Daniel's chapter's work. The Bible Dictionary: Daniel “corresponds in the Old Testament to the Revelation of John in the New Testament,” with Moses 7 and 1 Nephi 8 and 11 as earlier visions of the kind.
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** One reading is in the Church edition's footnotes: 5:28 (the word given as “Upharsin”). It belongs in chapter 5 with a ledger row from the footnote (`lds-scriptures`).
- **The additions in the Apocrypha.** The Bible Dictionary names “the Song of the Three Children, the History of Susanna, and Bel and the Dragon” as additions “not included in the present canon.” Chapter 3 may say so in a sentence, from the Bible Dictionary; Brenton's page for chapter 3 on Bible Hub does not print the Song, so where it stands in the Greek is said only if a page that shows it is found. Nothing is quoted from the additions as scripture. D&C 91 is the Lord's word on the Apocrypha, if a source joins it.
- **Book of Mormon comparison panel: none.** Use the `scripture` kind for Book of Mormon passages.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports what runs across the book; the coordinator adds it to `THEME-CANDIDATES.md`. No theme pages are written until the author chooses them.

**Habits to avoid, learned on Ezekiel** (its brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter (the setting, the thread, a note, and the Witness of Christ each say what only they say); don't re-explain what an earlier chapter explained, point back with a `[[reference]]`; a Witness of Christ does not open by saying what the chapter lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer, not a promise of one (the site shows that sentence alone as the note's card); a reading stays a reading in every sentence; “In plain words” adds nothing to the verse; Hebrew or Aramaic only where it changes what the reader understands.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that twelve chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-08.

- **When the book was written, and by whom.** The Church's sources treat Daniel as a prophet of the sixth century B.C. and the visions as his: the Bible Dictionary (“prophetic visions seen by Daniel and reported in his own name”), *Scripture Helps*, and the Student Manual; the Savior speaks of “Daniel the prophet” (Matt. 24:15; JS—M 1:12, 32). No Church source read raises a later date. Proposed: the chapters follow the Church's sources and the book's own dates and do not argue the question. The view that the book, or its second half, was written in the second century B.C. was not read on any page in setting up; Keil's introduction (on his chapter 1 page) answers “the modern critics” who count 1:1 among the errors that “must disprove the genuineness of this book,” and was read only as far as that sentence. A view known from memory is not stated (`STANDARDS.md` §4). If a writer meets the question on a page it is reading (Keil's introduction and Ludlow's article are the likeliest), it may be reported in a sentence as that page's, in chapter 7 or 8 and nowhere else. This is the one decision in this brief the author is most likely to want to make himself (`OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`, D1).
- **The third year of Jehoiakim (1:1) beside Jeremiah.** See “Years B.C.” above. `[[Jer. 25:1]]` calls the fourth year of Jehoiakim the first of Nebuchadnezzar. A reconciliation is given only from a source read, as that source's (Keil treats it on his chapter 1 page, at 1:1, and concludes for the fourth year of Jehoiakim, “606 b.c.”).
- **The king's meat and the Word of Wisdom (1:8–16).** *Scripture Helps*: the food “may have first been offered to Babylonian gods,” and “may have violated dietary laws given in the law of Moses.” The Bible Dictionary speaks of “abstinence from wine”; the Church's footnote on “pulse” points to D&C 89:14; the Institute manual builds a lesson on the Word of Wisdom. Proposed: the note gives *Scripture Helps*' two reasons, in its “may have”; D&C 89 is set beside the passage as the Church's footnote and lesson do, in a sentence, without saying Daniel kept the Word of Wisdom, which was not yet given.
- **“The thing is gone from me” (2:5).** The Church's footnote: “Persian: sure with me; i.e., he knew his dream and desired to test them.” The Student Manual (28-9) says the same from Ellis T. Rasmussen. A good note: it changes how the whole scene reads. Give it from the footnote and from the word itself (`hebrew.mjs`, a lexicon page); Rasmussen's book is cited only as the manual prints it.
- **The four kingdoms of chapter 2.** The text names only the first (“Thou art this head of gold,” 2:38). *Scripture Helps* names none of the others (“kingdoms that would rise after Babylon's fall”). The Student Manual (28-13) gives President Rudger Clawson's list (the Medes and Persians, Macedonia under Alexander, Rome, and then the kingdoms Rome broke into) and President Spencer W. Kimball's (“a group of nations of Europe represented by the toes”); *Scripture Helps* cites the same talk of President Kimball's in an endnote. For chapter 7, *Scripture Helps* gives both scholarly lists (Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, Rome; or Babylon, Media, Persia, Greece) and says that “regardless of the specific meaning of the beasts,” earthly kingdoms are temporary and God's will endure. Proposed: chapter 2 says what the text names, then gives the list the Church's leaders have taught as theirs (President Kimball's talk is readable in full: `kimball-stone`), and says in a sentence that chapter 8 itself names Media and Persia and Grecia (8:20–21). Chapter 7 gives *Scripture Helps*' two lists as it gives them. Neither chapter argues for one. **No modern nation is named for a toe or a horn, and no present-day event is brought in.**
- **The stone cut out without hands (2:34–35, 44–45).** This is settled and is the book's best-known Restoration connection: D&C 65:2; the chapter heading (“The stone is the latter-day kingdom of God”); Elder Christofferson (“The Church is that prophesied latter-day kingdom”); President Kimball; President Hinckley (“The Stone Cut Out of the Mountain,” Oct. 2007). The Student Manual (28-14) adds a distinction between the Church now and the kingdom in the Millennium, from *Mormon Doctrine* and *Doctrines of Salvation*; those are cited only as the manual prints them, and the distinction is given only if the note needs it. The note's own work: what “without hands” means in the text, “in the days of these kings,” the mountain, and what the stone does (it strikes the feet, 2:34, and the whole image falls together, 2:35). Quote one of the modern statements, once; cite the others. This will be the book's most-read note: keep it clear and unhurried.
- **The image of gold (3:1) and the dream.** Whether Nebuchadnezzar's image answers his dream of a head of gold is said only from a source read.
- **“But if not” (3:17–18).** *Scripture Helps* gives the modern rendering of “we are not careful to answer thee” and President Joy D. Jones's sentence; Elder Dennis E. Simmons's talk “But If Not …” (Apr. 2004) is the fuller treatment and is readable. Explain the words; the application is theirs, in a sentence.
- **The fourth man in the furnace (3:25).** *Scripture Helps* (2025): “It is unclear from the text whether this was Jehovah or an angel. Some translations render the phrase as ‘a divine being’ or ‘son of the gods.’” Nebuchadnezzar himself says God “hath sent his angel” (3:28). Proposed: the newest wins. The note gives the Aramaic (a lexicon page and the interlinear), what the king says three verses later, and *Scripture Helps*' statement as the frame; the chapter does not say the fourth man was Jesus Christ. Its Witness of Christ rests on what a source read does say (the Lord with His people in the fire: `[[Isa. 43:2]]`, which the Church's footnote at 3:27 joins to it).
- **Nebuchadnezzar's madness (chapter 4).** Seven “times” pass over him (the footnote: “OR years, or seasons”; at 4:34, “IE the seven years”). Describe what the text says happened; a medical name for it, or a claim that Babylonian records confirm or lack it, needs a source read. The chapter's point is the text's own (4:17, 25, 32).
- **Belshazzar (chapter 5).** The text calls him king and Nebuchadnezzar his father. The Bible Dictionary: Nabonidus “had a son Belshazzar … who was never king, but was prince-regent during his father's reign.” *Scripture Helps* (endnote 22): “the son of Nabonidus, the final king of Babylon, and … co-regent to his father.” The Student Manual (28-22, 28-25) explains “the third ruler in the kingdom” (5:7) from it. Proposed: say this once, at 5:1, from those; “father” (5:2, 11, 18) is explained only from a source read. **A slip to keep out:** *Scripture Helps*' endnote 22 says Belshazzar “lived until approximately 543 BC,” which does not agree with its own date for the fall of Babylon (539 B.C.) on the night he was killed (5:30). Don't repeat the 543; use 539 for that night.
- **The writing on the wall (5:25–28).** *Scripture Helps* gives two levels: three weights in descending order, and three verbs they sound like (numbered, weighed, divided). The Student Manual (28-27) adds the play of *peres* on “Persians.” The text has “UPHARSIN” in 5:25 and “PERES” in 5:28; the Joseph Smith Translation reads “Upharsin” in 5:28. All of that is the note; the weights' sizes are from *Scripture Helps*' endnote 24, cited as *Scripture Helps*.
- **Babylon taken in a night (5:30–31).** The Student Manual (28-29) tells the diverting of the Euphrates from Herodotus as an encyclopedia relays him, and Cyrus's reception from a modern handbook. Those are relayed twice over (`STANDARDS.md` §4 rule 6). Herodotus is readable in an old translation (find one on archive.org or Project Gutenberg and propose an entry); cite him for what he says, or leave the river out. Isaiah and Jeremiah on the site foretell the fall (`[[Isa. 13:17–22]]`, `[[Isa. 45:1]]`, `[[Jer. 51:11]]`, `[[Jer. 51:57]]`): link.
- **Darius the Mede (5:31; 6; 9:1; 11:1).** The Bible Dictionary: “It is impossible to identify him with any of the kings of Babylon known to secular history.” Proposed: say that, once, at 5:31 or 6:1, as the Bible Dictionary's; an identification (with a governor under Cyrus, or with Cyrus) is given only from a source read and as that source's. The chapters treat him as the text does.
- **Daniel 6 and the life of the Savior.** *Scripture Helps* prints a table of five likenesses (enemies who found no fault; faithful at the cost of life; a ruler who wished to release him; a sealed stone; coming out alive). It is beside the chapter in the Gospel Library already. Proposed: chapter 6's Witness of Christ names the likeness in two or three sentences and cites the table; it does not reproduce it.
- **The beasts are images of kingdoms (chapter 7).** Joseph Smith, in the passage *Scripture Helps* cites from the Joseph Smith Papers (`jsp-history-d1`, page 1522; the entry exists for another page, so propose one for this page): God used “the figure of a beast” for “the kingdoms of the world” and never for His own kingdom. The Student Manual (28-35) prints more of the same sermon from *Teachings of the Prophet Joseph Smith*; read it in the Papers. For which kingdoms, see the four kingdoms above.
- **The little horn (7:8, 20–25) and the fourth beast.** The Student Manual (28-37): the horn “cannot be positively identified with any specific individual or kingdom of the world”; it prints Sidney B. Sperry's suggestion, offered as a suggestion, that the fourth beast is “the ‘great and abominable church’ of our modern scriptures.” *Scripture Helps* does not treat the horn. Proposed: say what the text says the horn does and that its end is the judgment (7:26); give the manual's sentence that it is not identified; Sperry's suggestion may be reported in a sentence as his, from the manual. **No church, pope, ruler, or nation is named for it,** whatever an older commentary read on Bible Hub does.
- **The Ancient of days and one like the Son of man (7:9–14).** Settled by revelation: D&C 116 and 27:11 (Adam is the Ancient of Days); the chapter heading; Joseph Smith's statement, printed in *Teachings of Presidents of the Church: Joseph Smith* (chapter 8) and in *Scripture Helps*; Joseph Fielding Smith on the council at Adam-ondi-Ahman (cited as *Scripture Helps* or the manual prints him). The Bible Dictionary: “The ‘Son of Man’ is Jesus Christ.” Ludlow's article (`ludlow-son-of-man`) is what *Scripture Helps* rests on for the title in the Gospels (`[[Mark 14:62]]`) and is the place to look beyond the Gospel Library. A reader coming from other Christian reading will have met the Ancient of days as God the Father; a note may say that Latter-day revelation identifies him as Adam, and why it matters to the scene (the Son of Man is brought to him and is *given* dominion). “Son of man” in 8:17 is Daniel himself, as in Ezekiel (`[[Ezek. 2:1]]` has the note): say so there in a sentence. This is the chapter's Witness of Christ.
- **The ram, the goat, and the little horn of chapter 8.** The text explains itself: Media and Persia, Grecia and its first king, four kingdoms after him (8:20–22). The Student Manual (28-42): the first king is Alexander; the little horn “has generally been interpreted to represent Antiochus IV (Epiphanes), who ruled Syria 175–164 B.C.”; and it holds that the vision reaches further, since the Church's footnotes read “the last end of the indignation” (8:19) as “the last days.” The chapter heading: “in the last days, a fierce king who will destroy the holy people.” Proposed: give the text's own key; then Antiochus as the reading the manual reports, with what he did from the Bible Dictionary (“Antiochus Epiphanes,” “Maccabees”) and the manual's Enrichment K; then the Church's footnotes and heading for the reach to the last days. Both, in that order, neither argued down. Josephus (*Antiquities* 12.5; `josephus-antiquities` is in `sources.yaml`) is readable for what Antiochus did. 1 Maccabees is not readable at the address tried; find another before citing it.
- **The 2,300 days (8:14) and the numbers of 12:7, 11–12.** The Student Manual (28-52), on chapter 12: “The interpretation of the time periods mentioned in these verses has not been revealed by the Lord as yet,” and calculations have been “each in their turn … proven wrong”; it names William Miller's 1844 prediction, which Joseph Smith “declared to be false.” Proposed: say what the numbers are, what the Hebrew of 8:14 says (“evening, morning,” two words, where the King James has “days”; `hebrew.mjs daniel 8:14`), and that their meaning has not been revealed. **No calculation is made or reported with approval, and no date is derived.** The Miller episode may be told in a sentence in chapter 12, from the manual and, if found, the Joseph Smith Papers.
- **Gabriel is Noah; Michael is Adam.** The Student Manual (28-43, 28-47) from Joseph Smith; the Bible Dictionary (“Gabriel,” “Michael”); D&C 27:11; 107:53–56; 128:21. Say each once (8:16; 10:13). `bd-gabriel` is in `sources.yaml`; the Joseph Smith statement is in the Papers (`jsp-discourse-1839-a`, already an entry: check that it is the page).
- **Daniel's prayer (9:3–19).** He reads Jeremiah (`[[Jer. 25:11–12]]`, `[[Jer. 29:10]]`, both with notes on the site) and prays for a people whose sins he counts as his own. This is the chapter's larger half and its plainest; give it its due before the seventy weeks. The Student Manual (28-44) prints Sperry on what Daniel was working out; cite as the manual prints him.
- **The seventy weeks (9:24–27).** The Student Manual (28-45): the word is “sevens”; Sperry called the verses “one of the most difficult passages in all of the Old Testament”; 9:24 “is undoubtedly a reference to the coming of Christ and His Atonement”; 9:26, “cut off, but not for himself,” “seems to be an allusion to His Crucifixion”; the rest “parallels very closely” Matthew 24:15 and JS—M 1:12; and the one week of 9:27 “has not been satisfactorily explained.” The chapter heading: “Gabriel reveals the time of the coming of the Messiah … The Messiah will be cut off.” Proposed: say what the heading and the manual say, in that measure (the Messiah, His Atonement, His being cut off, the city and sanctuary destroyed), and say plainly that the arithmetic is not settled. **No scheme of years is worked out, from Artaxerxes or anywhere else, even where Keil or Clarke works one.** A note may report that such reckonings exist and differ. This carries the chapter's Witness of Christ.
- **The man clothed in linen (10:5–6).** The chapter heading: “Daniel sees the Lord and others in a glorious vision.” The Student Manual (28-46) heads its note “The Appearance of the Lord to Daniel” and compares Ezekiel 1:26–28 (on the site), Revelation 1:13–15, and D&C 110:2–3. But the one who speaks from 10:10 on says he was withstood twenty-one days until Michael came to help him (10:13), which a reader will not take to be the Lord. Proposed: follow the heading for 10:5–9 (“the Lord and others”), and say from the text that the messenger of 10:10–21 is one of the others; a source read that divides the chapter so is wanted, and if none is found the note says what the text shows and that the heading speaks of more than one.
- **The prince of the kingdom of Persia (10:13, 20).** The Student Manual (28-48): “Satan or one of his host,” offered with “if that reasoning is correct.” Give it in that measure.
- **Chapter 11.** The Student Manual (28-50) does not walk the kings; it quotes Keil that the chapter is “an ideal description of the war of the kings of the north and the south in its general outlines,” says “the lack of direct correspondence between the chapter and history” suggests an overview, and reads much of it as dual: Antiochus, Rome in A.D. 70, and Armageddon (“it is very possible that all four answers are correct”). The chapter heading: kings and wars “that lead up to the Second Coming of Christ.” The Church's footnotes identify a few things (11:2, “IE kingdom of Macedonia”). Proposed: the setting says what kind of chapter this is, from the manual; notes cover runs of verses (11:2–4, Persia and the mighty king; 11:5–20, the kings of the south and north; 11:21–35, the vile person; 11:36–45, the king who exalts himself and the time of the end), identifying kings only from a source read and as that source's (Keil's and Barnes's pages on Bible Hub name them; Enrichment K gives the Ptolemies and Seleucids). Where the older commentators part ways (at 11:36, on whether Antiochus is still meant), say that they do. No modern nation is named for the king of the north or the south.

- **The lions' den and the accusers' families (6:24).** The wives and children are thrown in with the accusers. A reader will stop here. Say what the text says; that it was the king's act and Persian practice is said only from a source read (the Student Manual has a paragraph, 28-33); don't justify it and don't pass over it.

## The text

`data/kjv/daniel.json` was imported on 2026-10-08 (12 chapters, 357 verses) and compared verse by verse with the Gospel Library edition by `scripts/check-kjv.mjs daniel`. It found 20 verses that differed: the hyphen in Abed-nego (fifteen verses), spellings (“inquired,” 1:20; “steadfast,” 6:26), two commas (5:10), a lower-case “god” in Nebuchadnezzar's mouth (4:8), and “Lord” (*adonai*) where the source file has LORD (9:7, 9:16). Seven entries in `LDS_SPELLINGS` in `scripts/fetch-kjv.mjs` (under a `// Daniel` comment) correct them, and the two now agree in every verse.
