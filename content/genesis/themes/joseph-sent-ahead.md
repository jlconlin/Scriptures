Brothers who “could not speak peaceably” to Joseph sell him. When he makes himself known years later he says that they sold him and that God sent him, and the story ends with brothers who talk with him. What changes from first passage to last is what the sale is called: “sold” at the start, “sold” and “sent” at the end.

## Hated and sold

Jacob loves Joseph most, and his brothers “hated him, and could not speak peaceably unto him” ([[Gen. 37:4]]). They strip him, “cast him into a pit,” and sell him “for twenty pieces of silver” ([[Gen. 37:24]], [[Gen. 37:28]]). The [chapter 37 notes](/genesis/37/) carry the detail.

## Out of the pit

In prison Joseph says, “they should put me into the dungeon” ([[Gen. 40:15]]), and Pharaoh’s men bring him “hastily out of the dungeon” ([[Gen. 41:14]]). The word behind “dungeon” in both verses is *bor*, the word for the “pit” of [[Gen. 37:24]].[@oshb-wlc] See the notes on [[Gen. 40:15]] and [[Gen. 41:14]].

## Bread in the famine

The famine is “in all lands; but in all the land of Egypt there was bread” ([[Gen. 41:54]]). His brothers come and “bowed down themselves before him” ([[Gen. 42:6]]); “Joseph remembered the dreams” ([[Gen. 42:9]]) in which sheaves “made obeisance” ([[Gen. 37:7]]).

## Guilt found out

After three days in ward the brothers say to one another, “We are verily guilty concerning our brother” ([[Gen. 42:21]]). Later Judah says, “God hath found out the iniquity of thy servants” ([[Gen. 44:16]]; the note on that verse weighs which guilt he means). Reuben’s remorse and Judah’s offer to take Benjamin’s punishment are read as signs that the brothers had changed.[@scripture-helps-gen-42-50] The [Judah page](/genesis/themes/judah/) follows that offer.

<h2 id="sold-sent">Sold, and sent</h2>

Joseph says, “I am Joseph your brother, whom ye sold into Egypt.” Then: “be not grieved, nor angry with yourselves, that ye sold me hither: for God did send me before you to preserve life” ([[Gen. 45:4–5]]). Verse 8 adds, “it was not you that sent me hither, but God.” Joseph names the sale twice before he names God. His words do not mean that God caused the brothers to sell him; they recognize that God knows all things and can bring good out of what was done.[@scripture-helps-gen-42-50] In Genesis the Hebrew verbs for “sold” (*makar*, 10 times) and “sent” (*shalach*, 66 times) stand in one verse only at 45:5.[@oshb-wlc] A psalm puts the same two verbs in one line: “He sent a man before them, even Joseph, who was sold for a servant” ([[Ps. 105:17]]).[@oshb-wlc] The [chapter 45 notes](/genesis/45/) take up the verses.

## Brothers again

Joseph “kissed all his brethren, and wept upon them: and after that his brethren talked with him” ([[Gen. 45:15]]). Set beside “could not speak peaceably unto him” ([[Gen. 37:4]]), the verb for speaking is the same, *dabar*.[@oshb-wlc] It is the ordinary Hebrew verb for speaking (73 times in Genesis; Joseph “spake roughly” with it at [[Gen. 42:7]]), so the word alone proves little. The note on [[Gen. 45:15]] follows “could not” through both chapters.

Set beside Esau, who “ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept” ([[Gen. 33:4]]): Joseph “fell upon his brother Benjamin’s neck, and wept” ([[Gen. 45:14]]), then kisses his brothers. The Hebrew words for falling on the neck, kissing and weeping are the same, and Genesis has all three together only in these two meetings; Joseph also falls on his father’s neck and weeps ([[Gen. 46:29]]).[@oshb-wlc]

When Jacob dies the brothers fear again: “Joseph will peradventure hate us” ([[Gen. 50:15]]). Joseph answers, “ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive” ([[Gen. 50:20]]). “Thought” and “meant” are one Hebrew verb, *chashav*.[@oshb-wlc] He “comforted them, and spake kindly unto them” ([[Gen. 50:21]]).

<h2 id="like-the-savior">Like the Savior</h2>

The Church’s study helps list ways Joseph’s life can remind a reader of Jesus Christ, several at points this page has passed: each was sold for the price of a slave (twenty pieces of silver, thirty), betrayed by a man named Judah, a savior to his people who gave them lifesaving bread, and willing to forgive those who repented.[@scripture-helps-gen-37-41] The notes on [Genesis 37](/genesis/37/) give the source’s words. The Bible itself does not present Joseph as a type in the way it does Israel’s going into and coming out of Egypt, though believing readers “of all centuries” have read his life as “a type of the life of Christ.”[@keil-genesis]
