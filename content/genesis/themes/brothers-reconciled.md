Genesis tells three stories of a brother who kills a brother or means to. Cain kills Abel. Esau says in his heart, “then will I slay my brother Jacob” (27:41). Joseph’s brothers plan to slay him and sell him instead (37:20, 28). The first story ends with Cain gone out from the presence of the LORD. The other two include an embrace, and the two embraces are told in the same words. Esau “fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept” (33:4); Joseph “fell upon his brother Benjamin’s neck, and wept,” and “kissed all his brethren” (45:14–15). Genesis has “fell on the neck” in these two meetings and in Joseph’s meeting with his father (46:29), and nowhere else.[@oshb-wlc] What differs between the reconciliations is not the embrace. It is what the brothers say. Cain, asked where his brother is, denies knowing. Esau and Jacob never speak of the stolen blessing; Jacob sends gifts to “cover his face.” Joseph’s brothers name the wrong three times: to one another, to the ruler they do not recognize, and at last to Joseph. Their last message puts the plea in their dead father’s mouth. Joseph also names the wrong: “whom ye sold into Egypt.” Among the three stories, only in theirs is the wrong confessed to the one wronged and forgiveness asked, and the book’s only “guilty” and its only “forgive” are spoken there. “Guilty” stands once in Genesis, at 42:21. “Forgive” stands only at 50:17, twice in one verse. The “trespass” the brothers ask Joseph to forgive is a word Genesis uses elsewhere only when Jacob demands of Laban, “What is my trespass?” (31:36).[@oshb-wlc]

This page follows the three stories by what the brothers say. It reads the third, the one the Church’s manuals set beside the Savior who “willingly forgave those who repented,” as the one in which the wrong is confessed and forgiveness asked.[@scripture-helps-gen-37-41] The word counts are the text’s; the comparison of the three stories is this page’s reading, and no commentary I read makes it. Elder Neal A. Maxwell, for one, pairs the second and third as alike: Esau’s generosity “could be linked to the later generosity of Joseph toward his brothers.”[@maxwell-teaching-opportunities]

## “Where is Abel thy brother?”

The Lord’s question gives Cain the first chance in scripture to name a wrong done to a brother. Andrew Skinner writes that “it seems the question was intended to elicit a confession of guilt,” and that Rashi judged the same.[@skinner-cain-abel] Cain answers, “I know not: Am I my brother’s keeper?” (4:9). Keil sets the answer against the garden: “Adam and Eve fear before God, and acknowledge their sin; Cain boldly denies it.”[@keil-genesis] The Cambridge commentary calls the reply “a statement which is a falsehood” and “a question which is defiance,” and adds that “The first words of the first murderer renounce the obligations of brotherhood.”[@ryle-genesis] The Student Manual notes that the Book of Moses shows the Lord warning Cain beforehand “that if he did not repent, he would rule over Satan.”[@ot-manual-gen-4-11]

When the sentence falls, Cain speaks of his burden: “My punishment is greater than I can bear” (4:13). The Hebrew word, *ʿāwōn*, means both guilt and its penalty, and the Church’s footnote offers the other sense: “My iniquity is too great to be forgiven.”[@lds-scriptures] The Cambridge commentary reads the cry as “not that of repentance for his sin, but of entreaty for the mitigation of his doom.”[@ryle-genesis] Keil agrees, and explains why the verse can be read either way: the phrase *ʿāwōn nāśāʾ* “signifies to take away and bear sin or guilt,” so that “God takes guilt away by forgiving it,” while “man carries it away and bears it, by enduring its punishment.”[@keil-genesis] That verb, *nāśāʾ*, “lift” or “carry,” is among the commonest in Genesis, and its root runs through all three stories. the noun translated “accepted” in Cain’s story comes from this root and means a lifting (4:7); Jacob hopes Esau “will accept of me,” literally “lift up my face” (32:20), and Ellicott, rendering it so, sends the reader back to 4:7; and Joseph’s brothers ask Joseph to “forgive,” to lift away, their trespass (50:17), the only place the King James Genesis has the word.[@oshb-wlc][@ellicott-genesis] Cain speaks of his guilt as a weight he must carry himself; Jacob hopes to have his face lifted by a gift; the brothers ask the one they wronged to lift away their trespass. Following the verb through the three stories is this page’s reading; no commentary I read joins the verses.

The Book of Moses gives Cain words Genesis does not. Cain says, “Satan tempted me because of my brother’s flocks. And I was wroth also; for his offering thou didst accept and not mine” (Moses 5:38), and he expects to be slain “because of mine iniquities” (5:39).[@lds-scriptures] Cain does speak of iniquities, but after “I am free” (5:33) and after the denial (5:34), and the account ends with the mark and with Cain “shut out from the presence of the Lord” (5:40–41). Mercy reaches him. The mark protects him, and the Cambridge commentary reads the “Therefore” of 4:15 as “on account of Cain’s entreaty, Jehovah’s mercy is shewn to the first murderer.”[@ryle-genesis] But nothing between the brothers is mended. Abel is dead, and “Cain went out from the presence of the LORD” (4:16).

## “I will appease him”

Esau’s story begins with the same intent. “Esau hated Jacob because of the blessing,” and “said in his heart … then will I slay my brother Jacob” (27:41). The verb for his hatred, *śāṭam*, is a rarer word than the ordinary “hate”; Genesis has it three times, here, in Jacob’s blessing of Joseph (49:23), and when Joseph’s brothers fear that “Joseph will peradventure hate us” (50:15).[@oshb-wlc] Rebekah’s hope is not that Jacob will confess but that Esau will “forget that which thou hast done to him” (27:45).

Twenty years later Jacob prays, “Deliver me, I pray thee, from the hand of my brother” (32:11), and sends a present ahead: “I will appease him with the present that goeth before me, and afterward I will see his face; peradventure he will accept of me” (32:20). The Hebrew of “appease” is *ʾăkapprâ pānāyw*, “I will cover his face.” The Cambridge commentary explains: “The present will so ‘cover his face,’ that Esau cannot look upon Jacob’s offence.”[@ryle-genesis] Ellicott adds that this covering of an offended face “became the usual legal word for making an atonement.”[@ellicott-genesis] The verb is *kipper*, and Genesis has it in this sense only here; its one other use is the pitch that covers Noah’s ark (6:14).[@oshb-wlc] The Bible Dictionary defines atonement as “the setting ‘at one’ of those who have been estranged,” and Matthew Bowen reads chapter 33 as Jacob’s “‘reconciliation’ or ‘at-one-ment’ with his estranged brother Esau.”[@bd-atonement][@bowen-wrestled-man] Keil reads Jacob’s sevenfold bow as the act of a man who “humbled himself before him as the elder, with the feeling that he had formerly sinned against him.”[@keil-genesis]

Then “Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept” (33:4). Neither brother speaks of the stolen blessing. The nearest the text comes is Jacob’s word for his gift, “Take, I pray thee, my blessing” (33:11), *birkātî*, the form of Esau’s own cry, “he hath taken away my blessing” (27:36).[@oshb-wlc] The Cambridge commentary reads the word as a gift, “the material side of the ‘blessing’”; the echo of 27:36 is this page’s observation, not a commentator’s.[@ryle-genesis] The Church’s chapter heading says the brothers “meet and are reconciled,” and *Come, Follow Me* asks, “What do you learn about forgiveness from Esau’s example?”[@lds-scriptures][@cfm-ot-2026-10] Elder Neal A. Maxwell said, “Notice how not only Jacob but Esau has grown.”[@maxwell-teaching-opportunities] The Cambridge commentary calls Esau’s conduct “that of a good-natured and forgiving disposition,” and Keil says Jacob “must have discerned the work of God in the unexpected change in his brother’s disposition towards him.”[@ryle-genesis][@keil-genesis]

The reconciliation is real, and Genesis gives it no word for the wrong and none for forgiveness. The offence is covered. Esau offers to travel on together (33:12); the brothers go their ways, Esau to Seir and Jacob to Succoth (33:16–17), and stand together again at their father’s grave, where “his sons Esau and Jacob buried him” (35:29).[@lds-scriptures]

## “We are verily guilty concerning our brother”

Joseph’s brothers say the wrong three times, and each time they say it closer to him.

The first time they say it to one another. Shut up three days in Egypt, they say, “We are verily guilty concerning our brother, in that we saw the anguish of his soul, when he besought us, and we would not hear” (42:21), and Reuben adds, “behold, also his blood is required” (42:22). The word “guilty,” *ʾāšēm*, stands nowhere else in Genesis.[@oshb-wlc] “They knew not that Joseph understood them” (42:23), and he “turned himself about from them, and wept” (42:24). Ellicott reads the memory as “a sign of the reality of their repentance,” and the tears as the sign that “There was no bitterness in Joseph’s heart, and at their first word of regret he melted.”[@ellicott-genesis] The Cambridge commentary hears “the vitality of conscience after a long interval of years.”[@ryle-genesis]

The second time Judah says it to the ruler he takes for an Egyptian. With the cup found in Benjamin’s sack, he asks, “how shall we clear ourselves? God hath found out the iniquity of thy servants” (44:16). The word is *ʿāwōn*, Cain’s word at 4:13.[@oshb-wlc] The Cambridge commentary reads it as confession: “Judah confesses the wrong-doing of himself and his brothers (Genesis 42:21).”[@ryle-genesis] Keil hears the same: God “is now punishing the crime committed against our brother.”[@keil-genesis] Then Judah offers himself, “let thy servant abide instead of the lad” (44:33). The Student Manual finds here “evidence of sincere repentance on the brothers’ part,” shown because Joseph’s demand for Benjamin “allowed them to demonstrate this repentance,” and *Scripture Helps* reads the tests as “opportunities to prove that they had changed.”[@ot-manual-gen-37-50][@scripture-helps-gen-42-50] The page [One in Another’s Place](/genesis/themes/in-anothers-place/) follows Judah’s offer.

The third time they say it knowing he is Joseph, and with the word Genesis has kept back. After Jacob’s burial they send a message that puts the plea in their father’s mouth, “Thy father did command before he died, saying” (50:16): “Forgive, I pray thee now, the trespass of thy brethren, and their sin; for they did unto thee evil: and now, we pray thee, forgive the trespass of the servants of the God of thy father” (50:17). “Forgive” is *nāśāʾ*, twice, the book’s only “forgive”; “trespass,” *pešaʿ*, twice, a word Genesis has otherwise only in Jacob’s protest to Laban (31:36).[@oshb-wlc] Keil defends the message: “There is no reason whatever for regarding the appeal to their father’s wish as a mere pretence.”[@keil-genesis]

Joseph names the wrong too. “I am Joseph your brother, whom ye sold into Egypt” (45:4), and at the end, “ye thought evil against me” (50:20). *Come, Follow Me* observes that he “helped them see divine purpose in his suffering.”[@cfm-ot-2026-12] When his forgiveness began is read two ways. John Gee writes that Joseph “does forgive his brothers, but not at first,” because “He needed to see fruits meet for repentance,” and Ellicott that he “melted” at their first word.[@gee-clothes-cups][@ellicott-genesis] The text gives his tears at 42:24, after he has heard them confess to one another and before any brother knows who he is, and his word at 45:5, “be not grieved, nor angry with yourselves,” before any brother asks.

## “Am I in the place of God?”

The brothers’ last fear carries Esau’s word. “Joseph will peradventure hate us, and will certainly requite us all the evil which we did unto him” (50:15); the verb is *śāṭam*, the verb of 27:41, and the Church’s footnote reads it “bears a grudge.”[@oshb-wlc][@lds-scriptures] The brothers fear that Joseph will be to them what Esau was to Jacob; the shared verb is the text’s, and the pairing is this page’s. The Cambridge commentary says only, “Their conscience cannot leave them alone,” and Elder Maxwell, “Of course, their fears were groundless.”[@ryle-genesis][@maxwell-teaching-opportunities]

“Joseph wept when they spake unto him” (50:17), and Keil reads the tears as grief that they “could impute anything so bad to him.”[@keil-genesis] His answer is a question: “Fear not: for am I in the place of God?” (50:19). The Cambridge commentary paraphrases, “am I the person to punish for wrongdoing? God alone knows the hearts,” and notes that Jacob asked Rachel the same words, “Am I in God’s stead” (30:2).[@ryle-genesis] Then the verse that gives the Church’s lesson its title: “ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good” (50:20). Ellicott: “The verb in the Heb. is the same, and contrasts man’s purpose with God’s purpose.”[@ellicott-genesis]

Genesis closes the story with “he comforted them, and spake kindly unto them” (50:21); the Hebrew of “spake kindly” is “spake to their heart,” and the note on [[Gen. 45:15]] follows the verb of speaking from the brothers who “could not speak peaceably unto him” (37:4) to the brothers who at last “talked with him.”[@oshb-wlc] Joseph never says “I forgive.” He says, “I will nourish you, and your little ones” (50:21).

## “They willingly forgave those who repented”

*Scripture Helps* sets the scene of 45:3–5 in its table of likenesses between Joseph and Jesus Christ: “They willingly forgave those who repented,” with Mosiah 26:30 beside it, “as often as my people repent will I forgive them their trespasses against me.”[@scripture-helps-gen-37-41][@lds-scriptures] The verse before it names the confession: “if he confess his sins before thee and me, and repenteth in the sincerity of his heart, him shall ye forgive” (Mosiah 26:29).[@lds-scriptures] The Seminary manual’s chart has the same pairing with a wider line, “Joseph forgave those who wronged him.”[@ot-seminary-2026-gen-42-45] The Student Manual’s section “Joseph—A Type of Christ” says of chapter 45 that “He forgave without bitterness, extended love when undeserved,” and *Come, Follow Me* says, “In many ways, Joseph was like Jesus Christ,” and that “Joseph’s example points us to the Savior, the true source of healing and reconciliation.”[@ot-manual-gen-37-50][@cfm-ot-2026-12] Keil warns against “typological trifling,” since Joseph is not represented “either by the prophets or by Christ and His apostles, as typical of Christ,” while allowing the reading that “believing commentators of all centuries” have made.[@keil-genesis] The Church’s manuals make it, and this page follows them.

The parable of the lost son has both embraces in it. The father “ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him” (Luke 15:20), the words of Esau running and of Joseph on Benjamin’s neck; Bowen sets the verse beside 33:4.[@lds-scriptures][@bowen-wrestled-man] Then the son speaks: “Father, I have sinned against heaven, and in thy sight” (Luke 15:21). The father’s embrace does not wait for the confession, and the confession is still spoken. Reading the parable’s two verses as the two halves of Genesis’s brother stories is this page’s; Bowen joins only verse 20 to Esau.

Both halves of the Church’s teaching on forgiveness stand in these stories. To the one wronged, the Lord says, “of you it is required to forgive all men” (D&C 64:10), and Elder Jeffrey R. Holland testifies that “forgiving and forsaking offenses, old or new, is central to the grandeur of the Atonement of Jesus Christ.”[@lds-scriptures][@holland-ministry-reconciliation] *Come, Follow Me* speaks of forgiving “whether or not they have asked for forgiveness.”[@cfm-ot-2026-12] Esau forgives a brother who never says what he did, and Joseph weeps at a confession the brothers did not know he heard, and tells them “be not grieved” before any of them asks. To the one who did the wrong, the Seminary resource manual quotes Elder Spencer W. Kimball: “Forgiveness and recovery are dependent upon the offender’s repentance, which begins with recognition of the sin and acceptance of personal responsibility for it.”[@ot-seminary-resource-gen-42-50] Joseph’s brothers say it three times, and ask. Cain, asked, denies it, and when he speaks of his iniquity it is of what he must bear. Genesis gives its only “guilty” and its only “forgive” to the brothers who spoke, and the commandment that requires forgiveness of all men begins, “I, the Lord, will forgive whom I will forgive” (D&C 64:10).[@lds-scriptures]
