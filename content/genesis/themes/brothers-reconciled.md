Genesis tells of three pairs of brothers at odds. Cain kills Abel, and nothing is mended. Esau means to kill Jacob and, twenty years later, runs to embrace him. Joseph’s brothers sell him, and he feeds them, weeps on their necks, and says of the sale, “God did send me before you to preserve life.” What changes is how the wrong is met: murder; a meeting in which no wrong is named; and a reconciliation in which it is named, with God’s purpose beside it.

## Cain and Abel

“Cain rose up against Abel his brother, and slew him.” Asked where Abel is, he answers, “Am I my brother’s keeper?” The Lord replies, “the voice of thy brother’s blood crieth unto me from the ground” ([[Gen. 4:8–10]]). The story ends with Cain going “out from the presence of the LORD” ([[Gen. 4:16]]). There is no reconciliation. The [chapter 4 notes](/genesis/4/) carry the story.

## Esau and Jacob

After the blessing, Esau says, “then will I slay my brother Jacob” ([[Gen. 27:41]]). Jacob, who has spent “twenty years” in Laban’s house ([[Gen. 31:41]]), prays, “Deliver me, I pray thee, from the hand of my brother” ([[Gen. 32:11]]). Then “Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept” ([[Gen. 33:4]]). Jacob says, “I have seen thy face, as though I had seen the face of God” ([[Gen. 33:10]]). As told, the meeting does not name the old wrong.[@lds-scriptures] Esau’s generosity has been linked to Joseph’s later generosity toward his brothers.[@maxwell-teaching-opportunities] See [Genesis 33](/genesis/33/).

## Hated and sold

Joseph’s brothers “hated him, and could not speak peaceably unto him” ([[Gen. 37:4]]). They cast him into a pit and sell him “for twenty pieces of silver” ([[Gen. 37:24]], [[Gen. 37:28]]). Judah asks, “What profit is it if we slay our brother, and conceal his blood?” ([[Gen. 37:26]]). Set beside Cain’s “thy brother’s blood” ([[Gen. 4:10]]), the Hebrew word for “blood” (*dam*) and the word for “brother” stand together in one verse in Genesis only at 4:10–11, 9:5 and 37:26.[@oshb-wlc] Two common words do not make a retelling, and nothing in the text says Judah has Cain in view. See [Genesis 37](/genesis/37/).

## Guilt found out

In Egypt, where “there was bread” ([[Gen. 41:54]]), they “bowed down themselves before him” ([[Gen. 42:6]]). In prison they say, “We are verily guilty concerning our brother” ([[Gen. 42:21]]). Later Judah says, “God hath found out the iniquity of thy servants” ([[Gen. 44:16]]; the note on that verse weighs which guilt he means). Reuben’s remorse and Judah’s offer to take Benjamin’s punishment are read as signs that the brothers had changed.[@scripture-helps-gen-42-50] The page [One in Another’s Place](/genesis/themes/in-anothers-place/) follows that offer.

<h2 id="sold-sent">Sold, and sent</h2>

Joseph says, “I am Joseph your brother, whom ye sold into Egypt.” Then: “be not grieved, nor angry with yourselves, that ye sold me hither: for God did send me before you to preserve life” ([[Gen. 45:4–5]]). Verse 8 adds, “it was not you that sent me hither, but God.” Joseph names the sale twice before he names God. His words do not mean that God caused the brothers to sell him; they recognize that God knows all things and can bring good out of what was done.[@scripture-helps-gen-42-50] In Genesis the Hebrew verbs for “sold” (*makar*, 10 times) and “sent” (*shalach*, 66 times) stand in one verse only at 45:5.[@oshb-wlc] A psalm puts the same two verbs in one line: “He sent a man before them, even Joseph, who was sold for a servant” ([[Ps. 105:17]]).[@oshb-wlc] See [Genesis 45](/genesis/45/).

## Brothers again

Joseph “kissed all his brethren, and wept upon them: and after that his brethren talked with him” ([[Gen. 45:15]]). Set beside “could not speak peaceably unto him” ([[Gen. 37:4]]), the verb for speaking is the same, *dabar*.[@oshb-wlc] It is the ordinary Hebrew verb for speaking (73 times in Genesis; Joseph “spake roughly” with it at [[Gen. 42:7]]), so the word alone proves little.

Set beside Esau’s meeting ([[Gen. 33:4]]): Joseph “fell upon his brother Benjamin’s neck, and wept” ([[Gen. 45:14]]), then kisses his brothers. The Hebrew words for falling on the neck, kissing and weeping are the same, and Genesis has all three together only in these two meetings; Joseph also falls on his father’s neck and weeps ([[Gen. 46:29]]).[@oshb-wlc]

When Jacob dies the brothers fear again: “Joseph will peradventure hate us” ([[Gen. 50:15]]). Joseph answers, “ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive” ([[Gen. 50:20]]). “Thought” and “meant” are one Hebrew verb, *chashav*.[@oshb-wlc] He “comforted them, and spake kindly unto them” ([[Gen. 50:21]]).

<h2 id="like-the-savior">Like the Savior</h2>

The Church’s study helps list ways Joseph’s life can remind a reader of Jesus Christ: each was sold for the price of a slave (twenty pieces of silver, thirty), a savior to his people who gave them lifesaving bread, and willing to forgive those who repented.[@scripture-helps-gen-37-41] The Bible itself does not present Joseph as a type in the way it does Israel’s going into and coming out of Egypt, though believing readers “of all centuries” have read his life as “a type of the life of Christ.”[@keil-genesis]
