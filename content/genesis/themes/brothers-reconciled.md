Three times in Genesis a brother kills a brother or means to. Cain kills Abel, and nothing is mended. Esau means to kill Jacob and, twenty years later, runs to embrace him. Joseph’s brothers mean to kill him and sell him instead; years later he weeps over them and keeps them alive. Set side by side, the three end differently: in blood; in an embrace where the old wrong goes unspoken; and in a reunion where the wronged brother names the wrong and sets God’s purpose beside it.

## Cain and Abel

“Cain rose up against Abel his brother, and slew him.” Asked where Abel is, he answers, “Am I my brother’s keeper?” The Lord replies, “the voice of thy brother’s blood crieth unto me from the ground” ([[Gen. 4:8–10]]). Cain goes “out from the presence of the LORD” ([[Gen. 4:16]]). There is no reconciliation. See [Genesis 4](/genesis/4/).

## Esau and Jacob

After the blessing, Esau says, “then will I slay my brother Jacob” ([[Gen. 27:41]]). Twenty years later ([[Gen. 31:41]]) Jacob prays, “Deliver me, I pray thee, from the hand of my brother” ([[Gen. 32:11]]). Then “Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept” ([[Gen. 33:4]]). Neither brother speaks of the blessing Jacob took; Jacob urges on Esau the present he had sent to “appease him” ([[Gen. 32:20]]; [[Gen. 33:11]]).[@lds-scriptures] An apostle suggested that this generosity “could be linked” to Joseph’s later generosity toward his brothers.[@maxwell-teaching-opportunities] See [Genesis 33](/genesis/33/).

## Hated and sold

Joseph’s brothers “hated him, and could not speak peaceably unto him” ([[Gen. 37:4]]). They sell him “for twenty pieces of silver” ([[Gen. 37:28]]). Judah asks, “What profit is it if we slay our brother, and conceal his blood?” ([[Gen. 37:26]]). Set his words beside the Lord’s to Cain, “thy brother’s blood” ([[Gen. 4:10]]): the Hebrew words for “slay,” “brother” and “blood” are those of chapter 4, and here the brother is not killed.[@oshb-wlc] The words are common, and nothing in the text says Judah has Cain in view. See [Genesis 37](/genesis/37/).

## Guilt found out

In Egypt, before Joseph and not knowing he understands them, they say, “We are verily guilty concerning our brother” ([[Gen. 42:21]]), and Reuben adds, “behold, also his blood is required” ([[Gen. 42:22]]; see the note on that verse). Later Judah says, “God hath found out the iniquity of thy servants” ([[Gen. 44:16]]; the note on that verse weighs which guilt he means). Reuben’s remorse and Judah’s offer to take Benjamin’s punishment are read as signs that the brothers had changed.[@scripture-helps-gen-42-50] The page [One in Another’s Place](/genesis/themes/in-anothers-place/) follows that offer.

<h2 id="sold-sent">Sold, and sent</h2>

Joseph says, “I am Joseph your brother, whom ye sold into Egypt.” Then: “be not grieved, nor angry with yourselves, that ye sold me hither: for God did send me before you to preserve life” ([[Gen. 45:4–5]]). Verse 8 adds, “it was not you that sent me hither, but God.” His words do not mean that God caused the brothers to sell him; they recognize that God knows all things and can bring good out of what was done.[@scripture-helps-gen-42-50] A psalm keeps both the selling and the sending: “He sent a man before them, even Joseph, who was sold for a servant” ([[Ps. 105:17]]).[@lds-scriptures] See [Genesis 45](/genesis/45/).

## Brothers again

Joseph “kissed all his brethren, and wept upon them: and after that his brethren talked with him” ([[Gen. 45:15]]). Set beside “could not speak peaceably unto him” ([[Gen. 37:4]]), the verb for speaking is the same ordinary one, *dabar*;[@oshb-wlc] see the note on [[Gen. 45:15]].

Set this beside Esau’s meeting ([[Gen. 33:4]]): Joseph “fell upon his brother Benjamin’s neck, and wept” ([[Gen. 45:14]]), then kisses his brothers. The Hebrew words for falling on the neck, kissing and weeping are the same, and Genesis has all three together only in these two meetings; Joseph also falls on his father’s neck and weeps ([[Gen. 46:29]]), and on his dead father’s face, weeping and kissing him ([[Gen. 50:1]]).[@oshb-wlc]

When Jacob dies the brothers fear again: “Joseph will peradventure hate us” ([[Gen. 50:15]]). Joseph answers, “ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive” ([[Gen. 50:20]]). “Thought” and “meant” are one Hebrew verb, *chashav*.[@oshb-wlc] “I will nourish you, and your little ones. And he comforted them, and spake kindly unto them” ([[Gen. 50:21]]). See [Genesis 50](/genesis/50/).

<h2 id="like-the-savior">Joseph and the Savior</h2>

Joseph’s life can remind a reader of Jesus Christ: each was willing to forgive those who repented, was a savior to his people who gave them lifesaving bread, and was sold for the price of a slave.[@scripture-helps-gen-37-41] The Bible itself does not present Joseph as a type in the way it does Israel’s going into and coming out of Egypt, though believing commentators “of all centuries” have read his life as “a type of the life of Christ.”[@keil-genesis]
