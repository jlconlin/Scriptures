Twice in Genesis a living thing is offered in another’s place so that the other may go free. On Moriah, with Isaac bound on the altar, Abraham sees “a ram caught in a thicket by his horns: and Abraham went and took the ram, and offered him up for a burnt offering in the stead of his son” ([[Gen. 22:13]]). In Egypt, with Benjamin caught with the cup, Judah says to the lord of the land, “let thy servant abide instead of the lad a bondman to my lord; and let the lad go up with his brethren” ([[Gen. 44:33]]). “In the stead of” and “instead of” are the same Hebrew word, *taḥat*.[@oshb-wlc] It is a common word; it means “under” more often than anything else, and Genesis uses it thirty-one times.[@oshb-wlc] Set aside the ones that mean “under,” and what is left is Seth given “instead of Abel,” seven Edomite kings who each “reigned in his stead,” the ram, and Judah.[@oshb-wlc] In only the last two does the one put in another’s place take on what the other faced, death on the altar or bondage in Egypt, so that the other goes free. That is the fact this essay builds on.

Set the two scenes side by side and they are opposites. On Moriah the substitute is provided, and by God: Abraham did not bring the ram from his flocks; he looked up and it was there.[@hopkin-covenant] The ram chooses nothing, and it dies. In Egypt the substitute is a man, and he provides himself: no one asks Judah to stay, and no one caught him in a thicket. He offers himself willingly, and he does not die; his offer is never taken, because the lord of the land weeps and says, “I am Joseph” ([[Gen. 45:3]]). The ram is substituted without choosing; Judah chooses to be substituted and is never taken. Genesis does not give us both in one figure.

My argument is that the two pictures are best read together, with the Savior in the gap between them. He is the Lamb God provided, which is how Church teaching reads Moriah; and He is the one who offered Himself in another’s place, which is how the Church’s current study help reads Judah. What neither Genesis figure could be, He is: a willing substitute whom God provided, and whose offer was accepted. I will also argue that it matters that Judah is the one who makes the offer. The man who offers himself in another’s place is the brother who once said, “let us sell him,” and the Church’s study help sets the two sentences side by side: Judah “was the one who suggested selling Joseph into slavery. But many years later in Egypt, Judah offered to take Benjamin’s place as a servant or slave.”[@scripture-helps-gen-42-50] The Messiah’s line runs through him. The comparison of Moriah with Egypt is my own; no source I have read sets the two verses together, and I say below where each step rests on a source and where it rests on the text alone.

## In the stead of his son

The site’s chapter on [Genesis 22](/genesis/22/) explains Isaac’s offering, and this essay does not repeat it. Three things about the ram matter here. First, it was provided. Isaac had asked where the lamb was, and Abraham had answered, “God will provide himself a lamb for a burnt offering” ([[Gen. 22:8]]). Shon Hopkin points out what is easy to miss: “God, in the end, does not require Abraham to offer an animal sacrifice from his own herds at all. In this case, the offering God himself provided was that of a ram in the place of Isaac.”[@hopkin-covenant] Keil says the same: “as an offering provided by God Himself, he sacrificed it instead of his son.”[@keil-genesis] Second, the ram did nothing. It was caught, it was taken, it was offered; every verb belongs to someone else. Third, it died in Isaac’s place, and Isaac lived.

The Church reads this scene of Christ in two ways, and Elder Oaks gives both in one talk, a paragraph apart. The first is the one the Book of Mormon gives: Abraham’s offering of Isaac “is a similitude of God and his Only Begotten Son” ([[Jacob 4:5]]), “what the scriptures call a type,” as Elder Oaks says.[@oaks-1992-bible-stories] On that reading Isaac is the Son, and the ram is what the Son did not get. “There was no ram in the thicket at Calvary to spare Him,” Elder Maxwell said, “this Friend of Abraham and Isaac.”[@maxwell-1981-divine-redeemer] The second is the one Elder Oaks adds next, in which Isaac is us: “Because of our sins and our mortality, we, like Isaac, are condemned to death. When all other hope is gone, our Father in Heaven provides the Lamb of God, and we are saved by his sacrifice.”[@oaks-1992-bible-stories] *Scripture Helps* sets Abraham’s promise, “God would provide a lamb,” beside “Jesus was the Lamb offered up for us.”[@scripture-helps-gen-18-23] This essay needs the second reading, and it keeps the first in view: the Son who had no substitute is the substitute God provided for everyone else. The one picture Moriah gives of Him is the provided one. The ram did not offer itself.

## Let us sell him

Judah enters the story of Joseph with a proposal. His brothers mean to kill Joseph; Judah says, “What profit is it if we slay our brother, and conceal his blood? Come, and let us sell him to the Ishmeelites, and let not our hand be upon him; for he is our brother and our flesh” ([[Gen. 37:26–27]]). The site’s note on that verse weighs his motives. For this essay the plain fact is enough: Judah is the brother who turned a brother into merchandise, and Genesis never lets the reader forget it. The Church’s own materials remember it the hard way. The Student Manual, listing how Joseph’s life prefigures Christ’s, writes, “Judah, the head of the tribe of Judah, proposed the sale of Joseph… Judas (the Greek spelling of Judah) was the one who actually sold Jesus,”[@ot-manual-gen-37-50] and *Scripture Helps* puts Judah on the same side of its chart: Joseph and Jesus “were betrayed by men named Judah.”[@scripture-helps-gen-37-41] So when the Church’s newest study help says of chapter 44 that Judah’s actions “can remind us of Jesus Christ,”[@scripture-helps-gen-42-50] it is saying it of a man whose name it has already set beside Judas’s. That is not a contradiction. It is the arc.

## She hath been more righteous than I

What happened to Judah between the sale and the offer is a story Genesis tells and does not explain. He leaves his brothers, marries a Canaanite, loses two sons, and withholds the third from his widowed daughter-in-law Tamar. She disguises herself, and he hands her his signet, cord and staff as a “pledge” for a goat he has not yet sent ([[Gen. 38:18]]). When she is found with child and brought out to be burned, she sends the tokens back with the words, “Discern, I pray thee, whose are these” ([[Gen. 38:25]]). Scripture Central notes that the Hebrew behind “discern, I pray thee” is the phrase the brothers used when they held up the bloodied coat to Jacob, “know now whether it be thy son’s coat,” and that “these are the only two places this phrase appears in the Hebrew Bible.”[@sc-knowhy-841] The deceiver is deceived with his own words, and he says, “She hath been more righteous than I” ([[Gen. 38:26]]). The site’s chapter on [Genesis 38](/genesis/38/) has the rest.

Two sources draw the line from this chapter to the next ones, and I take it from them. Camille Fronk Olson: “Judah, the betrayer, offered his own life to redeem his victim’s successor. The only story included in the Genesis narrative to possibly explain Judah’s transformation is the story of Tamar.”[@olson-matriarchs] Ben Spackman notices the pledge: “Judah progresses from selfishness (offering tokens of himself as a pledge to pay a prostitute) to selflessness and redemption in the Joseph story (offering himself as a pledge for the good of his family, redeeming Benjamin and sparing Israel pain).”[@spackman-judah-tamar] The Hebrew bears him out, and this observation is the site’s rather than his: the “pledge” Judah left with Tamar, *ʿērābôn*, shares its root with the verb he will use of himself in Egypt, “I will be surety,” *ʿārab*, and Genesis has the noun only in this chapter and the verb only in chapters 43 and 44.[@oshb-wlc] Scripture Central is careful to add that “it is impossible to know for sure how to understand Judah’s actions,”[@sc-knowhy-841] and I am as careful. Genesis shows a man who left tokens of himself and later offered himself. It does not say what changed him.

## I will be surety for him

The famine sends the brothers back to Egypt, but Jacob will not let Benjamin go. Reuben has already tried: “Slay my two sons, if I bring him not to thee” ([[Gen. 42:37]]). Judah offers something else. “I will be surety for him; of my hand shalt thou require him: if I bring him not unto thee, and set him before thee, then let me bear the blame for ever” ([[Gen. 43:9]]). The Cambridge commentary marks the difference: “In Genesis 42:37 Reuben had been ready to pledge the lives of his two sons for Benjamin’s safety. Here Judah is ready to pledge his own life.”[@ryle-genesis] The verb is the lexicon’s word for going “surety for the safety of” a person, and it occurs in Genesis only in these two verses, both with a person as object.[@bdb] And “let me bear the blame” is one Hebrew verb, the verb for sin: “I shall have sinned against thee for ever.”[@ryle-genesis] Judah stakes himself rather than his sons: his life, and his standing before his father for the rest of it.

The Student Manual sees the shape of the whole: “It is significant that Judah, who suggested that Joseph be sold (see Genesis 37:26–27), became the one who was willing to become ‘the surety’ for Benjamin… When the pressure was on, Judah’s change of heart was shown to be complete (see Genesis 44:33).”[@ot-manual-gen-37-50]

## Instead of the lad

The pressure comes in chapter 44. Joseph’s cup is found in Benjamin’s sack, and Joseph names the penalty: “the man in whose hand the cup is found, he shall be my servant; and as for you, get you up in peace unto your father” ([[Gen. 44:17]]). It is the test of chapter 37 set again, as the site’s [Genesis 44](/genesis/44/) shows: a favored son of Rachel in their power, and a way home without him. Judah speaks, and his speech ends here:

> Now therefore, I pray thee, let thy servant abide instead of the lad a bondman to my lord; and let the lad go up with his brethren. For how shall I go up to my father, and the lad be not with me? ([[Gen. 44:33–34]])

Three things in the Hebrew. “Instead of” is *taḥat*, the word of Moriah.[@oshb-wlc] “A bondman” is *ʿebed*, the word Joseph has just used for the penalty ([[Gen. 44:17]]).[@oshb-wlc] Joseph was sold out of the pit and bought into Egypt ([[Gen. 37:28]]; [[Gen. 39:1]]); Judah now asks to stay in Egypt as a bondman. He is offering to become what his proposal made Joseph. No source I have read says that, and the word can mean a servant as well as a slave, but the shape is in the story. The Greek has *anti* for both “instead of”s, as it does wherever *taḥat* means “in place of.”[@swete-lxx-genesis] The Cambridge commentary calls the offer “the noble climax” of the speech and names what it is: Judah would remain “as substitute for Benjamin.”[@ryle-genesis]

Set beside Moriah, every term is reversed. There God provided; here a man offers. There the substitute had no will; here the will is the whole of it. There the substitute died; here he is not taken. Joseph, who has heard his own story told to his face, “could not refrain himself” ([[Gen. 45:1]]), and the offer is answered not by acceptance but by a revelation: “I am Joseph your brother, whom ye sold into Egypt” ([[Gen. 45:4]]). Judah is never a bondman. His offer remains an offer; he never has to serve in Benjamin’s place.

I read it beside two refusals. Twice in this book a man is treated as if he stood in God’s place, and twice he answers with the same words. Jacob to Rachel: “Am I in God’s stead?” ([[Gen. 30:2]]). Joseph to these same brothers, when they fear his revenge: “am I in the place of God?” ([[Gen. 50:19]]).[@oshb-wlc] Jacob and Joseph refuse God’s place; Judah takes a man’s. The pairing is mine. But it says where the limit of a human substitute lies. A man may offer to stand where another man stands. No man in Genesis will stand where God stands, and no man in Genesis is both the one who offers and the one who is provided.

## The one who is both

Here the Church’s study help makes the step this essay has been walking toward. “Judah’s actions can remind us of Jesus Christ, who was Judah’s descendant. As Judah offered to take Benjamin’s punishment so that Benjamin could return to his father, so Jesus Christ took our sins upon Himself so that we can return to our Heavenly Father.”[@scripture-helps-gen-42-50] The passage it sets beside the verses is Abinadi’s: the Son, “standing betwixt them and justice; having broken the bands of death, taken upon himself their iniquity and their transgressions, having redeemed them, and satisfied the demands of justice” ([[Mosiah 15:9]]).[@scripture-helps-gen-42-50] No Church source I have read calls Judah a type of Christ; the Student Manual gives that name to Joseph, and *Scripture Helps* sets Judah’s name beside Judas’s in the same chapters.[@ot-manual-gen-37-50][@scripture-helps-gen-37-41] I keep to that. What Judah gives the reader is not a portrait but one half of a picture.

The other half is Moriah’s, and the two halves meet in the Savior. He is what the ram was: provided by God, not brought from any man’s flock. “Our Father in Heaven provides the Lamb of God,” in Elder Oaks’s words, and we, like Isaac, go free.[@oaks-1992-bible-stories] And He did what Judah offered to do: He stayed in another’s place so that the other could go home to his father. “I lay down my life for the sheep… No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself” ([[John 10:15]]; [[John 10:18]]). The ram could not say that. Judah said it and was not taken. The Savior said it and was. He is the willing substitute whom God provided, and whose offer was accepted at the full price, which is the thing Genesis shows in two pieces and nowhere in one. That is the connection I am making, and no source I have read makes it; the Church’s footnotes join Judah’s verse to nothing outside its own chapters, and the commentaries that call each scene a substitution do not cross-refer.

One more word belongs here, with a caution. Hebrews says, “By so much was Jesus made a surety of a better testament” ([[Heb. 7:22]]), eight verses after saying “our Lord sprang out of Juda” ([[Heb. 7:14]]). The Student Manual explains the word: “‘surety’ in Hebrews 7:22 refers to one who guarantees another person’s financial debt… Through taking upon Himself our sins and giving His life, Christ paid our spiritual debt.”[@nt-manual-hebrews-7-13] Judah’s surety was for a person’s safety rather than a debt, but the office is the same, one man answering with himself for another, and the King James translators used the same English word for both. But the Greek of Hebrews is not the word the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, used for Judah’s pledge, and no commentary I have found joins the two verses.[@swete-lxx-genesis] The join is by idea, not by word, and I offer it as that: the office Judah took for one brother, Hebrews gives to the Lord who came of Judah’s tribe, for all.

## Until Shiloh come

Genesis closes Judah’s story with a blessing. Jacob passes over Reuben, Simeon and Levi, and then: “Judah, thou art he whom thy brethren shall praise… The sceptre shall not depart from Judah, nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come” ([[Gen. 49:8]]; [[Gen. 49:10]]). The Church reads Shiloh as the Messiah, “confirmed by JST Gen. 50:24,” a name that “can be read as a short form for ‘He to whom it belongs.’”[@bd-shiloh] Genesis does not say the blessing answers the offer. Keil, alone among the commentators I have read, ties them, and he reads the sale more kindly than I do: “Judah had already shown to a certain extent a strong and noble character, when he proposed to sell Joseph rather than shed his blood (Genesis 37:26.); but still more in the manner in which he offered himself to his father as a pledge for Benjamin, and pleaded with Joseph on his behalf.”[@keil-genesis] That is one reading, and the site’s [Genesis 49](/genesis/49/) gives the others. What the text does say is this: the line of the Messiah goes through the brother who proposed the sale and then offered himself for one, and through Tamar ([[Matt. 1:3]]). Genesis did not give the offer of oneself to Joseph, the brother the Church calls the type. It gave it to Judah, the brother who had to learn it.

## In another’s place

What this leaves a disciple is two places to stand, and the Savior in both. The first is Isaac’s, on Elder Oaks’s reading: bound, condemned, and set free by a substitute we did not choose and could not have provided. Isaac yields to the will of God, as the Church’s heading to the chapter says, and then he goes free by another’s death.[@lds-scriptures] That is the first aspect of the Atonement these two scenes picture, and Genesis puts it first.

The second is Judah’s. Having been set free, a disciple finds himself before a judge with a brother in trouble, and he knows what he once did to a brother. No one asks him to be the ram; that place is taken. What the Savior asks is in His own words: “This is my commandment, That ye love one another, as I have loved you. Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends” ([[John 15:12–13]]). “Let thy servant abide instead of the lad” is what that love sounds like in a man who has learned it late, and Genesis honors it without pretending it is the whole thing. Judah’s offer was not taken, because Joseph was alive and merciful; the disciple’s is still asked, by the one who has already stood in his place. The brother who said “let us sell him” became the brother who said “let me stay,” and the Lord who provided the ram came, in the flesh, from him.
