Jeremiah's brief (`content/jeremiah/BRIEF.md`) is the model, and its section “What a chapter is for” holds here. Obadiah's brief (`content/obadiah/BRIEF.md`) is the nearest in kind, another short oracle against a nation; Jonah's (`content/jonah/BRIEF.md`) is the counterpart, the same city spared a century earlier.

## What a chapter is for here

Nahum is 47 verses in three chapters, a single oracle against one city, in some of the most vivid poetry of the prophets. A reader comes to it from Jonah and asks: *Who was Nahum, and where was Elkosh? Is this the Nineveh that repented? Why would a prophet rejoice over a city's ruin, and what is a faithful reader to make of “the LORD revengeth” (1:2)? How can God be jealous? How does “slow to anger” fit beside “will not at all acquit”? Who is the “wicked counsellor” (1:11)? Why does 1:15 sound like Isaiah, and who has the beautiful feet? Who is “he that dasheth in pieces” (2:1)? What are the river gates, and who is Huzzab? Why lions? What is “No,” and why does it matter to Nineveh? Why the harlot and the shaming (3:4–7)? Did it happen as he said? Why does the book end with clapping?* A chapter answers those from the text first.

**Say what the oracle meant to Judah before what the chapter headings read in it.** The headings read Nineveh's fall as “a symbol of what will be in the latter days” (chapter 2) and chapter 1 of “the burning of the earth at the Second Coming”; *Scripture Helps* says the prophet “used Nineveh as a symbol of sinful, oppressive, worldly powers that the Lord will ultimately bring down.” The chapters give that reading, and give it as the Church's; each note first says what the words meant to a Judahite under Assyria's yoke. Give the picture, then the reading, and keep them apart.

**Read it as poetry.** Rasmussen: “Nahum was a poet.” Three poems, one to a chapter (Rasmussen; Keil: “three strophes, answering to the division into chapters”): the hymn and oracle of chapter 1, the “taunt-song” of the siege in chapter 2, the woe of chapter 3. Each thread follows its poem's movement; the notes explain the images by what they meant.

**Jonah is the other half of Nineveh's story; link it, don't retell it.** *Scripture Helps*: “The people of Nineveh in Nahum's day were not the same people who had repented of their sins after Jonah preached in Nineveh more than a century earlier.” The site has Nineveh and Assyria's cruelty (`[[Jonah 1:2]]`), the Lord who relents (`[[Jonah 3:10]]`, `[[Jonah 4:2]]`) and Nineveh's later fall (Jonah 3's setting). Chapter 1's setting says in a sentence or two that the city spared in Jonah is the city judged here, and links. Chapter 3's `christ` or thread may set the two books' last verses side by side: both end on a question about Nineveh (`[[Jonah 4:11]]`; 3:19).

## Divisions

**Proposed: three divisions, one for each chapter** (`book.yaml`), because the three poems are the chapters (Rasmussen; Keil). Chapter 1 (`nahumjealous`): the hymn to the jealous and patient Lord (1:2–8 or 1:2–10) and the oracle that turns between Nineveh and Judah (1:9–15). Chapter 2 (`nahumsiege`): the siege, the fall, the plunder and the lions' den. Chapter 3 (`nahumwoe`): the woe on “the bloody city,” Thebes, the useless defenses, the epitaph. The Bible Dictionary describes the three chapters the same way. **The Hebrew's chapters differ**: King James 1:15 is Hebrew 2:1, and King James 2:1–13 is Hebrew 2:2–14 (`hebrew.mjs` prints both). The King James break is kept; chapter 1's thread may say that 1:15 also opens the next poem.

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, which scripture takes up which line) is the coordinator's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **When.** The book gives no king. It speaks of Thebes' fall as past (3:8–10) and Nineveh's as to come. The Bible Dictionary: “probably written between the fall of Nineveh, 606 B.C., and that of No-amon (Nahum 3:8) or Thebes, in Upper Egypt, taken by Assurbanipal about 660 B.C.” *Scripture Helps*: “likely date to the period just before Nineveh's fall in 612 BC.” Sperry, in the Student Manual (20-2): Thebes fell “in 663 B.C.,” so “between the years 663 B.C. and 612 B.C.” The Guide: “sometime between 642 and 606 B.C.” **Proposed: every chapter's `when` is “After Assyria sacked Egyptian Thebes (about 660 B.C.) and before Nineveh fell (612 B.C.), probably near the end of that time,”** with rows from the Bible Dictionary and *Scripture Helps*. **Nineveh fell in 612**, the date of *Scripture Helps* and Van Dyke and Ogden; the Bible Dictionary's 606 is older: don't print 606. Keil's date, under Hezekiah just after 701, cannot stand beside Thebes' fall and is not used. `book.yaml`'s period is `babylon` (`CONTEXT.md` puts Nahum there).
- **Who.** “Nahum the Elkoshite” (1:1) is all the book says. The name means “consoler” or “comforter” (Bible Dictionary: “Consoler”; Ball: “derived from the Hebrew root nâcham, which means to pity, to be sorry, to comfort, or to be comforted”; BDB 5151, “from H5162”). Elkosh: the Bible Dictionary, “native of Elkosh in Galilee”; the Guide, “An Old Testament prophet of Galilee.” Keil follows Jerome's Galilee and rejects the Assyrian Alkush's “pretended tomb” and the Capernaum (“village of Nahum”) guess, which Breitenstein repeats: **neither is used**. There is no Bible Dictionary entry “Elkosh” (the address returns the index). One note, at 1:1.
- **“The burden of Nineveh” (1:1).** *Massāʾ* (`hebrew.mjs nahum 1:1`, H4853), “burden,” an oracle. The site's note “What is a ‘burden’?” at `[[Isa. 13:1]]` explains it: link. “Vision” (*ḥāzôn*): the Obadiah note on `[[Obad. 1:1]]` mentions that the word heads Nahum: link.
- **The name and the book's comfort** (see the contested question below). *Nāḥam* is the verb of Jonah's God who “repented” of the evil (`[[Jonah 3:9–10]]`, `[[Jonah 4:2]]`; `hebrew.mjs jonah --word 5162`), and the participle “comforters” at 3:7, “whence shall I seek comforters for thee?” (*mᵉnaḥămîm*, H5162). A Language note at 3:7 may give the wordplay, the comforter's book finding no comforter for Nineveh, **only if a source read makes it**; Ball gives the root, the lexicon the word, and a writer must not draw the irony from memory.
- **Nineveh and Assyria.** Explained on the site: Nineveh at `[[Jonah 1:2]]` and `[[Gen. 10:11]]`; Assyria's empire in the guides [Two Kingdoms and Assyria](/old-testament/guides/two-kingdoms-and-assyria/#nations) and [Babylon and the Exile](/old-testament/guides/babylon-and-the-exile/#rulers). Van Dyke and Ogden give the last years: Nabopolassar's revolt (626), Asshur taken (614), and Nineveh two years later, besieged “for three months” and “razed … to the ground.” The Medes are explained at `[[Isa. 13:17]]` and `[[Jer. 51:11]]`. Chapter 2's setting says once who took the city, and links; no chapter names the besiegers in a note where the text does not (2:1 says only “He that dasheth in pieces”; Breitenstein reads the scarlet shields of 2:3 as the Babylonians': give it as a reading).
- **Assyria's own language turned back on it.** Uriona (*Interpreter*, 2023), citing Gordon Johnston, says the “yoke” is “one of the most common Assyrian metaphors” for its rule, “distinctly Assyrian” (the source for 1:13, “I break his yoke from off thee”), and that “Nahum also subverts the ‘flood’ trope in Nahum 1:8.” KnoWhy #718 reads “the faces of them all gather blackness” (2:10) as a “subversive reversal” of a curse; the Church's footnote: “Hebrew idiom meaning ‘gloom.’” Give the footnote's sense first; the reversal, if used, as the KnoWhy's reading. The lion (2:11–12) was a royal Assyrian image: say so only from a source read (the Student Manual reads the lions as Nineveh's royalty, 20-6).
- **Thebes (3:8–10).** “Populous No” is No-amon, Thebes (the Church's footnote: “HEB No-amon; i.e., Thebes”; `bd-no`: “Capital of Upper Egypt, taken by Assurbanipal about 660 B.C.”). Explained already at `[[Jer. 46:25]]` (“No, the city of Amon”), `[[Ezek. 30:13]]`, and `[[Isa. 20:4]]`, which names Nahum: link. The note at 3:8 says what Nahum adds: the argument (a city ringed by water and backed by Egypt and Cush fell; so will Nineveh) and the date it gives the book. Put and Lubim: the footnotes (“IE the Libyans”).
- **Names of God.** “God,” “the LORD,” “the LORD of hosts” (2:13; 3:5); copy every phrase from `data/kjv/nahum.json`. The footnote at 1:3 sends “slow to anger” to the Topical Guide's “Forbear.”
- **Other books on the site.** Link, and don't say who borrowed from whom except as a source read says. 1:2 and `[[Ex. 20:5]]` (the note “What jealous means when it is said of God”), `[[Ex. 34:14]]`, `[[Deut. 32:35]]` (“Vengeance belongs to the LORD”); 1:3 and `[[Ex. 34:6–7]]` (the footnote gives Ex. 34:7; the site's notes on `[[Joel 2:13]]` and `[[Jonah 4:2]]` treat the merciful half of the same formula); 1:5 and `[[Ps. 97:5]]` (the footnote; the site's Psalms 97 explore already lists Nahum 1:5); 1:6 and `[[Mal. 3:2]]` (a lead: “who may abide the day of his coming”; read before using); 1:7 and `[[Ps. 34:8]]`, `[[Lam. 3:25]]` (footnotes); 1:10 and `[[Mal. 4:1]]` (stubble; the footnote gives JS—H 1:37, where Moroni quotes it); 1:15 and `[[Isa. 52:7]]` (below); 2:10 and `[[Joel 2:6]]` (footnote); 3:1 and `[[Ezek. 22:2]]` (the note “Judge the bloody city” already quotes Nahum 3:1); 3:10 and `[[Joel 3:3]]` (the note “People divided like plunder” quotes 3:10); 3:13 and `[[Jer. 51:30]]`; **Zephaniah's oracle on Nineveh** (`[[Zeph. 2:13–15]]`), the same event foretold by a contemporary, in chapter 3's setting or explore; and Revelation 18, whose fallen city borrows from Nahum 3 (Robinson: “some of John's language comes from Nahum's description of Nineveh”).
- **Modern revelation.** The footnotes give `[[D&C 1:9]]` (1:3), `[[D&C 101:66]]` (1:5), `[[D&C 70:18]]` (1:7), `[[D&C 29:9]]` and `[[JS—H 1:37]]` (1:10), `[[D&C 128:19]]` (1:15). A cross-reference that only repeats a footnote is not a note. The Latter-day Saint reading of Nineveh as a type of the world, and of the great and abominable church in `[[1 Ne. 13:5]]`, is KnoWhy #711's and Uriona's: one sentence at most, in chapter 3's `christ` or `explore`, linking, not explaining Nephi's vision.
- **Joseph Smith Translation.** None. The footnotes list no JST reading, and Jackson's introduction lists Nahum among the books that “received no changes” (most marked “Correct”). Say so only if a note needs it.
- **Theme candidates.** A writer reports what runs across the book; the coordinator adds it to `THEME-CANDIDATES.md` (not yet created). A three-chapter book may need no theme page.

**Habits to avoid** are listed in Amos's and Ezekiel's briefs and hold here. In Nahum especially: **don't gloat with the text, and don't apologize for it**. Describe what Nahum says and what it meant to Judah; the reading of the whole is in the contested questions below. And **don't fill the thin scholarship with Keil**: when a point rests only on him, ask whether the reader needs it.

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that three chapters say the same thing. Each quotation was copied from the page named, read with `source.mjs` on 2026-10-23.

- **A book of vengeance on a nation: how a faithful reader reads 1:2.** “God is jealous, and the LORD revengeth; the LORD revengeth, and is furious.” Van Dyke and Ogden say it plainly: the book's “tone is accusatory and vengeful, seemingly bereft of ethical and theological empathy,” yet Nahum “was called to pronounce the Lord's condemnation,” and testified that “the God of Israel was still God of all the earth.” Rasmussen: “His mercy shall not rob justice, but neither will justice rob His mercy.” Draper (RSC, 2015), on the Hebrew of “jealous”: it “identifies the strong feelings aroused when a cherished object or relationship is threatened,” and “the jealousy of God is the guarantee of justice.” **Proposed:** the note on 1:2 says what “jealous” means by linking `[[Ex. 20:5]]`, then what “revengeth” means here: vengeance is the Lord's alone (`[[Deut. 32:35]]`, linked), executed on “his adversaries” who have oppressed His people, not a license for Judah's. Verse 3 belongs in the same breath: the hymn quotes the Lord's name from `[[Ex. 34:6–7]]`, “slow to anger” beside “will not at all acquit the wicked”; Jonah quoted the merciful half against the Lord (`[[Jonah 4:2]]`), and Nahum gives the other. That pairing is the book's answer to the reader's question, and chapter 1's thread. Together the two books say the Lord spared Nineveh when it repented and judged it when it returned to its sins (*Scripture Helps*, paragraph 26).
- **The violence of chapters 2–3: describe, don't dwell** (`STANDARDS.md` §3). The siege (2:3–10), the slain and the corpses (3:3), the infants dashed in pieces at Thebes (3:10), and the city stripped and shamed as a harlot (3:4–7). The notes say what the images are and why they are there (a city that had done these things to others; a prophetic image of public disgrace, which `[[Isa. 47:2–3]]`, `[[Jer. 13:22–26]]` and `[[Ezek. 16:37]]` use of Babylon and of Jerusalem: a lead, check the site's notes there and link), and do not quote the worst lines at length. Assyria's boasts of cruelty are summarized, not quoted (Rasmussen: kings who “boast of the captives they have maimed”; more at `[[Jonah 1:2]]`). “Witchcrafts” and “whoredoms” (3:4): the Student Manual (20-7) reads them as Nineveh exporting its wickedness “through her power and influence”; one sentence, from a source read.
- **The acrostic of chapter 1.** Rasmussen: “His book begins with an acrostic, with one strophe (stanza) for each of the first fifteen letters of the Hebrew alphabet, with two alterations of the sequence,” the first seven on God's power with verse 3a breaking in on His goodness, the second seven the same with verse 7, and verse 10 the transition. **Proposed:** one note, at 1:2 or in chapter 1's setting, says the hymn follows the alphabet for about half its length, as Rasmussen describes, and links the site's explanation of the device (`[[Ps. 9:1]]`; Lamentations 4's setting). Don't print a Hebrew letter list from memory; if the note names letters, take them from `hebrew.mjs nahum 1:2-10`, verse by verse.
- **“The LORD is good, a strong hold in the day of trouble” (1:7).** The book's center for a reader now, and the verse *Come, Follow Me* gives children. Rasmussen ends on it. The note says what a “strong hold” (*māʿôz*, a refuge; check the lexicon) was, and that “he knoweth them that trust in him” is the knowing of `[[Ps. 1:6]]` (the site's note “What it means that the LORD knows a way”: link). Chapter 1's `christ` section turns on it.
- **“A wicked counsellor” (1:11) and “I will make thy grave” (1:14).** The Student Manual (20-5) names Sennacherib, reads the yoke as his tribute, and says his murder in Nisroch's temple fulfilled 1:14 “as Nahum had prophesied” (`[[2 Kgs. 19:37]]`). That cannot stand with the date the same manual gives (20-2): Sennacherib died in 681 (the site's guide [Two Kingdoms and Assyria](/old-testament/guides/two-kingdoms-and-assyria/) gives his reign as 705–681; `[[Isa. 37:38]]`, the note “Sennacherib's end”), before Thebes fell. Keil, who does date the book under Hezekiah, still says 1:14 has no “allusion to his death or murder.” **Proposed:** the counsellor is left unnamed, as the text leaves him: an Assyrian king who “imagineth evil against the LORD.” Sennacherib's invasion (701; `[[2 Kgs. 18:13]]`) may be background, not fulfillment. The Student Manual's reading of 1:14 is not used.
- **The feet upon the mountains (1:15) and Isaiah 52:7.** Nahum: “Behold upon the mountains the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace! O Judah, keep thy solemn feasts.” Isaiah 52:7 opens with nearly the same words. No source read settles who echoed whom (Breitenstein says Nahum “referenced” Isaiah). **The chapter does not say who borrowed.** In Nahum the messenger brings the news of Nineveh's fall and Judah's freedom to keep its feasts (Van Dyke and Ogden: Nahum “invites Judah to rejoice in the destruction of Assyria and claim the opportunity to cleanse the temple and worship God freely”). **Proposed: one note at 1:15**, in this order: what the verse meant in Nahum (the runner with the news; the feasts and vows resumed); that Isaiah 52:7 has the same words of the herald of Zion's redemption, linking the site's note on `[[Isa. 52:7]]` (“Abinadi and the beautiful feet”); that Paul applied them to those who preach (`[[Rom. 10:15]]`, the footnote); and that Abinadi read the feet as finally the Lord's, linking the note on `[[Mosiah 15:18]]` (“Whose feet are beautiful”) without retelling it. Chapter 1's `christ` may point from the fallen oppressor to the Lord who breaks every yoke, in a statement. Nothing is added under Isaiah 52 or Mosiah 15.
- **“Turned away the excellency of Jacob” (2:2).** The verb is *šûb* (`hebrew.mjs nahum 2:2`, H7725). Breitenstein: “Most scholars tend to render that phrase to read, ‘For the Lord hath restored the excellency [or glory] of Jacob.’” A Language note is earned if the lexicon and a modern translation read (NRSV, ESV, NIV) confirm it, since the King James reads the opposite way; Keil (paragraph 5) takes it as the Lord “returns to the eminence of Israel.”
- **Huzzab (2:7).** The Church's footnote: “OR the queen.” The Hebrew is a verb, not a name (`hebrew.mjs nahum 2:7`: H5324, a *hophal* perfect, “it is established” or “decreed”: check the lexicon page). Breitenstein: some take it as the queen, some as Nineveh personified, “Other translations render the original Hebrew word as ‘it is decreed.’” **Proposed:** a short Language note giving the three readings, the King James's as a name, and settling nothing.
- **The fall as foretold (2:6, 8; 1:8).** “The gates of the rivers shall be opened, and the palace shall be dissolved.” Breitenstein reads sluice gates and the Khosr River; ancient accounts of a flood at the fall are a lead from memory: **use only what a source read says**, and don't claim archaeology confirms it. Breitenstein's wall figures cite Wikipedia: **not used**.
- **The comfort the name means.** Ball: “Nahum's contemporaries would have indeed found some hope and comfort in the prophet's assurance that their oppressors would eventually be destroyed”; Keil: “it may be called a prophecy of consolation for that kingdom.” **Proposed:** the name is explained once, at 1:1. The book's comfort is named in chapter 1's thread (the yoke broken, 1:13; the feasts kept, 1:15) and, if a source supports it, at 3:7 (“whence shall I seek comforters for thee?”): the comfort is Judah's, and none is left for Nineveh. Ball's analogy and questions are not used.
