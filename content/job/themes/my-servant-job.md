The first thing the LORD says about Job is a title. “Hast thou considered my servant Job, that there is none like him in the earth, a perfect and an upright man, one that feareth God, and escheweth evil?” ([[Job 1:8]]). He says it again in the next chapter, and then the title is not heard for thirty-nine chapters. It comes back when the LORD has finished speaking out of the whirlwind, four times in two verses: “my servant Job shall pray for you: for him will I accept” ([[Job 42:8]]).

A reader of Isaiah has heard the title before. “Behold, my servant shall deal prudently, he shall be exalted and extolled, and be very high” ([[Isa. 52:13]]) begins the poem about the man of sorrows. The Church's Topical Guide has an entry “Servant,” a long list of servants of every kind. Job's verse is in it, and further down are “my servant shall deal prudently” and “shall my righteous servant justify many.”[@tg-servant] The footnotes at Job 1:8 and at Isaiah 53:11 both send the reader to that entry.[@lds-scriptures]

Latter-day Saint writers have gone a step further and called Job a type of Christ. S. Brent Farley wrote in the *Ensign* in 1980 that “in his life we find a type of some of the most distinctive and divine traits of the Savior.”[@farley-job-parallels] Donald Parry calls Job “a type of the suffering servant, the man of grief.”[@parry-job-type] Mack Stirling says that “many have rightly considered Job to be a type of Christ.”[@stirling-job]

This essay takes them at their word and asks what the type teaches. My claim is that it teaches in two ways. Where Job is like the Servant of Isaiah, his book lets us hear at length what Isaiah says in a line. Where he is unlike Him, there are three differences, and they do not spoil the comparison. Job opened his mouth, and the Servant did not. Job was blameless, and he was not sinless. Job's wounds were “without cause,” and they healed no one. Each of the three marks something that only Jesus Christ did. Reading Job against Isaiah's Servant in this way, and arguing from the places where the likeness breaks, is my own doing, and I will make the case from the words of the two books.

## A servant and a type

The title alone proves little. The LORD calls Abraham “my servant” ([[Gen. 26:24]]), and Moses ([[Num. 12:7]]), and David ([[2 Sam. 7:5]]). If Job is a likeness of the Servant of Isaiah 53, it has to be shown from what happens to him between the first “my servant” and the last.

It helps to say first what a type is. Nephi's statement is the broadest: “all things which have been given of God from the beginning of the world, unto man, are the typifying of him” ([[2 Ne. 11:4]]). Farley says what that means when the type is a man. To call Job's life a type “is not to say he was manipulated to make an example”; it is to say that “his endurance and faith were so significant that they prefigure the endurance and faith of the Savior.”[@farley-job-parallels]

One thing about types matters for what follows. A type is always less than the thing it figures. The lambs of the altar were animals, and “it is not possible that the blood of bulls and of goats should take away sins” ([[Heb. 10:4]]). The high priest of Israel had to offer “first for his own sins, and then for the people's” ([[Heb. 7:27]]).

The Epistle to the Hebrews does not treat those shortfalls as an embarrassment. It argues from them to Christ. A type teaches by its likeness, and it teaches again at the point where it gives out. That is the way I mean to read Job.

## A man of sorrows

Here are the likenesses that have been drawn. Parry sets his out in a chart, Farley in an article, and Stirling in a paragraph. The third column says whose each is.

| Job | Jesus Christ | Drawn by |
|---|---|---|
| “None like him in the earth, a perfect and an upright man” ([[Job 1:8]]) | Perfect, and unlike any other man | Farley;[@farley-job-parallels] Parry[@parry-job-type] |
| Put in Satan's hand, within a limit ([[Job 1:12]]; [[Job 2:6]]) | Tempted “in all points” ([[Heb. 4:15]]); offered the kingdoms of the world ([[Matt. 4:8–10]]) | Farley; the Student Manual[@ot-manual-job] |
| Once “as a king in the army” ([[Job 29:25]]), now held “in derision” ([[Job 30:1]]) | The King who came down and was held in derision | Farley; Stirling[@stirling-job] |
| “My kinsfolk have failed, and my familiar friends have forgotten me” ([[Job 19:14]]) | A stranger in His own house | Farley |
| Smitten on the cheek ([[Job 16:10]]), and they “spare not to spit in my face” ([[Job 30:10]]) | Struck and spat upon | Farley; Stirling |
| “They have smitten me upon the cheek” ([[Job 16:10]]) | “I gave my back to the smiters, and my cheeks to them that plucked off the hair” ([[Isa. 50:6]]) | Delitzsch[@delitzsch-job] |
| “Not for any injustice in mine hands” ([[Job 16:17]]) | “He had done no violence” ([[Isa. 53:9]]) | Delitzsch |
| His losses and his sores | “A man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief” ([[Isa. 53:3]]) | Parry; Farley |
| He prays for the friends who accused him ([[Job 42:10]]) | The “one mediator” ([[1 Tim. 2:5]]); the prayer from the cross | Parry; Farley; Stirling |
| “The LORD blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning” ([[Job 42:12]]) | Raised and exalted; the Firstborn, whose is the double portion | Farley; Stirling; Parry |

A list like this can be read as ten coincidences. I think it is one portrait, and three of its rows say whose. The smitten cheek is Isaiah 50. The man of sorrows and the hands that did no violence are Isaiah 53. These are the poems about the LORD's Servant (see [The Servant Songs](/isaiah/themes/servant-songs/)), and Franz Delitzsch, a commentator of the nineteenth century, noticed that “in the description of the Servant of Jehovah, one is here and there involuntarily reminded of the book of Job.”[@delitzsch-job]

One of his examples is closer in Hebrew than in English. The King James has “injustice” in Job and “violence” in Isaiah, but the word is the same, and so are the two small words in front of it.[@oshb-wlc] Job says of himself, very nearly in Isaiah’s words, what Isaiah says of the Servant.

I would add a line to the portrait. In chapter 19 Job begs, “Have pity upon me, have pity upon me, O ye my friends; for the hand of God hath touched me” ([[Job 19:21]]). In Isaiah the onlookers say of the Servant, “we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted” ([[Isa. 53:4]]). “Touched” and “stricken” are one Hebrew verb,[@oshb-wlc] and the lexicon lists the two verses under a single sense, together with Satan's “touch all that he hath” at the beginning of the book.[@bdb]

Isaiah's “we” are onlookers. They saw a man struck by God and drew the natural conclusion about him, and in the next breath they are confessing that they had it wrong. Job has such onlookers, and we know their names. “Remember, I pray thee,” Eliphaz says, “who ever perished, being innocent?” ([[Job 4:7]]). By his third speech he is past asking: “Is not thy wickedness great?” ([[Job 22:5]]).

Isaiah gives the onlookers' mistake one line. Job gives it a book. Speech after speech, three devout men look at a blameless sufferer and reason from the blow to the guilt, and the sufferer has to listen. As I read it, this is the largest thing the likeness has to show. The book of Job is what “we did esteem him stricken” sounds like to the man it is said about.

The Gospels show that it was said about Jesus. The chief priests at the cross use the friends' reasoning: “He trusted in God; let him deliver him now, if he will have him” ([[Matt. 27:43]]).

In both books the onlookers learn that they were wrong, and in both it is through the man they misjudged that they are spared. The LORD says to Eliphaz, “ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right” ([[Job 42:7]]), and sends him to Job. Delitzsch says of that moment that God will accept only the man “whom they regarded as one punished of God.”[@delitzsch-job]

## Job opened his mouth

The first difference is the most obvious. For two chapters Job is very nearly the silent sufferer. He loses everything, and “in all this Job sinned not, nor charged God foolishly” ([[Job 1:22]]). He loses his health, and “in all this did not Job sin with his lips” ([[Job 2:10]]). The friends arrive and sit with him seven days, “and none spake a word unto him” ([[Job 2:13]]).

Then: “After this opened Job his mouth, and cursed his day” ([[Job 3:1]]). Of the Servant, Isaiah says this:

> He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth. ([[Isa. 53:7]])

The verb and the noun are the same in both verses.[@oshb-wlc] It is an ordinary phrase, and I build nothing on its being rare. But the two books use it to say opposite things when the innocent man has been struck and the question is what he will do.

Once Job's mouth is open it stays open. “Therefore I will not refrain my mouth” ([[Job 7:11]]). His speeches run to the end of chapter 31, and when the LORD has spoken his answer is “I will lay mine hand upon my mouth” ([[Job 40:4]]).

Jesus before His judges is the other picture. “And when he was accused of the chief priests and elders, he answered nothing,” and when Pilate pressed Him, “he answered him to never a word; insomuch that the governor marvelled greatly” ([[Matt. 27:12–14]]).

It would be easy to draw the wrong lesson here, that Job failed a test which Christ passed. The book is more careful than that. The Bible Dictionary speaks of “words without knowledge” which “he afterwards retracts,”[@bd-job-book] and President Henry B. Eyring says that Job “repented of calling God unfair.”[@scripture-helps-job] But the LORD never tells Job that he was guilty. It is the friends, who said he was, who are told that they “have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath” ([[Job 42:7]]). (The note “The thing that is right” on [chapter 42](/job/42/) gives the readings.)

So a blameless man may plead his innocence under a blow he did not earn and still be “my servant” when it is over. I think that changes how the Servant's silence should be read. If it were a sin to plead one's innocence, His silence would be only the absence of a sin. Job shows that it was more. The plea was open to an innocent man, and the Servant did not make it.

Consider what Job wanted from speaking. “I know that I shall be justified” ([[Job 13:18]]). And of the hearing he hoped for: “so should I be delivered for ever from my judge” ([[Job 23:7]]). He spoke in order to go free. Jesus could have gone free. “Thinkest thou that I cannot now pray to my Father, and he shall presently give me more than twelve legions of angels?” ([[Matt. 26:53]]). He did not ask, and before Pilate He did not defend Himself. I read Isaiah's “he was numbered with the transgressors” ([[Isa. 53:12]]) beside that silence. He let Himself be counted as what He was not.

The difference shows in the grammar of the two verses that Delitzsch joined. They share the word for striking and the word for the cheek.[@oshb-wlc] Job says, “They have smitten me upon the cheek.” The Servant says, “I gave my back to the smiters, and my cheeks to them that plucked off the hair: I hid not my face from shame and spitting” ([[Isa. 50:6]]). In Job's sentence other people act and he suffers it. In the Servant's sentence the acting is His.

Nobody asked Job. The raiders, the fire and the great wind came on him in one day ([[Job 1:13–19]]), and the sores after them ([[Job 2:7]]), and he chose none of it. The Servant hands Himself over. “No man taketh it from me,” Jesus said of His life, “but I lay it down of myself” ([[John 10:18]]). President Dieter F. Uchtdorf's word for it is that the Savior “willingly took upon Himself the pain and sickness and suffering of us all.”[@uchtdorf-you-are-my-hands]

That is the first thing the broken likeness shows. Job's suffering happened to him. Christ's was chosen, and His silence before His accusers is how the choosing looked from outside.

## The iniquities of my youth

Job is “perfect and upright,” and the Hebrew word “can also mean ‘blameless,’ ‘complete,’ or ‘whole.’” The Church's *Scripture Helps* adds at once that “while Job was a devout follower of God, he was not without sin.”[@scripture-helps-job]

Job does not claim to be sinless. Zophar and Elihu say that he did ([[Job 11:4]]; [[Job 33:9]]), but they give his words their own turn. “Thou writest bitter things against me,” he tells God, “and makest me to possess the iniquities of my youth” ([[Job 13:26]]). “And why dost thou not pardon my transgression?” ([[Job 7:21]]). Stirling opens his own statement of the type with the concession: “Although Job was not without sin, admitting to youthful iniquities.”[@stirling-job] What Job maintains to the end is narrower than sinlessness. Nothing in his hands accounts for what has been done to him.

The line the two books share makes the same point. Job says, “Not for any injustice in mine hands.” Isaiah says of the Servant, “he had done no violence,” and goes on, “neither was any deceit in his mouth” ([[Isa. 53:9]]). Peter quotes that verse of Jesus and widens it: “Who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth” ([[1 Pet. 2:22]]). The Church's footnote in Isaiah sends the reader there.[@lds-scriptures]

Job could say the first half. The second half Peter gives as “did no sin,” and Job does not claim that. Nor is his mouth beyond reproach. It was not deceit that he spoke, but by the end of the book he is withdrawing things he had said: “therefore have I uttered that I understood not; things too wonderful for me, which I knew not” ([[Job 42:3]]). Three verses later comes “Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes” ([[Job 42:6]]), and faithful readers hear it differently. *Scripture Helps* notes that “myself” was supplied by the translators and that Job may have been saying, “Therefore do I recant.”[@scripture-helps-job] Delitzsch hears a man who “has penitently acknowledged his error.”[@delitzsch-job] Stirling hears no repentance for sin at all, only Job “changing his mind.”[@stirling-job] (The note “What Job repented of” on [chapter 42](/job/42/) sets these out.) On every one of these readings Job has something to take back. Nothing of the kind is said of the Servant.

Then there is the plainest fact of all. The type needed the One he typifies. Job is the man who said “my redeemer” ([[Job 19:25]]), and the other two essays on this book, [A Daysman Betwixt Us](/job/themes/daysman-betwixt-us/) and [He That Liveth, and Was Dead](/job/themes/he-that-liveth/), follow what he asked for. A lamb on the altar does not need the sacrifice it stands for. Job does.

Here is what I take from it. By the LORD's own estimate Job is the best the earth had, and the best the earth had speaks of the iniquities of his youth and looks for a redeemer. When scripture says that Jesus “did no sin,” it is not saying that He was a very good man. The book of Job has shown us a very good man, and the distance still to go. In Farley's words, “No mortal has lived the kind of perfect life lived by the Savior.”[@farley-job-parallels]

## Without cause

The third difference is about what the suffering did.

The LORD gives His own account of Job's ruin in the second chapter. He tells Satan that Job “holdeth fast his integrity, although thou movedst me against him, to destroy him without cause” ([[Job 2:3]]). “Without cause” is one Hebrew word, the word Satan had used when he asked, “Doth Job fear God for nought?” ([[Job 1:9]]).[@oshb-wlc] Job uses it of himself later: God “multiplieth my wounds without cause” ([[Job 9:17]]).

Isaiah does not say that the Servant was wounded without cause. He says this:

> But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed. ([[Isa. 53:5]])

Job's wounds were without cause. The Servant's were not. They had a cause, and it was not in Him.

This puts the friends in a strange light. They were sure that this man's wounds were wounds for transgression. Of Job that was false. Isaiah's onlookers thought the same of the Servant, and of Him it was true, except that the transgression was theirs. Elder Dale G. Renlund, writing about Job, says it in a sentence: “our sins did cause the Savior to innocently suffer.”[@renlund-trust-god]

Now count what Job's wounds did for anyone else. His children are dead by the end of chapter 1, and nothing he endures afterward brings them back. The friends are spared in chapter 42, but not by his sores. They bring an offering, and he prays.

Ezekiel states a limit, and names Job in stating it. Of a land under judgment the Lord says, “Though these three men, Noah, Daniel, and Job, were in it, they should deliver but their own souls by their righteousness” ([[Ezek. 14:14]]), and then, “they shall deliver neither son nor daughter” ([[Ezek. 14:20]]). To deliver one's soul here is to come through the judgment alive, as the notes on [Ezekiel 14](/ezekiel/14/) explain. Ezekiel is speaking of famine and sword in a guilty land, and the book of Job never says that Job's children died for sin. The point is how far one man's righteousness reaches.

Set that beside the last “my servant” in Isaiah's poem: “by his knowledge shall my righteous servant justify many; for he shall bear their iniquities” ([[Isa. 53:11]]). Putting the two verses together is my own doing. In Ezekiel the righteousness of Job reaches no one but Job. In Isaiah the righteous Servant justifies many.

Amulek gives the reason as doctrine. “Now there is not any man that can sacrifice his own blood which will atone for the sins of another” ([[Alma 34:11]]). So the great and last sacrifice “shall not be a human sacrifice; but it must be an infinite and eternal sacrifice” ([[Alma 34:10]]), and it “will be the Son of God, yea, infinite and eternal” ([[Alma 34:14]]).

I read Job as the test of Amulek's sentence. Take the most upright man on earth and let him suffer, innocently, as much as a book can hold. His suffering purchases nothing for anyone else. That is no failing in Job. It is what a man is. Farley says that Job “had but a foretaste” of what the Savior suffered,[@farley-job-parallels] and the difference of degree is real. But degree is not the whole of it. Innocence and depth alone do not redeem. Amulek says that the sacrifice must be infinite and eternal, the Son of God.

## For him will I accept

One thing does pass from the type to Christ without falling short.

The LORD called Job “my servant” before the first messenger came, and He calls him “my servant” when it is over. Nothing in between revokes the title. Delitzsch thought this the point of the ending: “The principal thing is not that Job is doubly blessed, but that God acknowledges him as His servant.”[@delitzsch-job]

On that acknowledgment other people are spared. “My servant Job shall pray for you: for him will I accept.” How that scene is a likeness of Christ's advocacy is the argument of [A Daysman Betwixt Us](/job/themes/daysman-betwixt-us/), and I will not make it twice.

What belongs here is why this part holds when the others gave out. Job's speech, his uprightness and his suffering were his own, and each was a man's. The acceptance is the LORD's act, and in Job's book it already does what that other essay shows it doing in the gospel. Others are spared because the LORD accepts His servant.

The restoration should be read in that light, and I do not read the doubled flocks as wages. Satan's charge was that Job served God for pay, and Job answered it by serving when there was none. An ending that paid him for his suffering would concede the charge. What the text says is that “the LORD gave Job twice as much as he had before” ([[Job 42:10]]). Tanner says, “Righteousness does not insulate us from suffering or assure us of material rewards.”[@tanner-considered-job]

## Thou art not yet as Job

Peter once wrote to servants who did well and suffered for it ([[1 Pet. 2:18–20]]). What he told them holds both halves of this essay:

> For even hereunto were ye called: because Christ also suffered for us, leaving us an example, that ye should follow his steps: Who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth: Who, when he was reviled, reviled not again; when he suffered, he threatened not; but committed himself to him that judgeth righteously: Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed. ([[1 Pet. 2:21–24]])

The sinless mouth, the silence and the stripes are all from Isaiah 53. Some of it Peter holds out as “an example, that ye should follow.” Some of it no one follows: “his own self bare our sins.”

The example lies where Job stopped short of the Servant, in a life without sin and in not answering back. Peter asks a disciple to follow Christ further than Job went.

What Job and the disciple share with Christ is the wrongful suffering itself, and Peter says of it, “this is acceptable with God” ([[1 Pet. 2:20]]). The blow is not a verdict. The friends thought it was, and so did the men at the foot of the cross. President Uchtdorf has said, “It is unworthy of us as Christians to think that those who suffer deserve their suffering.”[@uchtdorf-you-are-my-hands] Anyone who looks at a stricken neighbour and works backward to a sin has taken Eliphaz's seat.

In the bearing of sins the disciple does not follow at all, and that is a mercy. The suffering that answers for sin has been borne already: “For behold, I, God, have suffered these things for all, that they might not suffer if they would repent” ([[D&C 19:16]]). What a disciple suffers wrongfully, as Job did, is not payment for anything, his own sins or another's.

And one who has cried out as Job did is not cast off for it. Job took back what he had said amiss, and God called him “my servant” afterward.

Two revelations from Liberty Jail put Job and the Lord side by side. In the first the Savior “comforted the Prophet Joseph Smith by comparing his afflictions to those of Job”:[@seminary-intro-job] “Thou art not yet as Job; thy friends do not contend against thee, neither charge thee with transgression, as they did Job” ([[D&C 121:10]]). In the second He gave the other measure. “The Son of Man hath descended below them all. Art thou greater than he?” ([[D&C 122:8]]).

The revelations measure Joseph twice, once against Job and once against Christ. He is not yet as Job. And beneath everything he has suffered or could suffer is the Son of Man. The Church's seminary manual gives the doctrine of that verse as “The Savior suffered the pains and afflictions of all people.”[@dc-seminary-2014-126]

I think that is what the type is for. Job goes as far down as his book can take a man, and from there a reader can see how much farther the Servant went.
