Near the end of his answer to Zophar, Job asks the question that every mourner has asked since: “If a man die, shall he live again?” ([[Job 14:14]]). Five chapters later he says the sentence for which his book is best known: “For I know that my redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth” ([[Job 19:25]]).

Latter-day Saints have long read the second sentence as the answer to the first. Elder Russell M. Nelson quoted the question, said of Job, “In faith, he answered his own question,” and went straight on to the testimony of chapter 19.[@nelson-life-after-life] A seminary lesson has students mark Job's questions in chapter 14 and then read chapter 19 “looking for the answer Job gives to his questions.”[@ot-seminary-2026-job-14-19]

I think that reading is right, and this essay is about something strange in it. The answer does not answer the question that was asked. Job asked about a man, whether he will live when he has died. He answers about someone else, who lives. In Hebrew the mismatch is plainer than in English. In chapter 14 Job said that man “riseth not,” and asked whether a dead man “shall live.” To live and to rise are the two things he says in chapter 19 that his Redeemer does. The Hebrew roots are the same, and only the subject has changed.

My claim is that the change of subject is the answer. From chapter 3 to chapter 19 Job looks for some ground of hope in the face of death. He looks in himself and in the nature of things and finds none, and then he looks to what God feels for the creature He made. What he comes to know at last is not a fact about dead men. It is a fact about a living Person. The gospel of Jesus Christ teaches that this is the right shape for the answer, and it adds what Job's sentence leaves unsaid. Reading the two verses by their shared words is my own doing, and I will make the case from the text.

## The thing that I long for

Job's first thought about death is that it would be a relief. In the grave, he says, “the wicked cease from troubling; and there the weary be at rest” ([[Job 3:17]]).

It is Eliphaz who brings the word “hope” into the book. “Is not this thy fear, thy confidence, thy hope, and the uprightness of thy ways?” ([[Job 4:6]]). Eliphaz is pointing Job to himself. A man who has feared God and lived uprightly has, in that, his reason to expect good. For Eliphaz, Job's hope rests on Job's own fear of God and upright ways.

Job takes up the word and turns it over. The first time he uses it of himself the King James hides it: “Oh that I might have my request; and that God would grant me the thing that I long for!” ([[Job 6:8]]). “The thing that I long for” is, in Hebrew, “my hope,” Eliphaz's noun.[@oshb-wlc] The next verse says what the hope is: “that it would please God to destroy me” ([[Job 6:9]]). What Job hopes for is to die.

He also says why no better hope is open to him. “What is my strength, that I should hope?” ([[Job 6:11]]). If hope rests on what a man has in himself, as Eliphaz said, then Job has nothing left to rest it on.

And death, as he sees it then, is the end. “As the cloud is consumed and vanisheth away: so he that goeth down to the grave shall come up no more” ([[Job 7:9]]). (The note “No coming up from Sheol” on [chapter 7](/job/7/) explains the grave as Job understood it.)

Yet the last line of that same speech has an odd thought in it. “For now shall I sleep in the dust; and thou shalt seek me in the morning, but I shall not be” ([[Job 7:21]]). Job expects that God will come looking for him. He only expects to be gone when it happens. That thought, that God might want him, is the one he builds on next.

## The work of thine hands

It comes back in chapter 10 as a complaint. “Thine hands have made me and fashioned me together round about; yet thou dost destroy me. Remember, I beseech thee, that thou hast made me as the clay; and wilt thou bring me into dust again?” ([[Job 10:8–9]]). There is an argument inside the complaint. A maker does not lightly unmake what he has taken such care over.

Chapter 14 begins with the case against hope. A tree has a future: “For there is hope of a tree, if it be cut down, that it will sprout again” ([[Job 14:7]]). The root is still in the ground, and “through the scent of water it will bud” ([[Job 14:9]]). A man is not like that. “But man dieth, and wasteth away: yea, man giveth up the ghost, and where is he?” ([[Job 14:10]]). And then the verdict: “So man lieth down, and riseth not: till the heavens be no more, they shall not awake, nor be raised out of their sleep” ([[Job 14:12]]).

Keep the words “riseth not” in mind. We will need them.

Then, with no warning, Job says this:

> O that thou wouldest hide me in the grave, that thou wouldest keep me secret, until thy wrath be past, that thou wouldest appoint me a set time, and remember me! If a man die, shall he live again? all the days of my appointed time will I wait, till my change come. Thou shalt call, and I will answer thee: thou wilt have a desire to the work of thine hands. ([[Job 14:13–15]])

Two things in the wish are taken from what Job has already said. The first is the call. One chapter earlier he had demanded a hearing from God in these words: “Then call thou, and I will answer” ([[Job 13:22]]). The two verbs are the same in both places.[@oshb-wlc] In chapter 13 the summons was to a court, in this life. Now Job imagines the same summons reaching him in the grave, and himself answering from there.

The second is the hands. “The work of thine hands” looks back to “thine hands have made me,” and the word for “thine hands” is the same.[@oshb-wlc] The complaint of chapter 10 has become the ground of a wish. God will want back what His hands have made.

Notice what has moved. Nothing in these verses says that a man is more than verse 12 said he was. Job has learned no new fact about human beings. What he has found is a thought about God, that God's attachment to His creature might outlast His anger and outlast the creature's death. In these three verses hope rests on what God desires and not on what man is.

How much Job is claiming here has been read in more than one way. The Church's chapter heading reads the passage as “the guarantee of a resurrection,” with Job waiting for “the Lord’s call to come forth from the grave.”[@lds-scriptures] Mack Stirling, a Latter-day Saint reader of the book, calls it the first of Job's great insights, a glimpse of “the possibility of renewal of life in a resurrection,” and adds that the vision “is not immediately sustained.”[@stirling-job] Franz Delitzsch, in the nineteenth century, heard only a wish, “a craving of his heart,” and no conviction.[@delitzsch-job]

The chapter does not end on the wish. Its closing verses come back to loss: “thou destroyest the hope of man” ([[Job 14:19]]). The tree had hope, and for man as man Job still sees none. What verses 13 to 15 have, on any of these readings, is a ground outside man, the Lord's call. (The notes “A hiding place in Sheol” and “If a man die, shall he live again?” on [chapter 14](/job/14/) take the verses one at a time.)

## Where is now my hope?

In his next speech Job comes back to the grave, and to the word. “If I wait, the grave is mine house: I have made my bed in the darkness” ([[Job 17:13]]). “And where is now my hope? as for my hope, who shall see it?” ([[Job 17:15]]). It will go down with him, he says, “when our rest together is in the dust” ([[Job 17:16]]).

Latter-day Saint readers hear these lines differently. Stirling hears defiance. Job once longed for death, and now he “refuses to yield to the grave or worm by letting go of his hope”; he has “a new kind of hope, born of travail.”[@stirling-job] Ryan Davis reads the lines more plainly: if Job cannot find hope from his friends, “he is certainly not going to find it in the grave.”[@ot-minute-job-4]

I do not need to choose between them, and I would not say that Job has stopped hoping. He has told us that he will trust, and that he will wait ([[Job 13:15]]; [[Job 14:14]]). What I notice is something both readings can grant, because it is in the question. “Who shall see it?” Whatever hope Job still has, there is nothing in sight for it to stand on. Everything he can see leads to the house in the dark.

In the speech after that, the tree returns. “He hath destroyed me on every side, and I am gone: and mine hope hath he removed like a tree” ([[Job 19:10]]). “Hope” and “tree” are the two nouns of 14:7.[@oshb-wlc] I read the line as Job's reply to his own comparison. In chapter 14 the tree was the thing that had hope, because when it is cut down the root stays in the earth. Here the tree is not cut down. The lexicon gives the verb as “remove,”[@bdb] and a modern translation has “he has uprooted my hope like a tree.”[@nrsvue] An uprooted tree has no stump left to sprout from. Job is saying that even the hope he allowed to the tree is not his.

This is where Job stands, fifteen verses before the best-known sentence in the book. He has no strength to hope with, and his hope has been uprooted.

## My redeemer liveth

> Oh that my words were now written! oh that they were printed in a book! That they were graven with an iron pen and lead in the rock for ever! For I know that my redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth: And though after my skin worms destroy this body, yet in my flesh shall I see God. ([[Job 19:23–26]])

The Church's sources read this passage with two emphases, and both should be heard. A redeemer in Old Testament times was a kinsman who restored what a relative had lost, and so *Scripture Helps* says that Job “was expressing his faith that he would ultimately be vindicated and that justice would be restored.”[@scripture-helps-job] The chapter heading says that “Job prophesies that he will be resurrected and that in his flesh he will see God,”[@lds-scriptures] and the Bible Dictionary counts “Job’s assurance of the bodily resurrection and his testimony of the Redeemer” among the high points of the book.[@bd-job-book] (What kind of person this redeemer would have to be is the subject of another essay, [A Daysman Betwixt Us](/job/themes/daysman-betwixt-us/).)

My interest is in two words of verse 25.

The first is “liveth.” Job's question in chapter 14 was “shall he live again?” The Hebrew word for “liveth” comes from the verb of that question.[@strongs-concordance]

The second is “shall stand.” In Hebrew this is the same verb, in the same form, as “riseth” in 14:12, where it had “not” in front of it.[@oshb-wlc] Man “lieth down, and riseth not.” The Redeemer shall rise. The form is a common one in Job.[@oshb-wlc] The weight is not on its rarity but on the pair, live and rise, in two passages that Elder Nelson and the seminary lesson already join.

I should say what I am not claiming. The lexicon gives the verb a different sense in each place. In chapter 14 it is rising from where one lies dead; in chapter 19 it is rising to vindicate, as a witness stands up in court.[@bdb] Job does not say that his Redeemer will rise from the dead. He says nothing about how the Redeemer comes to be standing there. The Church's footnote at “stand” sends the reader to the Resurrection of Jesus Christ,[@lds-scriptures] and President Gordon B. Hinckley said that Job “prophetically declared” these verses “concerning the resurrected Master.”[@hinckley-empty-tomb] I am speaking here only of what the two words carry by themselves.

What I do claim is this. Job said in chapter 14 that man “riseth not,” and asked whether a dead man “shall live.” In chapter 19 he reaches for the same two words, and he does not give them to the dead man. He does not say, “I know that I shall live.” He says, “I know that my redeemer liveth.” The question was about himself, and the answer is about Him.

That matters because of what has not changed. Job still expects to die. In the next breath he speaks of his body destroyed. Eleven chapters later he will say so again, and begin the sentence the same way: “For I know that thou wilt bring me to death, and to the house appointed for all living” ([[Job 30:23]]). “I know” is the same word in the same form, and “living” is the word translated “liveth.”[@oshb-wlc] So Job knows two things and holds them together. Every living thing is on its way to the house of death, and his Redeemer is living.

He has not found a reason for hope in man after all. He has stopped looking for one there. The hope that was uprooted in verse 10 is not planted again in Job. In its place is knowledge of another, and what Job expects beyond death, to see God, depends on that other: “whom I shall see for myself, and mine eyes shall behold, and not another” ([[Job 19:27]]).

I think this is what Stirling's phrase “a new kind of hope” describes, which he ties to Job's plea for a pledge in chapter 17,[@stirling-job] and I would say that what is new is where the hope rests. Davis says that because of the redeemer Job is confident “that his earlier hope will come true,” the hope of being taken out of the grave.[@ot-minute-job-4] The wish of chapter 14 had wanted a reason. The reason, when it comes, is a person.

Nor do I say that Job understood the Resurrection as later revelation sets it out. He “does not say who this redeemer is.”[@ot-minute-job-4] The Hebrew of verse 26 says “from my flesh,” which, in words the Old Testament Student Manual quotes, “can be interpreted in either sense,” in the body or apart from it; and Latter-day Saints “do not depend upon this passage to establish their belief in a literal resurrection, but point to it as one more glorious affirmation of it.”[@ot-manual-job] (The notes on [chapter 19](/job/19/) set out the Hebrew.) My claim is smaller than that. Job moved his hope to the right place.

## And was dead

Job says two things about his Redeemer for certain, that he is a redeemer and that he lives. There may be a third. What the King James renders “at the latter day” is in Hebrew the one word “last.”[@oshb-wlc] It can be read of time, as the King James reads it and as a modern translation does with “in the end,”[@nrsvue] or of the Redeemer, as Delitzsch reads it: He “will rise up as the last One.”[@delitzsch-job] (The note “At the last, upon the dust” on [chapter 19](/job/19/) gives both.) The next step depends on Delitzsch's reading.

On that reading, Isaiah puts two of Job's words into the mouth of God. “Thus saith the LORD the King of Israel, and his redeemer the LORD of hosts; I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God” ([[Isa. 44:6]]). “Redeemer” and “last” are the words of Job 19:25,[@oshb-wlc] and Delitzsch explains Job's “last” from this verse.[@delitzsch-job] In Isaiah the Redeemer who is the Last is the LORD Himself, and the Church's footnote there sends the reader to the Topical Guide's “Jesus Christ, Jehovah.”[@lds-scriptures]

Then, on Patmos, John sees the risen Lord and falls at His feet as dead, and hears this:

> Fear not; I am the first and the last: I am he that liveth, and was dead; and, behold, I am alive for evermore, Amen; and have the keys of hell and of death. ([[Rev. 1:17–18]])

Setting Job beside this verse is my own doing. “The first and the last” is the title of Isaiah's Redeemer, and Jesus takes it as His own. And the One of whom Job said that he lives, as I read it, here names Himself “he that liveth.” To call God living is common in the Old Testament. Job himself swears “As God liveth” ([[Job 27:2]]), with the same word.[@oshb-wlc] What comes next is not common. He adds three words that neither Job nor Isaiah has. “And was dead.”

That is what Job's sentence does not say. He says that his Redeemer lives. He does not say that the Living One would Himself go down to the bars of the pit and come up again. “If a man die, shall he live again?” The first full answer to that question is not about Job, and it is not about us. There is a Man who died and lives again, and He is the Redeemer. President Thomas S. Monson said it in a sentence: “The empty tomb that first Easter morning was the answer to Job’s question.”[@monson-he-is-risen] President Hinckley said of the Savior, “He it was who answered Job’s desperate question.”[@hinckley-empty-tomb]

There is a moment near the end of the book that I read in this light. Out of the whirlwind the LORD asks Job, “Have the gates of death been opened unto thee? or hast thou seen the doors of the shadow of death?” ([[Job 38:17]]). Job had described that country at length, “a land of darkness, as darkness itself” ([[Job 10:22]]). The question expects the answer no. All that he said about death in chapters 7 and 10 and 14 he said from this side of the gates. The One whom John saw speaks from the other side, and holds the keys.

So the change of subject in chapter 19 was not an evasion. It is how the doctrine runs. “But now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept” ([[1 Cor. 15:20]]). “For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive” ([[1 Cor. 15:22]]). The dead do not live again because of anything in the dead. They live because He does: “because I live, ye shall live also” ([[John 14:19]]).

At the grave of Lazarus, Jesus gives Job's question back as a promise, almost in Job's words. “I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live” ([[John 11:25]]). If a man die, shall he live? Though he were dead, yet shall he live. And the reason given is “I am.” Donald Parry sets Job's testimony beside this verse,[@parry-job-type] and President Hinckley went on from Job's words to it.[@hinckley-empty-tomb]

The Bible Dictionary sets beside Job's testimony a verse of Jacob's, “our flesh must waste away and die; nevertheless, in our bodies we shall see God” ([[2 Ne. 9:4]]).[@bd-job-book] Three verses on, Jacob says what would have been true without an infinite atonement: “this flesh must have laid down to rot and to crumble to its mother earth, to rise no more” ([[2 Ne. 9:7]]). That is Job 14:12, man lying down and rising not. I take that verse of Job's to be an accurate account of man as he would be without an infinite atonement.

The rest of his wish is kept as well. “Thou shalt call, and I will answer thee.” Jesus says that “the hour is coming, in the which all that are in the graves shall hear his voice, And shall come forth” ([[John 5:28–29]]), and at Bethany He did it: “he cried with a loud voice, Lazarus, come forth. And he that was dead came forth” ([[John 11:43–44]]). The heading to Job 14 had already called Job's line “the Lord’s call to come forth from the grave.”[@lds-scriptures] The voice that calls is the Son's.

“Thou wilt have a desire to the work of thine hands.” Job said that God would want back what He had made. The Church's footnote at “made” in 10:8 sends the reader to the Topical Guide's “Jesus Christ, Creator,”[@lds-scriptures] and the Restoration tells how thoroughly He wants His work back: “every limb and joint shall be restored to its body; yea, even a hair of the head shall not be lost” ([[Alma 40:23]]). Of the faithful dead it says, “Their sleeping dust was to be restored unto its perfect frame” ([[D&C 138:17]]). Job asked, “wilt thou bring me into dust again?” He will, and then He will bring him out of it.

## Them which are asleep

The last verse of the book is “So Job died, being old and full of days” ([[Job 42:17]]). Everything that was given back to him in chapter 42 he left behind again. The restoration was not the answer to the question of chapter 14, because the man who was restored still died. What answered that question he had said long before, on the ash heap, with nothing restored. His Redeemer lives.

A disciple standing at a grave stands where the book ends. Paul wrote to such disciples “that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope. For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so them also which sleep in Jesus will God bring with him” ([[1 Thes. 4:13–14]]). Look at the ground he gives. It is not a fact about the dead. It is a fact about Jesus, that He died and rose again. The comfort has the shape of Job's sentence.

Mormon gives hope the same footing: “ye shall have hope through the atonement of Christ and the power of his resurrection, to be raised unto life eternal” ([[Moro. 7:41]]), and the Guide to the Scriptures says that scripture often speaks of hope as “anticipation of eternal life through faith in Jesus Christ.”[@gs-hope] Hope in this sense is not what Eliphaz meant, a hope resting on a person's own fear of God and upright ways, and it is not the hope of the tree, something in the nature of things. It is what Job came to when both of those were gone, a confidence that rests on a Person.

Because it rests there, it can be held when nothing in sight supports it. Job held it so. Knowing that his Redeemer lived did not spare him from knowing that he would die, and it does not spare a disciple the graves of those he loves. The two sentences that begin “I know” were spoken by one man. President Hinckley said that every one of us must at some time face the question Job faced, “and because of the Atonement wrought by Jesus Christ we may answer it as Job answered it.”[@hinckley-empty-tomb]

Job answered it by saying who lives.
