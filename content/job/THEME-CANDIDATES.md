# Job: theme candidates

Topics that run across the book and might become theme pages (`STANDARDS.md` §8). **None is written until the author chooses it.** The candidates were Fable's and Codex's lists (`.cache/batch/job/reports/themes-fable.md` and `themes-codex.md`), shown to the author combined on 2026-11-10; only the author's choice is recorded here.

## Author's selection (2026-11-10)

Three pages. The author, of the combined list: “#2 and #3 are good. I also like the Job as a type of Christ.”

### 1. “A Daysman Betwixt Us” (from [[Job 9:33]])

**The arc.** Job begins as the one who stands between God and his children (1:5), and then has no one: “to which of the saints wilt thou turn?” (5:1); God “is not a man, as I am… neither is there any daysman betwixt us” (9:32–33). Each later plea asks for something more exact: a witness in heaven who would “plead for a man with God, as a man pleadeth for his neighbour” (16:19–21); God as His own pledge, “put me in a surety with thee” (17:3); a kinsman who lives and will stand on the earth (19:25). Elihu, without meaning to, adds the ransom (33:24). No third party arrives: the LORD comes Himself, tells Job his own right hand cannot save him (40:14), and makes the man who had no advocate the advocate: “my servant Job shall pray for you: for him will I accept” (42:8). Eliphaz's promise that the guilty are delivered by another's clean hands (22:30) lands on Eliphaz. One verb, *ykḥ* (H3198), ties the pleas: “daysman” (9:33) is its participle, and so is the LORD's “he that reproveth God” for Job (40:2); also 13:3, 15; 16:21; 23:7. “Interpreter” (*mēlîṣ*) is only at 16:20 and 33:23. The comforters' errand, “to mourn with him and to comfort him” (2:11), is done only at 42:11, after the prayer (H5110 only at those two verses); a sentence, not a section.

**Thesis.** Job's pleas, read in order, work out by need alone what the Mediator must be (on both sides at once, in heaven, God answering for God, next of kin and living), and the book answers them not by naming Him but by showing in Job what such a one does. The obstacle Job names at 9:32 is why the Mediator had to be both God and man.

**To Christ:** [[1 Tim. 2:5–6]] (the Church's footnote at 33:24); [[Heb. 7:22, 25]]; [[1 John 2:1]]; [[D&C 45:3–5]]; [[Heb. 4:15–16]]; [[2 Ne. 2:8–9]]; `gs-mediator`; [[Luke 23:34]] beside 42:8–10 as Parry's reading. **Applies:** a disciple who cannot argue their own case has an Advocate; the one pleaded for is sent to pray for those who wronged them ([[Matt. 5:44]]; [[D&C 64:9–10]]), and Job's restoration is dated to that prayer (42:10).

**Keep it apart from the site's other intercessor pages** (Micah's “Plead My Cause,” Ezekiel's “Stand in the Gap,” Numbers' two, Amos, Exodus's “Speak Thou with Us”): its own are the need reasoned out from below by the sufferer, and the sufferer made the intercessor. **Treats 19:25 as one plea among four**; the Resurrection is page 2's.

**Must not claim:** that Job names Christ or knew whom he asked for (the brief: comparison; “Job does not name Him”); that 17:3 teaches anything of the Godhead beyond [[Heb. 7:22]]; that Job is a type of Christ (that argument is page 3's).

### 2. “Upon the Dust” (from [[Job 19:25]]; first proposed as “Where Is Now My Hope?”, which is chapter 17's title)

**The arc.** Job first wants death as rest (3:11–22), then sees it as a land of no return (7:9–10; 10:21–22), then wishes it were a hiding place God would call him out of: “Thou shalt call, and I will answer thee” (14:13–15, the summons of 13:22, grounded in 10:8: “thou wilt have a desire to the work of thine hands”). “Hope” (*tiqwāh*, H8615, 13 times in 12 verses) is the friends' word for the reward of the upright (4:6; 5:16; 8:13; 11:18, 20); Job uses it to lose it: 7:6; “there is hope of a tree” (14:7); “thou destroyest the hope of man” (14:19); “where is now my hope?” (17:15, twice); “mine hope hath he removed like a tree” (19:10). After 19:10 he never says it of himself again (27:8 is of the hypocrite), and fifteen verses later comes “I know that my redeemer liveth.” The hope goes down to rest “in the dust” (17:16); the Redeemer stands “upon the dust” (19:25), the same two words (to confirm in the dossier). The LORD's “Have the gates of death been opened unto thee?” (38:17) shows how little Job could see; he dies “full of days” (42:17).

**Thesis.** Job's testimony of the Redeemer is not a recovery of hope: it comes after his hope has died, and a living Person takes its place, standing on the ground where the hope was buried.

**To Christ:** the Church's headings for 14 and 19 and the footnotes at 19:25; `bd-job-book` with [[2 Ne. 9:4]]; `monson-he-is-risen`; [[Alma 11:42–44]]; [[1 Cor. 15:19–22]]; [[1 Pet. 1:3]]; [[Moro. 7:41]]; the page's own: [[John 5:25–29]] and [[John 11:43]] beside 14:15; [[Rev. 1:17–18]] beside 19:25 and 38:17 (the Hebrew of [[Isa. 44:6]] to check before leaning on it). **Applies:** a disciple at a grave does not sorrow “as others which have no hope” ([[1 Thes. 4:13]]); that hope can be held, as Job held it, with nothing yet restored.

**Must not claim:** that the Hebrew of 19:26 is settled (the brief; Meservy's sentence stands); that Job understood the Resurrection as later revelation gives it; the Greek ending of 42:17 as scripture; that the children of 42:13 prove a resurrection (the Student Manual's reading, as the older reading it quotes).

### 3. “My Servant Job”: Job as a type of Christ (from [[Job 1:8]]; [[Job 42:8]])

**A different kind of essay, by the author's leave:** it may include the list of likenesses, but it is not only a list (author, 2026-11-10: “That can be a different kind of essay if it needs to be; one that incorporates a list of similar attributes. It shouldn't be just a list, but that can be part of it”).

**The material.** The LORD calls Job “my servant” six times, at the beginning and the end (1:8; 2:3; 42:7; 42:8 three times; H5650). Latter-day Saint authors read Job as a type: Parry (`parry-job-type`, with a table), Farley (`farley-job-parallels`), Stirling (`stirling-job` [182]), KnoWhy #640 [15]; the brief lists the verses they name (1:1, 5; 1:22; 19:13–19; 30:9–10; 42:8–10, 12). Among them: blameless and upright (1:1), struck “without cause” (2:3; [[John 15:25]]), forsaken by kin and friends, even his own servant will not answer him (19:13–19), mocked and spat on (30:9–10; 16:10), intercession for those who wronged him (42:8–10), and the double after the suffering (42:10, 12).

**Proposed shape (the coordinator's, for the outliner):** the likenesses carry the first half; the argument is in where the type stops. Job protests his innocence and demands a hearing; the Servant “opened not his mouth” ([[Isa. 53:7]]). Job is “perfect” but repents “in dust and ashes” (42:6), and needs the very daysman he could not find; Christ is that daysman and “did no sin” ([[D&C 45:4]]). Job's suffering redeems no one; his prayer for the friends is accepted because the LORD accepts *him* (42:8). The thesis to be tested: a type shows Christ as much by where it falls short as by where it matches, and Job's shortfalls are exactly what pages 1 and 2 show him asking for. Link Isaiah's “Servant Songs” page for Isa. 52–53 rather than repeat it.

**Overlap:** 42:8 belongs to page 1 as the answer to “no daysman”; this page points to it and does not argue it again. 2:3 (“without cause”) carries what was usable of the unchosen “For Nought.”

**The reading is the page's to make** (author, 2026-11-10: “It is just a reading, but it isn't against church doctrine. Remember, these themes are opportunities to introduce new connections. This may be new to the LDS community, but it isn't contrary to the doctrine”). The essay argues Job as a type plainly, as its own reading under `STANDARDS.md` §8 (said once, then argued, not hedged); a likeness Parry, Farley or Stirling already drew is credited to them, and the shortfall argument is the page's own. The brief's rule that a chapter gives each parallel “as that author's, in a clause” is for chapter notes, not this page.

**Must not claim:** that Job's suffering atoned or accomplished anything for others; that Job was sinless; that his restoration is the promised result of faithful suffering.

**Not chosen:** “For Nought” (1:9; 2:3; 9:17; 22:6; 41:11; author: “rather obvious and already covered in recent General Conference addresses”); “To Mourn with Him and to Comfort Him” (its 2:11/42:11 pair goes in page 1 as a sentence); and the rest of both lists.
