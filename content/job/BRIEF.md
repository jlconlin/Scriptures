Book-level decisions for Job. The site-wide standard is in `STANDARDS.md`; this file records what is particular to Job. Writers, checkers, and reviewers read both before starting a chapter. Ecclesiastes' brief (`content/ecclesiastes/BRIEF.md`) is the closest model: another book of wisdom, mostly one voice reasoning in poetry, where the speaker's words are not all doctrine. Read “Explained once: where” before writing.

## What the author has decided

Nothing for Job in particular beyond choosing it. The standing rules are in `STANDARDS.md` §1 and `AUTHORING.md`. How three land on this book:

- **Newest trumps oldest.** *Scripture Helps: Old Testament* (2025; `scripture-helps-job`) covers chapters 1–3, 12–14, 19, 21–24, 38–40 and 42, with sections on what the book is [4]–[9], who Job was [10]–[11], whether the Lord conversed with Satan [13], Job's lament [16], the Redeemer [17]–[21], the friends' accusations [23]–[24], what the Lord taught Job [26]–[31], the morning stars [32], behemoth and leviathan [33], and “abhor myself” [34]. The 2026 seminary and institute manuals (`ot-seminary-2026-job-1-13`, `-14-19`, `-21-42`; `institute-ot-2026-job`) agree with it. The Student Manual (`ot-manual-job`, 1981, mostly Keith Meservy's 1978 Sperry paper) is older; where they differ (the Satan scene, above all), follow *Scripture Helps*.
- **Don't replicate the Gospel Library.** *Scripture Helps*' sections and the Bible Dictionary's entry (`bd-job-book`) are beside the verse. A note at 1:1, 1:6, 3:1, 19:25, 22, 38:7, 40:15, 41:1 or 42:6 goes where they stop (the endnote's study, the Hebrew, the scripture that takes the verse up, the Joseph Smith Papers), or it is not written. Chapters 4–11, 15–18, 20, 25–37 and 41 have no *Scripture Helps*.
- **Quote the Church's modern material sparingly.** Elder Renlund's article (`renlund-trust-god`), which *Scripture Helps* and every seminary lesson quote, will be wanted in many chapters: its couplet “All sin causes suffering, but not all suffering is caused by sin” [9] is quoted once, at 4:7, and pointed to elsewhere; nothing *Scripture Helps* already prints of it is printed again. *Come, Follow Me* (`cfm-ot-2026-33`, Aug. 10–16) is what the Church asks a reader to see, not commentary.

## What a chapter is for here

Job is 1,070 verses in 42 chapters: a prose story (1–2), a long argument in poetry (3–41), and a prose ending (42:7–17). Most chapters are one speech, or a speech and its answer. The reader's questions, by part (a start, not a limit):

- **1–2:** *Who was Job, where is Uz, and when did he live? What does “perfect” mean? Who are the “sons of God,” and did God really bargain with Satan? Why does Job offer sacrifices for his children? Why does Job's wife say “curse God, and die,” and why does the Hebrew say “bless”? Who are the three friends, and why do they sit silent seven days?*
- **3–14:** *Why does Job curse his birthday, and is that sin? What did Eliphaz see in the night (4:12–21)? What is a “daysman” (9:33)? Why does Job want to take God to court? What does “Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him” mean, and does the Hebrew say that? What does Job think happens at death (7:9; 14:7–14)?*
- **15–21:** *Why do the friends grow harsher? What is the “witness in heaven” (16:19) and the pledge Job asks for (17:3)? Who is Job's Redeemer, does 19:26 teach the resurrection, and what does the Hebrew say? Why does Job say the wicked prosper (21)?*
- **22–31:** *What are Eliphaz's charges in 22, and are they true? Why does the third round break off (24–27)? Who speaks chapter 28, and what is wisdom? What is the oath of 31, and why does Job end by demanding an answer?*
- **32–37:** *Who is Elihu, why is he angry, and is he right? Why does no one answer him, and why is he missing from the ending? What is the “ransom” (33:24)?*
- **38–42:** *Why does God answer with questions and not with reasons? Who are the morning stars and the sons of God (38:7)? What are behemoth and leviathan? What did Job repent of, and does he “abhor” himself? What had Job said that was right (42:7)? Why is everything doubled but the children? Why are only the daughters named?*

(The `christ` section then applies the chapter to the reader, in statements: `STANDARDS.md` §1.)

**The speeches are speeches.** A chapter of Job is usually one man's answer to another's. Each chapter's `setting` says who is speaking, in which round, and what he is answering; `thread` follows his argument as a discourse is followed in Proverbs 1–9, and says where it turns. Don't summarize a speech verse by verse in `thread`: name what the speaker is claiming and where he moves.

**Graphic passages** (Job's sores, 2:7–8; 7:5; 19:20; 30:17–30): describe rather than quote at length (`STANDARDS.md` §3).

## Book-wide conventions

**What in this brief is a lead and not a fact.** Words in quotation marks below were copied from the page named. The rest (what a Hebrew word can mean, which scripture takes up which line) is the drafter's direction of where to look. None of it goes into a chapter until the writer has read it on a page and given it a ledger row (`STANDARDS.md` §4).

- **`when`.** The text dates nothing, and the Church's sources say “We do not know when or where the book of Job was written” (`seminary-intro-job` [5]). **Proposed:** `when` names the speaker and the moment in the book: “The prologue: Job in the land of Uz” (1–2), “Job's lament” (3), “The first round: Eliphaz” (4–5), “The first round: Job answers Eliphaz” (6–7), and so on; “Elihu's first speech” (32–33); “The LORD out of the whirlwind” (38–41); “Job's answer and the ending” (42). No B.C. year.
- **No Context link** (`noPeriod` in `book.yaml`): the volume timeline says “Job is given no period” (`/old-testament/guides/israels-story/#timeline`). A chapter that needs the patriarchal guess says so in a clause, as *Scripture Helps*' (“Some clues from the text suggest that Job may have lived around the same time as the patriarchs,” [11]), and goes no further.
- **Who is speaking.** Every speech opens with a heading verse (“Then answered Eliphaz the Temanite, and said,” 4:1). Name the speaker in `setting`, and in a note say “Eliphaz says,” “Job answers,” never “the book says” or “scripture teaches” of a friend's or Elihu's words. **The friends' words are not doctrine** (“Contested,” the friends): a note gives what a friend claims, and where the claim is the one the LORD rejects at 42:7, says so in a clause pointing to 4:7 or 42:7, not in every note. A friend's line that is true in itself (Paul quotes Eliphaz at [[1 Cor. 3:19]], from 5:13; Joseph Smith's 1839 letter echoes 5:13 and 8:14, `jsp-letter-1839-03-20` notes 43–44) is given as true, with where scripture uses it, and the note says what the friend did with it.
- **Job's words are not all doctrine either.** God says Job spoke “the thing that is right” (42:7) and also that he darkened counsel “by words without knowledge” (38:2), and Job takes back what he said (40:4–5; 42:3–6). So a note says *what Job claims* (“Job says God destroys the perfect and the wicked alike,” 9:22) without endorsing it, and gives his testimonies (13:15; 14:14; 16:19; 19:25–27; 23:10) their full weight as the Church's sources do.
- **Names of God.** The poetry says *'Eloah*, *'El* and *Shaddai* (“the Almighty,” 31 verses in 19 chapters); “the LORD” (*YHWH*) stands in the prose frame and the headings of the LORD's speeches (38:1; 40:1, 3, 6; 42:1, 7–12) and only once in the dialogue (12:9). The Gospel Library has “the Lord” (*'Adonai*) at 28:28. A note says so only where it answers a question (12:9, 28:28, 38:1); check with `occurrences.mjs job "LORD"` and `hebrew.mjs`.
- **Hebrew only where it answers a reader's question** (`STANDARDS.md` §3). Job's poetry is among the hardest Hebrew in the Bible, and the King James is often obscure; modern translations differ widely. Words that pay: *tām*, “perfect” (1:1; *Scripture Helps* [10]); *bērēk*, “bless,” where the King James says “curse” (1:5, 11; 2:5, 9); *haśśāṭān* (1:6; the seminary manual: “*hassatan*, meaning ‘the adversary,’” `seminary-ot-teacher-107` [14]); *Shaddai* (5:17); the “daysman,” *môkîaḥ* (9:33; footnote “arbiter”); the written and the read text at 13:15 (“Contested”); *gōʾēl* (19:25); *mibbĕśārî*, “from my flesh” (19:26; “Contested”); *tiqwāh*, “thread” and “hope” at 7:6–7 (`janus` wordplay, Noegel through Lindsay's review in *Interpreter* 27, not added to `sources.yaml`: read and propose it if a note uses it); *ʾemʾas* and *niḥamtî* at 42:6 (“Contested”). Not: the Hebrew behind an ordinary English word, or the hapax count. No more than about a quarter of a chapter's notes are Language notes. Where the King James is simply obscure, a note may give “a modern translation” (`nrsvue`, `niv`, `esv`) to say what the line means, KJV first.
- **The Joseph Smith Translation** changes “the sons of God” to “the children of God” at 1:6 and 2:1 (the footnotes, `fn1.txt`, `fn2.txt`; *Scripture Helps* endnote 10); there is no other JST reading in Job. The note at 1:6 gives it in a clause; 38:7 is not changed.
- **The Septuagint** is Brenton on Bible Hub (`biblehub.com/sep/job/<c>.htm`, cited `lxx-rahlfs`). It has a long speech for Job's wife at 2:9 and an addition after 42:17 (that Job “will rise again with those whom the Lord raises up,” and that his name before was Jobab, an Edomite, “fifth from Abraam”). Give them only where a reader would ask (2:9; 42:17), as the Greek text's, and say what is in the Hebrew. Any claim about how much shorter the Greek is, or about the Dead Sea Scrolls' copies and Targum of Job, needs a source read in the session; none was found for this brief.
- **Scripture reads Job.** The Church's own footnotes are the first finder (`fn<n>.txt`). Job is named three times elsewhere: [[Ezek. 14:14]] (on the site: agree), [[James 5:11]] (“the patience of Job”; Tanner: “might better be translated ‘steadfast,’” `tanner-considered-job` [13]: check the Greek before using it), and [[D&C 121:10]]. Restoration scripture beside the book: Liberty Jail ([[D&C 121:1–10]]; [[D&C 122:5–9]]); the premortal council ([[Abr. 3:22–26]]; [[D&C 38:1]]) at 38:7; [[2 Ne. 2:11]] (opposition) and [[Ether 12:27]] where *Come, Follow Me* sets them ([12]–[18]); [[D&C 10:27]] beside 1:7 (`frederick-old-wine` [89]: “to allude to Satan in general”); D&C 128:23, where Joseph Smith turns 38:7 into praise (Frederick [29]–[32]). Paul quotes 5:13 ([[1 Cor. 3:19]]). **No comparison panel.**
- **The other books on the site.** Job's lament is set beside Jeremiah's ([[Jer. 20:14]]), the satan beside Zechariah's ([[Zech. 3:1]]), the council beside Psalm 82, leviathan beside Isaiah 27:1 and 51:9, the doubled payment beside Isaiah 40:2, the man “perfect” beside Noah and Jacob, “What is man?” (7:17) beside Psalm 8:4 and the Psalms page “What Is Man?”, the righteous sufferer beside Psalm 73 and Ecclesiastes 7:15 and 8:17. Run `occurrences.mjs job "<phrase>" --site all` before writing a note on a verse another book has already quoted; the site has already written over a hundred sentences that name Job.
- **The `christ` section.** Begin from what scripture and the Church's sources say, in this order: the Redeemer who lives and will stand on the earth ([[Job 19:25]]; the chapter heading; the Topical Guide's “Jesus Christ, Redeemer” at the footnote; `bd-job-book`; Renlund [31]–[34]); the Resurrection that answers 14:14 (Monson, `monson-he-is-risen` [3], [37]); “The Son of Man hath descended below them all. Art thou greater than he?” ([[D&C 122:8]]); the Savior who took on Himself “the pain and sickness and suffering of us all—even those of us who appear to deserve our suffering” (Uchtdorf [27]). **Job as a type of Christ** is a reading of Latter-day Saint authors, not of the Church's manuals (no Church source read calls Job a type): Parry (`parry-job-type`), Farley (`farley-job-parallels`), Stirling (`stirling-job` [182]: “many have rightly considered Job to be a type of Christ”; Job's mediation for his friends “parallels Christ's as mediator for mankind”), KnoWhy #640 [15]. Give a parallel as that author's, in a clause, at the verse he names (1:1, 5; 1:22; 19:13–19; 30:9–10; 42:8–10, 12), never as the writer's own. **Job's longings for an arbiter (9:33), a witness in heaven (16:19), a surety (17:3) and a Redeemer (19:25)** are his words in his trouble: a chapter may set them beside what scripture says Christ is ([[1 Tim. 2:5]]; [[Heb. 7:22]]; [[D&C 45:3–5]]), worded as comparison, and says plainly that Job does not name Him. Then one to three plain sentences of application.
- **Theme candidates** come at the end of the book (`AUTHORING.md`), and none is written before the author chooses it. Arcs a reviewer may report: the courtroom (Job's demand for a hearing, 9; 13; 23; 31, and the LORD's answer); “the hedge” (1:10; 3:23; 19:8); the friends who come to comfort and stay to accuse (2:11–13 to 42:7–10); what Job knows of death (7:9; 14:14; 19:25–27).

**Habits to avoid** (Ezekiel's brief has the examples): say a thing once in a chapter; don't re-explain what another chapter explains, point with a `[[reference]]`; a Witness of Christ does not open by saying what the chapter lacks; a note's first sentence gives the answer, since the site shows it alone as the note's card; a reading stays a reading in every sentence; “In plain words” adds nothing to the verse; application only in `christ`, as statements resting on what it cites, never questions or exhortation; **no self-help commentary** (the seminary lessons' applications to a student's trials are applications: a note says what the verse meant and how scripture and the Church read it); **don't answer the friends in every note**: that the LORD rejected their reasoning is said at 4:7 and 42:7 and pointed to, not appended to each speech; **don't make Job an exhibit of patience**: the book's Job argues, protests and demands a hearing, and the Church's sources honor his faith without hiding that (Tanner [31]; *Scripture Helps* [16]; Eyring [35]).

## Contested and sensitive questions

One approach for each, so that 42 chapters say the same thing. Quotations were copied from the pages named on 2026-11-10 (by the research agents; the dossiers give the paragraphs); re-read the page before using one.

- **Who was Job, and when (1:1; 42:16–17).** The Church's sources: Job was real. “Some have wondered if Job was a fictional character, but both ancient scripture and modern revelation clarify that Job was a real person” (`seminary-ot-teacher-107` [11]); “It is important to understand that Job was a real person (see Doctrine and Covenants 121:10), but Job's life is told using some poetic elements” (`ot-seminary-2026-job-1-13` [22]); *Scripture Helps*' endnote 7: “because both the scriptures and modern prophets treat Job as a real historical figure, we can have confidence in his existence.” Who wrote it and when: “We do not know who wrote the book of Job” and “We do not know when or where” (`seminary-intro-job` [4]–[5]). Job “was likely not an Israelite”; Uz is “sometimes associated with Edom”; “Some clues from the text suggest that Job may have lived around the same time as the patriarchs” (*Scripture Helps* [11]); Uz, “probably east of Palestine and north of Edom” (`bd-uz`). Latter-day Saint scholars: Tanner “accept[s] the fact of Job's existence” and sees “evident literary fashioning,” “mixing both fact and fable” [9]–[10]; Stirling, “Job may well have been a historical figure,” but the book is “an extremely sophisticated literary composition” [44]; Meservy, in the Student Manual, that D&C 121:10 settles it, since “one cannot compare real with unreal things” (`ot-manual-job` [66]). Adams links the Elihu of [[D&C 84:8–9]] to Job's Elihu and so dates the book before Moses (`adams-job-message` [11]); the Bible Dictionary lists them as different men (`bd-elihu` [1], [4]). **Proposed:** chapter 1's `setting` and the note at 1:1 say Job was a real man, as the Church's sources and D&C 121:10 do; that the book tells his story in poetry framed by prose, and that its author and date are unknown; Uz and the non-Israelite setting in a sentence (KnoWhy #864 [4] on the friends' homes). Tanner's “fact and fable” and Stirling's “literary composition” are not set beside the Church's statement as an equal reading; a clause may say that the book's form (the speeches in poetry, the round numbers) is literary, as the 2026 manual does. **No chapter argues a date**, and the D&C 84 Elihu is not used.
- **God and Satan in the council (1:6–12; 2:1–7).** The Church's newest sources agree: “The conversations between the Lord and Satan in the book of Job likely do not describe an actual interaction between them. These conversations are best understood as a poetic way of conveying Satan's role as our adversary” (*Scripture Helps* [13]); “they don't describe an actual interaction between God and Satan” (`cfm-ot-2026-33` [6]); “the Lord does not really make agreements with Satan… the Lord has power over Satan and has no need to bargain with him” (`seminary-ot-teacher-107` [18]); the 2026 seminary and institute manuals the same. The Student Manual said “may be a poetic way” (`ot-manual-job` [67]) and quoted Meservy reading it literally ([68]); *Scripture Helps* is newer. *Scripture Helps*' endnote 10 adds the divine council and the JST's “children of God,” and reports that “the Satan” in the Hebrew “may indicate that the adversary in this account was an adversarial member of the divine council rather than Satan himself,” citing two study Bibles (not readable). **Proposed:** the note at 1:6 gives the Church's reading first, in the site's words with a phrase of *Scripture Helps*; the council and “the satan” in a clause each, pointing to `[[Ps. 82:1]]` and `[[Zech. 3:1]]`; the JST in a clause; endnote 10's possibility as what the Church's study aid reports, in a sentence. **No note builds on the bargain** (God “moved against him,” 2:3) as a statement of what God does; a reader's question at 2:3 is answered from 1:6. Agency: “Satan was allowed to afflict all that Job had” (`seminary-ot-teacher-107` [17]); “fire of God” (1:16) is the messenger's words, and the note says so.
- **The friends: suffering as punishment (4–27; 42:7).** *Scripture Helps*: the friends “believed the common misconception that suffering must be God's punishment for disobedience,” and “the Lord called these three friends to repentance for their false ideas about His justice” [24], with Uchtdorf's “It is unworthy of us as Christians to think that those who suffer deserve their suffering” (`uchtdorf-you-are-my-hands` [27]). Renlund: “All sin causes suffering, but not all suffering is caused by sin” [9]; “Job and his friends thought that all suffering was caused by sin. That, too, is fundamentally false” [11]. The Guide: affliction “does not necessarily mean that a person has sinned” (`gs-job` [2]). [[D&C 121:10]]: “thy friends do not contend against thee, neither charge thee with transgression, as they did Job”; the Joseph Smith Papers' note 39 to that line lists the accusing speeches, “Job chaps. 4–5, 8, 11, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 32–37” (`jsp-letter-1839-03-20`). Joseph Smith in 1841: “it is very Natureal for a man when he sees his fellow man afflicted his Natureal conclusion is that he is suffering the Rath of an angry God & turn from him in haste not knowing the purposes of God” (`jsp-discourse-1841-03-28`, p. 17). Tanner: their counsel was “based on human reason about suffering in general—upon hearsay, as it were—not upon revelation” [36]. **Proposed:** the note at 4:7 (Eliphaz's “who ever perished, being innocent?”) carries the full answer, once: what the friends assume, why the reader knows it is false (1:1, 8; 2:3), Renlund's couplet, D&C 121:10 with the Papers' note, and the LORD's verdict at 42:7 pointed to. Each later speech's `setting` says in a clause what the friend adds (Bildad: the children died for their sin, 8:4; Zophar: Job deserves worse, 11:6; Eliphaz: a list of invented crimes, 22:5–9). **No note mocks the friends**: they came to mourn, sat seven days in silence (2:11–13; `ot-seminary-2026-job-1-13` [68]), and say much that is true in general; their fault is applying it to Job.
- **“Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him” (13:15).** The Church reads the King James as one of Job's great testimonies (the chapter heading; `gs-job` [4]; `bd-job-book` [3]; the seminary introduction [6]; *Come, Follow Me* [4]; Meservy calls it the book's “high point,” `ot-manual-job` [52]). The Hebrew has a famous difference between the written and the read text: the consonants write *lōʾ*, “not,” and the Masoretes' marginal reading, which the King James follows, is *lô*, “for him” (`hebrew.mjs job 13:15` prints both); a modern translation follows the written text, “See, he will kill me; I have no hope” (`nrsvue`). The second half, “I will maintain mine own ways before him,” is Job's resolve to argue his case to God's face (Davis, `ot-minute-job-4` [30]). Delitzsch (`delitzsch-job`, chapter 13) is the older reading of the verse. **Proposed:** the note at 13:15 says first what the verse means as the Church reads it; then, because a reader of a modern Bible will find it, that the Hebrew text is written one way and read the other, with *qere* and *ketiv* defined once, and that the King James follows the reading the Hebrew tradition itself supplied; then what the second half adds (the trust is the trust of a man who will still plead his case). The note does not say the King James is wrong, and does not decide between the two beyond saying which the tradition reads.
- **The Redeemer and “in my flesh” (19:25–27).** The Church's sources read it as testimony of Christ and of the Resurrection: the chapter heading (“I know that my Redeemer lives—Job prophesies that he will be resurrected and that in his flesh he will see God”); the Bible Dictionary, “Job's assurance of the bodily resurrection and his testimony of the Redeemer (19:25–27; see also 2 Ne. 9:4) are one of the high points of the book” [3]; the seminary lessons (`seminary-ot-teacher-108` [25]–[26], [61]); Renlund [31]–[34]. *Scripture Helps* [17]: a redeemer was “usually a relative” who restored “possessions, status in society, or personal freedom,” and Job “was expressing his faith that he would ultimately be vindicated” (with endnote 19 citing study Bibles). The Hebrew of 19:26 is hard: Meservy, in the Student Manual, “The Hebrew text says, ‘from my flesh,’ and this can be interpreted in either sense,” and “Latter-day Saints do not depend upon this passage to establish their belief in a literal resurrection, but point to it as one more glorious affirmation of it” (`ot-manual-job` [76]–[78]); Stirling reads it as resurrection and seeing God, and that the Redeemer “will be God Himself” [97], from Janzen, Andersen, Hartley and Wilson (his note 21). Davis: “Job does not say who this redeemer is” [44]. **Proposed:** the note at 19:25 says what a *gōʾēl* is in a clause, linking `[[Lev. 25:25]]` and `[[Ruth 2:20]]`, then gives Job's testimony its full weight as the Church reads it, with the Bible Dictionary's pairing with [[2 Ne. 9:4]]; the note at 19:26 gives the King James and the Hebrew's “from my flesh” as Meservy states it, says the line is hard and both senses are possible, and gives his sentence on how Latter-day Saints use it. The resurrection is then taught from the scriptures that teach it plainly ([[Alma 11:42–44]], which *Come, Follow Me* sets beside 14:14; [[1 Cor. 15:20–22]]). Handel's aria and the hymn (“I Know That My Redeemer Lives,” *Hymns* 136) are reception history: not used, unless `explore` names the hymn once.
- **Elihu (32–37).** The Church's sources describe him differently: he “reproves Job's three friends and reasons with Job” (`bd-elihu` [1]); he “condemns Job for reasons other than the reasons of the first three friends” (`gs-job` [3]); his “main thesis is that God will ‘not pervert judgment.’ Job makes no reply to him” (`bd-job-book` [1]); he “thinks he can do a better job of solving the riddle” (`ot-manual-job` [64]); the Papers' editors list 32–37 among the speeches that charged Job with transgression (note 39). Tanner: Elihu “stands under the same divine disapproval as the other comforters,” and “most commentators see his discourse as a later interpolation” (his note 15). Davis finds his point on “profit” insightful and compares King Benjamin ([[Mosiah 2:21]]; `ot-minute-job-35` [4]–[6]). Stirling reads Elihu as Satan in disguise, after Freedman [141]–[143]. **Proposed:** the note at 32:2 says who he is (the text gives him a father and a clan, as no friend has), why he is angry (32:2–3), and the Bible Dictionary's summary; that the LORD neither answers him nor names him in 42:7; and, in a sentence, that many scholars think his speeches were added later (Tanner's note, as his report). His speeches are read for what they say: where he says something true (God speaks in dreams, 33:14–18; the ransom, 33:24; God's greatness in the storm, 36–37), give it, as his; where he repeats the friends' charge (34:7–9, 35–37), say so. **Elihu as Satan is not used.**
- **Chapter 28.** No Church source treats it. The text: 27:1 begins “Job continued his parable,” and 29:1 repeats it; no new speaker is named in 28. Stirling: “Although the text does not make it explicit, I consider chapter 28 to be Job's hymn to wisdom” [116]. Cornwall: “thought and language as beautiful as anything in the Bible” [39]. **Proposed:** chapter 28's `setting` says it stands inside Job's speech by the headings (27:1; 29:1) and changes tone, a poem on where wisdom is found; the note at 28:28 gives its answer (“the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom”) and links Proverbs 1:7 and Psalm 111:10 on the site. Don't argue who wrote it.
- **The third round (24–27).** It is short and uneven: Bildad's 25 is six verses, Zophar has no third speech, and 24:18–24 and 27:13–23 sound like the friends' doctrine in Job's mouth. Davis: “some scholars… argue that earlier versions of the book may have been corrupted during copying and transmission… These are certainly possibilities, but our responsibility is to read the text as it stands” (`ot-minute-job-4` [55]); Stirling reads the breakdown as the point (“Meaningful dialogue has aborted,” [112]) and 27:13–23 as Job quoting Zophar (20:29) [113]. **Proposed:** chapter 25's `setting` says what is missing and that scholars have proposed rearranging the speeches, then reads the text as the King James has it; 27:13 says Job's words repeat Zophar's (20:29) and gives Stirling's reading as his. **No chapter reassigns verses to another speaker.**
- **Why God answers with questions (38–41).** *Scripture Helps* [26]–[31] (Renlund: God “did not defend Himself, explain Job's suffering, or respond to Job's claims of innocence”); Renlund: the book “never answers the why” [8]; the seminary: “He did not seem, however, to answer the questions… Instead, the Lord gave answers in the form of more questions” (`seminary-ot-teacher-109` [60]); Tanner: the book is “at bottom about the need for personal revelation” [40], and Job's craving “is satisfied by the vision of God” (`bd-job-book` [1]); Davis: God cares for wild creatures and rains on uninhabited land (`ot-minute-job-38` [8]). **Proposed:** the note at 38:1 says the LORD answers by showing Job the creation he did not make and does not govern, that the speech gives no reason for Job's suffering, and what Job learns from it (42:2–5); Renlund's words are beside 38:1 in *Scripture Helps*, so a phrase at most.
- **The morning stars and the sons of God (38:4–7).** The Church reads the verse as the premortal life: “a passage confirming the reality of the premortal life” (`seminary-intro-job` [7]); *Scripture Helps* [32], with Monson (`monson-he-is-risen` [21]); Joseph Smith, reading Job 38 in 1841, quoted “where wast thou when I Laid the foundation of the Earth” as “Evedencing that Job was in Existing somewhere at that time” (`jsp-discourse-1841-03-28`, pp. 17–18). The Hebrew “sons of God” are the heavenly beings of 1:6 (`[[Gen. 6:2]]` on the site). **Proposed:** the note at 38:7 gives the Church's reading and Joseph Smith's words (the Papers, not *Scripture Helps*, which is beside the verse), with [[Abr. 3:22–26]]; the irony that Stirling sees in 38:4 (that Job “may well have been” there, [47]) in a clause, as his. A modern translation's “heavenly beings” is not used to narrow the Church's reading.
- **Behemoth and leviathan (40:15–41:34).** *Scripture Helps* [33]: behemoth is “the plural form of ‘beast,’” “possibly a poetic reference to a hippopotamus or some mythological beast”; leviathan “possibly… a crocodile or a mythological creature described in ancient Canaanite texts”; the Bible Dictionary (`bd-behemoth`, `bd-leviathan`). Stirling: they “typify the proud (see Job 41:34) and hard-hearted” [157]. Isaiah 27:1 and 51:9 on the site already explain leviathan and Rahab. **Proposed:** the notes at 40:15 and 41:1 go where *Scripture Helps* stops: why the LORD ends with two creatures no man can tame (40:24; 41:1–10, 34), what “king over all the children of pride” (41:34) says, and the Canaanite background only as far as a source read gives it. No identification is given as certain.
- **“I abhor myself, and repent” (42:6).** *Scripture Helps* [34]: “myself” is italic in the King James; the verb “can also mean to renounce or reject”; “Job may have been saying, ‘Therefore do I recant [or renounce], and I repent in dust and ashes,’” with Eyring: “After Job repented of calling God unfair” (`eyring-holiness-plan` [35]). The seminary manual: “Job did not hate himself. Rather, he humbly acknowledged his weaknesses, sins, and limitations” (`seminary-ot-teacher-109` [29]). Renlund: “he repented of attributing his suffering to sin” [19]. Stirling prefers Janzen's “I… change my mind concerning dust and ashes” [160], [171], against “most translations.” **Proposed:** the note at 42:6 goes where *Scripture Helps* stops: the second verb (*niḥamtî*, “repent,” also “be comforted” or “change one's mind”: check BDB 5162) and what “dust and ashes” recalls (2:8; 30:19); then what the Church's sources say Job repented of, Eyring's and Renlund's readings side by side as two apostles' readings (they differ, and neither is set above the other); Stirling's reading in a clause, as his.
- **“The thing that is right” (42:7–8).** The Bible Dictionary: Job “is declared by God to have spoken the thing that is right concerning the divine government (42:7) in saying that there is a mystery in the incidence of suffering that only a fresh revelation can solve” [1]; Tanner: “one can say something that is formally wrong but personally right (as did Job), and something formally correct but personally wrong (as did the comforters)” [32]; Stirling calls the verdict “determinative for interpreting the book” [174]; Davis: “Job is less wrong” (`ot-minute-job-overview` [8]). The seminary lesson draws the application that “The Lord wants us to pray for those who misjudge us” (`seminary-ot-teacher-109` [36]). **Proposed:** the note at 42:7 gives the Bible Dictionary's reading in its own words, then Tanner's, as his; it does not say all Job's words were approved (38:2; 40:2–5). The application belongs in `christ`.
- **The double and the children (42:10–17).** The text doubles the animals (1:3; 42:12) and gives the same number of children (42:13). The Student Manual quotes Keil and Delitzsch: the children are “also so far a doubling, as deceased children also, according to the Old Testament view, are not absolutely lost” (`ot-manual-job` [86]–[88]); Stirling sets the double beside the thief's restitution of [[Exod. 22:4]] [176]; Parry beside the firstborn's double portion ([[Deut. 21:17]]; `parry-job-type` [7]). The daughters are named and inherit “among their brethren” (42:15; Stirling [176], beside [[Num. 27:8]], where the site already has a note). **Proposed:** the note at 42:12–13 gives the count from the text and the Student Manual's reading as the older reading it quotes; Stirling's and Parry's as theirs, in a clause each; no note says the first children were “replaced.” The note at 42:15 says what is unusual from the text and the Numbers note.
- **Liberty Jail (D&C 121–122).** “Thou art not yet as Job” ([[D&C 121:10]]) comes from Joseph Smith's letter of 20 March 1839 (`jsp-letter-1839-03-20`, p. 8, with the editors' note 39); “all these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good” ([[D&C 122:7]]) from the letter of about 22 March, where “and shall be for thy good” is Joseph Smith's own insertion in the draft (`jsp-letter-1839-03-22`, p. 4, note 42). The Guide ties the book to D&C 122: affliction may be “for experience, discipline, and instruction as well as for punishment” (`gs-job` [2]); Eyring sets the two side by side (`eyring-holiness-plan` [22]–[29]). Tanner: “The Lord's purpose here is simply to remind Joseph that things could be worse, not to verify Job's existence” (his note 6); Meservy the opposite (`ot-manual-job` [66]). **Proposed:** D&C 121:10 is explained in full at 4:7 (above); D&C 122:7–8 at 23:10, with the Papers for the insertion; 1:1's note uses D&C 121:10 for Job's reality in a clause, without the argument between Tanner and Meservy. Chapter 42's `christ` section may set D&C 122:8 beside the book once.
- **The temple reading.** Stirling reads the book as “a literary analogue of the temple endowment ritual” [30], summarized in KnoWhy #640; he is “unaware of any evidence that Joseph Smith used the book of Job in developing the temple endowment” [179]. **Proposed:** not in the notes. One `explore` bullet, in chapter 38 or 42, may point to Stirling's essay as a temple reading of the whole book, in his name. Nothing about the endowment's content is described.

## Settled without the author

Decided by the standard, not asked of the author (Psalms' brief has the rule):

- **No Church, BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* source answers a reader's real question, and an older commentary does:** give its answer as “one older reading,” within the limit of a third of the notes. The Latter-day Saint studies cluster on 1–3, 13–14, 19, 38–42 and the book as a whole (*Scripture Helps*, the manuals, Tanner, Stirling, Davis, the KnoWhys). **Chapters 4–11, 15–18, 20, 24–27 and 29–37 will lean on the text, the footnotes, the scriptures that take it up, modern translations for the sense of a hard line, and Delitzsch**, and need fewer notes; a short chapter resting on the text is better than one filled with the lexicon.
- **A scholar's reading that a BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* author prints** (Janzen and Pope in Stirling, Terrien in Tanner, Unger in Adams, Alter in Davis) is cited as that author's, as one reading, beside the Church's, never above it. **Stirling** is a physician who says he has “no special expertise in Hebrew” and leans on Janzen and Pope throughout: use his readings as his, and never for a point about the Hebrew.
- **Two faithful sources read a verse differently** (42:6, what Job repented of; Elihu): give both, the Church's first, without choosing beyond what the standard settles.
- **The Church's footnote points somewhere the note does not go:** leave it.
