## Plan for the next book

Three agents write a chapter. **Opus writes and reviews** (author, 2026-10-07: “I think this settles it that Opus is the right model for writing”, after the test below), and the mechanical checking between them is Sonnet's (author, 2026-10-04: “We don't need Opus to make sure links exist and that kind of things. Opus may be helpful for reviewing text and making sure it reads right”):

1. **An Opus writer** researches and writes the chapter and its ledger (`chapter-writer`).
2. **The script checks the ledger** (`scripts/check-ledger.mjs`): every quote confirmed on its page.
3. **A Sonnet checker** reopens the sources, compares each claim with its page, tests the checkable rules, and writes its findings with the sources quoted (`chapter-checker`).
4. **An Opus reviewer** decides the findings and repairs the chapter, reading for sense and for the standard, without reopening the sources (`chapter-reviewer`).
5. **Codex reads for language, and Opus applies what holds up** (author, 2026-10-07: “a very useful fourth step”). After a batch is committed, `.claude/workflows/codex-pass.js` has Codex, a model of another family, read each chapter alone and the batch side by side (`scripts/codex-read.mjs`, read-only: it edits nothing and judges no citation); an Opus agent then decides each finding, cutting, merging and rewording but adding nothing, and reruns `check-chapter`. The Sonnet source check is not repeated, since no claim is added. Tried first on Ezekiel 1, 4 and 7 and across 1–12: 49 findings in the three chapters and 8 across the twelve, about nine in ten worth acting on (note openings that withhold their subject, a reading stated as fact, a point made in four places, the same explanation in three chapters), none of which the Opus reviewer had caught, and no suggestion that added a fact. What it showed about the batch went into Ezekiel’s brief (“Learned from the first twelve chapters”) for the chapters still to be written.

Opus stays in that last step (author, 2026-10-04: “I don't want to go all the way to Sonnet. But it felt through Genesis that we were using Opus more than we needed to”). In Genesis Opus wrote 26 of the chapters and, as reviewer, reopened the sources of all 50; reopening the sources is no longer its job.

**The writer test (2026-10-07, Jeremiah 19 and 45).** Each chapter was written afresh three times, by Haiku, Sonnet and Opus, in separate copies of the repository with the live chapters removed, then checked (Sonnet) and reviewed (Opus) as usual. The author's measure: “More notes is not necessarily better. The best would be the one that best anticipates the questions a human might ask and then finds answers to those questions.” Three models each listed a reader's questions from the scripture text alone, and an Opus judge scored the unlabelled versions against the merged list (answered well / partly / not):

| Writer | Jer. 19 draft (34) | Jer. 19 reviewed (38) | Jer. 45 draft (24) | Jer. 45 reviewed (25) | writing, both chapters |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opus | 25 / 7 / 2 | 26 / 10 / 2 | 17 / 4 / 3 | 16 / 6 / 3 | 9.3 min, 277k tokens |
| Sonnet | 17 / 12 / 5 | 18 / 15 / 5 | 13 / 8 / 3 | 12 / 9 / 4 | 9.4 min, 241k |
| Haiku | 9 / 13 / 12 | 15 / 13 / 10 | 5 / 10 / 9 | 6 / 13 / 6 | 13.4 min, 389k |

Opus asked the follow-up question (not “cannibalism” but “did it happen, and why does the Lord say ‘I will cause’ it?”) and answered the questions most readers would ask that the others skipped; its fault is length and lexical detail. Sonnet was the most economical but dropped questions it had asked (to stay inside the Keil limit, or because a footnote touched them). Haiku's questions were fair and its answers thin; review did not close the gap, and its chapter 19 improved only because the Opus reviewer did new research. Under the few-steps instructions an Opus writer now costs about what a Sonnet writer does (13 steps a chapter against 21), which was not so in Genesis. Limits of the test: two chapters, one run each, and an Opus judge who could guess a version from its length. **Two things it showed about the batch itself:** a reviewer may pull an earlier version of a chapter out of git when one exists (one did, and its review was redone), and an agent sometimes refuses to write its report or findings file, taking a session instruction against writing documents to forbid it (2 of 12 did; the coordinator saved their final messages as the files).

**Tested once, on Malachi (2026-10-04, four chapters).** Two questions were asked of it.

*Does the Sonnet checker report what Opus reviewers used to find by reading the sources themselves?* Yes. It reported 45, 37, 37 and 15 findings, with the page's words quoted, and the Opus reviewers acted on 97 of the 113 numbered ones. They included a claim that said more than its page (Keil's governor in 1:8, who “might also apply to Nehemiah,” written as the Persian governor), a quotation given to the wrong passage (3:16), a law stated too broadly (Lev. 22:19), outside readings followed where the Church's differs (1:10, 1:11, 3:1, 3:3), and the Septuagint contradicting two sentences in chapter 2. The checker also listed announced sources that `check-content` missed.

*Does it cost less?* Less Opus, more in all. Measured from the agents' own transcripts, a chapter's average, beside Genesis 27–50 (Sonnet writers, Opus reviewers, no checker and no research scripts):

| | turns | tool calls | tokens read again from cache | size at the end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis writer (Sonnet) | 61–78 | 65–85 | 10.5–17.6M | 261–329k |
| Malachi writer (Sonnet) | 64 | 72 | 11.4M | 311k |
| Malachi checker (Sonnet) | 30 | 37 | 4.4M | 221k |
| Genesis reviewer (Opus) | 40–49 | 57–66 | 6.2–6.5M | 190–255k |
| Malachi reviewer (Opus) | 15 | 38 | 2.2M | 242k |

The Opus reviewer took a third of the turns and reread a third of the tokens, but it still reads about as much once (the standard, the brief, the chapter, the whole ledger, and a long findings file). The writer is no cheaper: the research scripts did not reduce its calls. The checker is a third agent, about 220k Sonnet tokens a chapter, so a chapter's total by the harness's count went from 450–585k to about 770k. Malachi's chapters are also heavier than Genesis's (170 ledger rows a chapter against 125). Building the scripts cost about 1.6M Sonnet tokens. 

**Why the scripts did not lower the count.** The transcripts show the research scripts replaced the agents' own shell commands one for one: a Genesis writer ran about 63 `curl` and shell commands that returned 237,000 characters; a Malachi writer ran about 37 `source.mjs` and 20 other script and shell commands that returned 240,000. Fetching a page never cost tokens; reading what comes back does, and each later step reads it again. The scripts changed how a page is fetched (verbatim text, right verse numbers, a blocked page named), not how much of it the agent reads. A script saves tokens only where the model never sees the output, as `check-ledger` does (678 quotes confirmed with no page read by a model), and that script was already in use for Genesis. What would lower the count is less reading: each of the three agents reads the standard and the brief (86,000 characters), the reviewer reads the whole ledger (55,000), and the checker reads the page context of every row (149,000) where its instructions ask for the commentary and talk rows and a sample of the rest. 

**Changed for the next book (2026-10-04, untested).** The larger cost is not the reading itself but carrying it: a model keeps nothing between steps, so every message in which an agent calls a tool is sent the whole session again (a Malachi writer took 64 steps and was sent 11.4M tokens that way, charged at a fraction of the rate for new reading). Three changes to the agents' instructions and the batch's prompts, none to the scripts: (1) all three agents work in few steps, several tool calls in one message, with about 25 steps the aim for a writer; (2) the checker's `ledger-context.mjs` leaves out the rows that quote the scripture and Hebrew text themselves (`--skip-keys lds-scriptures,oshb-wlc,biblehub-interlinear`, about 30% less output on Malachi 1 and 3) and samples those in the ledger file; lexicon, translation and Septuagint rows stay in, because Malachi 2's checker caught the Septuagint contradicting two sentences; (3) the reviewer does not read the ledger through. Claude's guess is a quarter to a third less in all, mostly from the first. **Measure the first two chapters of the next book** with `node scripts/batch-usage.cjs <the run's transcript folder> --calls` and set them beside the table above; watch too that the reviewer, no longer reading the ledger, still catches a claim with no row.

**Measured on Lamentations (2026-10-06, five chapters, `batch-usage.cjs`).** A chapter's average: writer 24 steps, 35 tool calls, 2.3M tokens sent again from the cache, 144k at the end; checker 8 steps, 13 calls, 0.4M, 94k; Opus reviewer 6 steps, 19 calls, 0.3M, 102k. That is about 340k a chapter against Malachi's 770k, and 3.0M reread against 18M; the whole book, with its illustration, took 1.74M tokens and 21 minutes. The chapters are lighter than Malachi's (68 ledger rows a chapter against 170), so not all of the difference is the new instructions. The reviewers still added and removed rows (chapter 5 went from 37 rows to 61), and `check-content` reports no note without a row.

**How it got here.** Jeremiah: Sonnet writers, Opus reviewers that reopened nearly every row (about 120k Opus tokens a chapter). A test on Jeremiah 19 and 45 (2026-10-02) had Opus write as well: more notes (13 against 8 on chapter 19), the same sourcing discipline, and a lighter review; Claude recommended it, and wrote here that “the author chose depth,” which was wrong: the author has not compared the two. Genesis 1–26 were written that way, at 360–380k Opus tokens a chapter for the writer and 165–175k for the reviewer, about three times Jeremiah's cost and twice its time. Genesis 27–50 went back to Sonnet writers with Opus reviewers, as did Malachi, Lamentations, Moses and Abraham. The test of 2026-10-07 above settled it for Opus; the Opus versions of Jeremiah 19 and 45 from 2026-10-02 are the ones on the site.

Before that: the book's brief, with the author (`content/<book>/BRIEF.md`: divisions and intro, the author's questions, which sources are actually readable online, book-wide issues, guide and theme candidates, Restoration connections); then `book.yaml` and “Adding a new book” in `DEVELOPMENT.md`. Jeremiah's brief is the model, including its section “What a chapter is for.” The Workflow script for writing a book in batches (writer → reviewer pipeline, about twelve chapters at a time) is `.claude/workflows/write-chapters.js`, and the coordinator's steps around it are scripts too (`DEVELOPMENT.md`, “Writing a book in batches”).

**After a book is complete:** its illustration, the last stage of writing a book. Run the book's final batch of `write-chapters` with `lastBatch: true` and it ends by running `.claude/workflows/create-book-art.js` once every chapter is ready (or run that workflow by name). Its `book-art-director` reads the finished book and writes a proposed visual brief; `scripts/book-art.mjs generate <book>` then has Codex draw one candidate into `.cache/book-art/<book>/`. Show the candidate to the author, and only after approval run `node scripts/book-art.mjs approve <book>`, which saves the asset and marks the brief approved. Until then the build warns that the finished book has no approved illustration. This is an authoring stage, not a site build step; details are in `DEVELOPMENT.md`, “Book illustration workflow.”

## The language pass, in a session of its own

The author runs step 5 (Codex reads, Opus applies) in a separate session from the one writing chapters (author, 2026-10-07). Everything that session needs is here. It works **in the main checkout, on `main`**; the writing session works in a worktree on the book's branch and fast-forwards `main` to it whenever a batch is committed, so a chapter that is on `main` is finished and ready for the pass.

**What is waiting.** A chapter has had the pass when a commit says so:

```sh
git log --format=%s --grep "language pass" -- content/ezekiel/chapters | sort -V   # done
ls content/ezekiel/chapters                                                          # written
```

All 48 chapters of Ezekiel were done on 2026-10-07, in the writing session; nothing is waiting. For the next book, take a batch whole, so its chapters can also be read side by side.

**The older books (author, 2026-10-08: “do a codex check on every page that hasn't been already reviewed by codex”).** Done that day and merged into `main` (not pushed): every chapter of Genesis, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Lamentations, Malachi, Moses, Abraham and Mosiah (192 chapters: 3,422 findings, 2,560 applied), and 32 other pages: the eight book pages, Isaiah's and Jeremiah's theme and guide pages, the three facsimile pages, and About (231 findings, 176 applied). One commit a chapter or page, each after `check-pass` and `check-chapter`; no ledger row was added. The five volume pages have no text yet. What was left for the author is S5 in `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`. **Nothing is waiting.** What the day taught: Codex's usage limit (the ChatGPT plan's) ran out after about 70 readings a window on `gpt-6-sol`, when Codex opened the files itself (some 34,000 tokens a reading); with `--inline` (the chapter's text in the prompt) a reading took about 21,000, and on `gpt-5.6-terra` about 13,000, with somewhat fewer findings. Run Codex's readings first, a few at a time from the shell (they cost none of Claude's tokens), then the workflow, which skips a reading whose file exists; a reading that hangs is killed and run again. A facsimile page is read with `node scripts/codex-read.mjs abraham facsimile-<n>`.

**Run it.** Start a session in the main checkout and say “run the codex-pass workflow on Ezekiel 25–36” (or call it: `Workflow({ name: "codex-pass", args: { book: "ezekiel", chapters: [25, …, 36] } })`). It needs the `codex` CLI signed in (`codex login status`). For each chapter a small agent runs `node scripts/codex-read.mjs ezekiel <n>` (Codex, read-only, writes numbered findings to `.cache/batch/ezekiel/reports/NN-codex.md`; it reads the batch side by side into `25-36-codex.md` first), then an Opus agent decides each finding, edits only that chapter's two files, adds nothing, and reruns `check-chapter`. Twelve chapters took 6 minutes and 1.4M tokens once Codex had read them; Codex itself takes a few minutes a chapter and none of Claude's tokens. If Codex's model is at capacity, pass `codexModel`. If a run is cut off, **run it again with only the chapters that were not finished** (those `check-pass.mjs` reports unchanged, or half-edited and uncommitted): on 2026-10-07 a run resumed with `resumeFromRunId` after one of twelve agents failed sent all twelve again, 640k tokens to redo one chapter. Codex’s findings files are kept, so Codex is not run twice.

**Then, as coordinator:**

1. `node scripts/check-pass.mjs ezekiel 25 26 … 36`: what the edits added, beside the last commit. New references should only be pointers to another chapter's note; no ledger row should have been added; a “new or changed quotation” is usually an old one whose closing punctuation moved, so look at any that is not.
2. `node scripts/check-chapter.mjs ezekiel <n>` for each chapter. The warnings about commentaries read on Bible Hub were there before the pass and stay.
3. Read each agent's `declined` list in the workflow's result: a finding declined for a reason that touches doctrine or the author's taste goes to `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`; the rest need nothing.
4. Commit one chapter at a time, the chapter with its ledger: `Ezekiel 25: language pass (Codex's findings, applied by Opus)`. Commit to `main`; don't push (the book is in preview, and a push rebuilds the live site).
5. Touch nothing but those chapters' files. The writing session owns `content/sources.yaml`, the brief, `THEME-CANDIDATES.md`, and the chapters not yet on `main`; it merges `main` into its branch before each of its own commits reaches `main`.

**Other pages** (a theme or guide page, a book page, the About page) are read one at a time, with no workflow: `node scripts/codex-read.mjs <book> <page file name>`, `<book> book`, or `--file <path>`; apply the findings that hold up, adding nothing, and rerun that page's checks (`check-content <book> <page>` and `check-ledger <book> <page>` for a theme page; the build for a book page).

## Working with the author

- **Where changes go.** Commit chapter content, sources, and fixes directly to `main`, one commit a chapter as before. **Push only when the live site changes, and say so first** (author, 2026-10-07: “We should be cautious about pushing to main. I don't need CloudFlare to build a new site when nothing has changed”). Every push to `main` makes Cloudflare rebuild the site, so commits that change nothing a visitor sees stay local until something does: a book still in preview, a brief, the standard, the open questions, scripts, an unapproved illustration. When there is something to publish, push once, with whatever has gathered behind it. On 2026-10-07 Abraham’s setup, chapters and notes were pushed about twenty times while the book was in preview; none of those builds changed the site. Changes to how the site *looks* (layout, widths, new interface features) go on a separate branch until the author has seen them and approved; then fast-forward `main`.
- **Showing a change.** Before a change is pushed, show it in the private claude.ai preview artifact (https://claude.ai/artifact/W1iHyLZVvWzuCeSvoDZCTS): run `node scripts/preview-site.mjs`, which builds the site with books still in preview and makes every link relative, then republish `.cache/preview/dist` to the same artifact (`index.html` as the page, the files listed in `.cache/preview/files.json` beside it). The author reviews there and can leave comments on specific passages. Once pushed, changes are live at https://scriptures.conlin.io.
- **The author reads mostly on an iPad mini** (744px upright, 1133px sideways). Check layouts there as well as on a laptop and a phone.
- **A book's first research includes Scripture Central and the Interpreter Foundation** (author, 2026-10-09: “Please make sure that you include both of those sites in your initial research. They are good sources and stay true to the doctrines of the Church. They strike a good academic balance”). Before a brief is drafted, search each site by itself for the book (Scripture Central's archive of articles and book chapters as well as its KnoWhys; Interpreter's journal as well as its study aids), read what is found, and frame the brief's contested questions from those, BYU's publications and *Scripture Helps* before the Student Manual or an older commentary is opened. The brief says what was searched and what was read. Hosea's brief was drafted without this (one shared search, nothing read from either site), which the author noticed on first reading it.
- **Don't ask for focus questions before a book is written** (author, 2026-10-06: “I typically won't have focus questions while generating the books. Only after I start studying them in more depth will I get that”). Writers work from the questions a careful reader would ask (`STANDARDS.md` §3); the author's questions come later, while studying, and become commentary then (next item).
- **The author's study questions become commentary** (author, 2026-10-04). When the author asks about a verse while studying, answer from sources read in the session, then add the answer to that verse's note with ledger rows: deepen the existing note rather than restructure the chapter. First done for Isaiah 53:7.
- **Codex reads every new page** (author, 2026-10-07: “whenever we create a page, we should have codex check it. So the other pages we create and book pages and theme pages”). A chapter, a theme or guide page, a book’s landing page (`book.yaml`), a facsimile page, a volume introduction, the About page: once it is written and has passed its own review, `scripts/codex-read.mjs` has Codex read it for language (it edits nothing and judges no citation), the findings that hold up are applied with nothing added, and the page’s checks are run again, before the author is shown it. Chapters go through `.claude/workflows/codex-pass.js` a batch at a time; for a single page, run the script and apply the findings by hand or with one agent. Pages written before this rule are not gone back over unless the author asks.
- **One file of open questions.** Everything that waits for the author, for every book and the site, is in `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` at the top of the repository, numbered so it can be answered by number (author, 2026-10-04: “I can't keep track of those that are in multiple places”). Don't start another list anywhere else. A judgment call the standard or the brief settles is decided and recorded in the book's brief (“Decided while writing”); only what needs the author goes in that file. Delete an item once its answer is carried out.
- **Offload to scripts what a script can do exactly** (author, 2026-10-04: “Anywhere we can write a script to offload AI use to a simple script is a good idea”). It covers theme pages as well as chapters. A step an agent repeats mechanically (fetching, searching, counting, comparing a quote with a page, listing what the site already says) is a reason to write a script before the next batch, not to tell the next agent to do it again by reading.
- The per-chapter workflow (writer agent, ledger, checks, review, one commit per chapter) is in `STANDARDS.md` §10–11; the theme-page workflow (research, outline, writing, review) is in §8.
