## Plan for the next book

Three agents write a chapter, and Opus is kept for the last step only (author, 2026-10-04: “We don't need Opus to make sure links exist and that kind of things. Opus may be helpful for reviewing text and making sure it reads right”):

1. **A Sonnet writer** researches and writes the chapter and its ledger (`chapter-writer`).
2. **The script checks the ledger** (`scripts/check-ledger.mjs`): every quote confirmed on its page.
3. **A Sonnet checker** reopens the sources, compares each claim with its page, tests the checkable rules, and writes its findings with the sources quoted (`chapter-checker`).
4. **An Opus reviewer** decides the findings and repairs the chapter, reading for sense and for the standard, without reopening the sources (`chapter-reviewer`).

Opus stays in that last step (author, 2026-10-04: “I don't want to go all the way to Sonnet. But it felt through Genesis that we were using Opus more than we needed to”). In Genesis Opus wrote 26 of the chapters and, as reviewer, reopened the sources of all 50; neither is its job now.

**Tested once, on Malachi (2026-10-04, four chapters).** Two questions were asked of it.

*Does the Sonnet checker report what Opus reviewers used to find by reading the sources themselves?* Yes. It reported 45, 37, 37 and 15 findings, with the page's words quoted, and the Opus reviewers acted on 97 of the 113 numbered ones. They included a claim that said more than its page (Keil's governor in 1:8, who “might also apply to Nehemiah,” written as the Persian governor), a quotation given to the wrong passage (3:16), a law stated too broadly (Lev. 22:19), outside readings followed where the Church's differs (1:10, 1:11, 3:1, 3:3), and the Septuagint contradicting two sentences in chapter 2. The checker also listed announced sources that `check-content` missed.

*Does it cost less?* Less Opus, more in all. Measured from the agents' own transcripts, a chapter's average, beside Genesis 27–50 (Sonnet writers, Opus reviewers, no checker and no research scripts):

| | turns | tool calls | tokens read again from cache | size at the end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis writer (Sonnet) | 61–78 | 65–85 | 10.5–17.6M | 261–329k |
| Malachi writer (Sonnet) | 64 | 72 | 11.4M | 311k |
| Malachi checker (Sonnet) | 30 | 37 | 4.4M | 221k |
| Genesis reviewer (Opus) | 40–49 | 57–66 | 6.2–6.5M | 190–255k |
| Malachi reviewer (Opus) | 15 | 38 | 2.2M | 242k |

The Opus reviewer took a third of the turns and reread a third of the tokens, but it still reads about as much once (the standard, the brief, the chapter, the whole ledger, and a long findings file). The writer is no cheaper: the research scripts did not reduce its calls. The checker is a third agent, about 220k Sonnet tokens a chapter, so a chapter's total by the harness's count went from 450–585k to about 770k. Malachi's chapters are also heavier than Genesis's (170 ledger rows a chapter against 125). Building the scripts cost about 1.6M Sonnet tokens. 

**Why the scripts did not lower the count.** The transcripts show the research scripts replaced the agents' own shell commands one for one: a Genesis writer ran about 63 `curl` and shell commands that returned 237,000 characters; a Malachi writer ran about 37 `source.mjs` and 20 other script and shell commands that returned 240,000. Fetching a page never cost tokens; reading what comes back does, and each later step reads it again. The scripts changed how a page is fetched (verbatim text, right verse numbers, a blocked page named), not how much of it the agent reads. A script saves tokens only where the model never sees the output, as `check-ledger` does (678 quotes confirmed with no page read by a model), and that script was already in use for Genesis. What would lower the count is less reading: each of the three agents reads the standard and the brief (86,000 characters), the reviewer reads the whole ledger (55,000), and the checker reads the page context of every row (149,000) where its instructions ask for the commentary and talk rows and a sample of the rest. 

**Changed for the next book (2026-10-04, untested).** The larger cost is not the reading itself but carrying it: a model keeps nothing between steps, so every message in which an agent calls a tool is sent the whole session again (a Malachi writer took 64 steps and was sent 11.4M tokens that way, charged at a fraction of the rate for new reading). Three changes to the agents' instructions and the batch's prompts, none to the scripts: (1) all three agents work in few steps, several tool calls in one message, with about 25 steps the aim for a writer; (2) the checker's `ledger-context.mjs` leaves out the rows that quote the scripture and Hebrew text themselves (`--skip-keys lds-scriptures,oshb-wlc,biblehub-interlinear`, about 30% less output on Malachi 1 and 3) and samples those in the ledger file; lexicon, translation and Septuagint rows stay in, because Malachi 2's checker caught the Septuagint contradicting two sentences; (3) the reviewer does not read the ledger through. Claude's guess is a quarter to a third less in all, mostly from the first. **Measure the first two chapters of the next book** with `node scripts/batch-usage.cjs <the run's transcript folder> --calls` and set them beside the table above; watch too that the reviewer, no longer reading the ledger, still catches a claim with no row.

**Measured on Lamentations (2026-10-06, five chapters, `batch-usage.cjs`).** A chapter's average: writer 24 steps, 35 tool calls, 2.3M tokens sent again from the cache, 144k at the end; checker 8 steps, 13 calls, 0.4M, 94k; Opus reviewer 6 steps, 19 calls, 0.3M, 102k. That is about 340k a chapter against Malachi's 770k, and 3.0M reread against 18M; the whole book, with its illustration, took 1.74M tokens and 21 minutes. The chapters are lighter than Malachi's (68 ledger rows a chapter against 170), so not all of the difference is the new instructions. The reviewers still added and removed rows (chapter 5 went from 37 rows to 61), and `check-content` reports no note without a row.

**How it got here.** Jeremiah: Sonnet writers, Opus reviewers that reopened nearly every row (about 120k Opus tokens a chapter). A test on Jeremiah 19 and 45 (2026-10-02) had Opus write as well: more notes (13 against 8 on chapter 19), the same sourcing discipline, and a lighter review; Claude recommended it, and wrote here that “the author chose depth,” which was wrong: the author has not compared the two. Genesis 1–26 were written that way, at 360–380k Opus tokens a chapter for the writer and 165–175k for the reviewer, about three times Jeremiah's cost and twice its time. Genesis 27–50 went back to Sonnet writers with Opus reviewers. The author will judge the difference between the two halves on reading; the Opus versions of Jeremiah 19 and 45 are the ones on the site.

Before that: the book's brief, with the author (`content/<book>/BRIEF.md`: divisions and intro, the author's questions, which sources are actually readable online, book-wide issues, guide and theme candidates, Restoration connections); then `book.yaml` and “Adding a new book” in `DEVELOPMENT.md`. Jeremiah's brief is the model, including its section “What a chapter is for.” The Workflow script for writing a book in batches (writer → reviewer pipeline, about twelve chapters at a time) is `.claude/workflows/write-chapters.js`, and the coordinator's steps around it are scripts too (`DEVELOPMENT.md`, “Writing a book in batches”).

**After a book is complete:** its illustration, the last stage of writing a book. Run the book's final batch of `write-chapters` with `lastBatch: true` and it ends by running `.claude/workflows/create-book-art.js` once every chapter is ready (or run that workflow by name). Its `book-art-director` reads the finished book and writes a proposed visual brief; `scripts/book-art.mjs generate <book>` then has Codex draw one candidate into `.cache/book-art/<book>/`. Show the candidate to the author, and only after approval run `node scripts/book-art.mjs approve <book>`, which saves the asset and marks the brief approved. Until then the build warns that the finished book has no approved illustration. This is an authoring stage, not a site build step; details are in `DEVELOPMENT.md`, “Book illustration workflow.”

## Working with the author

- **Where changes go.** Commit chapter content, sources, and fixes directly to `main` and push. Changes to how the site *looks* (layout, widths, new interface features) go on a separate branch until the author has seen them and approved; then fast-forward `main`.
- **Showing a change.** Before a change is pushed, show it in the private claude.ai preview artifact (https://claude.ai/artifact/W1iHyLZVvWzuCeSvoDZCTS): run `node scripts/preview-site.mjs`, which builds the site with books still in preview and makes every link relative, then republish `.cache/preview/dist` to the same artifact (`index.html` as the page, the files listed in `.cache/preview/files.json` beside it). The author reviews there and can leave comments on specific passages. Once pushed, changes are live at https://scriptures.conlin.io.
- **The author reads mostly on an iPad mini** (744px upright, 1133px sideways). Check layouts there as well as on a laptop and a phone.
- **Don't ask for focus questions before a book is written** (author, 2026-10-06: “I typically won't have focus questions while generating the books. Only after I start studying them in more depth will I get that”). Writers work from the questions a careful reader would ask (`STANDARDS.md` §3); the author's questions come later, while studying, and become commentary then (next item).
- **The author's study questions become commentary** (author, 2026-10-04). When the author asks about a verse while studying, answer from sources read in the session, then add the answer to that verse's note with ledger rows: deepen the existing note rather than restructure the chapter. First done for Isaiah 53:7.
- **One file of open questions.** Everything that waits for the author, for every book and the site, is in `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` at the top of the repository, numbered so it can be answered by number (author, 2026-10-04: “I can't keep track of those that are in multiple places”). Don't start another list anywhere else. A judgment call the standard or the brief settles is decided and recorded in the book's brief (“Decided while writing”); only what needs the author goes in that file. Delete an item once its answer is carried out.
- **Offload to scripts what a script can do exactly** (author, 2026-10-04: “Anywhere we can write a script to offload AI use to a simple script is a good idea”). It covers theme pages as well as chapters. A step an agent repeats mechanically (fetching, searching, counting, comparing a quote with a page, listing what the site already says) is a reason to write a script before the next batch, not to tell the next agent to do it again by reading.
- The per-chapter workflow (writer agent, ledger, checks, review, one commit per chapter) is in `STANDARDS.md` §10–11; the theme-page workflow (research, outline, writing, review) is in §8.
