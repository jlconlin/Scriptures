# Decisions that frame the site

Choices that shape the whole site and that later work shouldn't quietly undo. Only framing decisions go here; the history of how work was done lives in git, not in files. Where a decision is spelled out elsewhere, this file points there.

- **Sourcing is part of writing (2026-09-30).** Content is written so that it needs no audit afterward: every factual claim rests on a source actually read. See `STANDARDS.md` §4.
- **Evidence ledgers are kept permanently (2026-09-30).** Each chapter's `content/<book>/evidence/NN.yaml` is the support for what the chapter currently says, not a record of how it was written. It stays in the repository and is updated when a note changes, and `check-content.mjs` relies on it. See `STANDARDS.md` §5.
- **Stay near Latter-day Saint theology; not a compendium (2026-10-01).** Each chapter centers on the text read in the light of Restoration scripture and latter-day prophets; outside scholarship serves that and doesn't survey the field. A peripheral detail is cut, not sourced. See `STANDARDS.md` §1 and §4.
- **Most content comes from the Church's own sources (2026-10-02).** Then BYU publications, Scripture Central and the Interpreter Foundation's journal (added 2026-10-07); other commentary sparingly, when those have no answer. Everything follows the Church's teaching and doctrine. See `STANDARDS.md` §1.
- **No Wikipedia, other wikis, or popular history websites (Livius.org) as sources.** See `STANDARDS.md` §4.
- **No length target (2026-10-01).** Chapters have no set number of notes or words; notes that are mostly cross-reference chains or curiosities are cut instead. See `STANDARDS.md` §3 (“Selectivity”).
- **Unsourced devotional readings are cut (2026-10-01).** A devotional reading stays only when scripture, a Church source, or a scholar supports it. See `STANDARDS.md` §3.
- **A theme page is an arc across chapters that points to Christ (2026-10-01).** Broader than a chapter, few per book, and worth writing only if it helps the reader see the bigger story that points to Jesus Christ. The text supplies the pattern; scripture and Church sources supply the meaning. On theme pages only, the site may make connections between passages that no published source makes, without inventing doctrine or contradicting what has been revealed (author, 2026-10-03; this replaced “the site adds no readings of its own”). See `PAGES.md`.
- **A theme page is a semi-academic essay that argues its insight (2026-10-25, reversing 2026-10-02's “something I can quickly look at”).** As long as its argument needs, 4,000 words at most and never padded to it; a thesis stated and argued from the text; the page's own connections marked as its own and then made, not left as comparisons. A book with no insight worth an essay has no theme pages. The pages written before this date are being gone over, rewritten or deleted. See `PAGES.md`.
- **A theme page is written under six instructions, by one mind (2026-10-26).** The form rules that had accumulated for theme pages are gone; the writer gets six instructions (argue one insight; write as yourself; facts sourced; own readings owned and argued; apply in statements; stop when made) and the coordinating Fable session writes the essay itself. The rules had produced prose the author called “*very* AI-generated.” See `PAGES.md`, “How a theme page is written.”
- **The theme pages and guides written before 2026-10-25 were triaged, and each is rewritten in its turn (2026-10-25 to 11-03).** Thirteen essays and twenty-five shorter pages were rewritten under §8 and went live on 2026-11-03, marked unread until the author reads them; four pages were deleted and five merged; the thirty-eight whose connection the manuals already make, and the narrative guides, stay live in the old form, marked `review: old-form`, until the author's study reaches their book (author: “Lets leave bin C live. We need to update them in the future, maybe near future”). A page's state is in its own front matter, not in a file (author, 2026-11-03: a file “showing every thing that was ever changed” is “`git`'s responsibility”). See `PAGES.md`, “Where a page stands.”
- **The Church's teaching manuals are finders, never sources (2026-11-17).** *Come, Follow Me*, *Scripture Helps*, the seminary and institute manuals and the Student Manuals are read for what they cite; the talk, scripture, document or study behind a statement is read and cited instead, or the statement is left out. The site does not duplicate those resources. For new writing; the pages written before it are re-sourced when the books are revised, not before. See `STANDARDS.md` §1.
- **Scripture is applied to the reader (2026-10-16, reversing 2026-09-30).** Chapters (in `christ`) and theme pages say where a principle reaches a disciple's life now, as statements grounded in scripture and the prophets, never as *Come, Follow Me*'s questions. The “Liken it” sections removed on 2026-09-30 stay removed. See `STANDARDS.md` §1 and §8.
- **Nothing about a single book is kept in a top-level file (2026-10-09).** A book’s state is read from the repository (`node scripts/status.mjs`), its decisions are in its `BRIEF.md`, and its history is its commit messages. The one exception is `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`, a single list at the author’s request. See `AUTHORING.md`, “Status.”
- **Why these models (2026-10-04 to 10-16).** Each was the author's decision after a trial; a change needs a trial of the same kind, judged blind against the questions a reader would ask (the author's measure: “The best would be the one that best anticipates the questions a human might ask and then finds answers to those questions”).
  - *Opus writes* (2026-10-07; confirmed 2026-10-16: “We stay with opus”). On two Jeremiah chapters Opus answered the most reader questions and asked the follow-up; Sonnet dropped questions it had asked; Haiku's answers were thin and review did not close the gap. On Numbers 13, 19 and 21, each written by Opus and by Sonnet 5.5 and then checked and reviewed as usual, the reviewed versions answered well 44 of 118 questions against 30 (an Opus judge) and 37 against 30 (a Codex judge). Sonnet left whole kinds of question untouched and gave the reviewer twice the findings, for about the same tokens. Neither made a serious error.
  - *Sonnet checks, Opus judges* (2026-10-04): Opus is not needed to see that links exist; it is for reviewing how the text reads.
  - *Opus reviews* (2026-10-16). Sonnet 5.5's reviewed chapters graded close to Opus's, but its reports claimed fixes that are not in the text six times against once, which nothing later catches.
  - *Codex is the last reader* (2026-10-07), as a model of another family: about nine in ten of its first findings were worth acting on and none had been caught by the Opus reviewer.
  - *Opus decides Codex's findings* (2026-10-16). On Deuteronomy, Sonnet 5.5 declined 14 of 141 findings where Opus declined 55 of 130: it followed Codex against the brief, left claims stated as fact, and cut verse words from the paraphrase. An Opus judge counted 28 harmful edits against 3. The Codex judge preferred Sonnet, but it was grading the handling of its own findings and counted every decline as an error.
  - *Fable proposes and outlines theme pages and writes the essays* (2026-10-14, 2026-10-26); Opus writes a shorter page from Fable's outline (2026-10-29). See `PAGES.md`.
- **Codex reads before the reviewer (2026-11-17).** Through Chronicles a chapter was reviewed, committed, read by Codex, and then repaired again by a separate Opus applier, with a writer-repair loop for what a copy edit could not fix. Over 145 chapters that second stage took 193 applier runs in about forty launches for a median net change of 17 words a chapter. Now Codex reads the draft and one reviewer decides its findings together with the checker's. The trial rewrote all of Nehemiah with the same models beside the version written the old way five days before: the reviewers acted on 87% of the checker's findings and 91% of Codex's (89% and 73% before), no chapter went back to the writer (three before), and the agents used about a fifth fewer tokens in about half the running time. Judged blind against 243 reader questions, the two were even: an Opus judge counted 94 answered well against 86 and a Codex judge 70 against 64, and each preferred the older version in slightly more chapters. The author kept the new arrangement (“I do want to use the new pipeline”), and three of the trial's chapters replaced the old. The earlier arrangement is at commit `584c7225`. See `PROCESS.md`, “Writing a book in batches.”
- **Instructions are split by who reads them (2026-11-17).** A file has one audience, and a rule has one home: `STANDARDS.md` for chapters, `PAGES.md` for theme pages and guides, `PROCESS.md` for the coordinating session, `DEVELOPMENT.md` for the code. Rules are not copied into one file per role, since the writer, the checker and the reviewer must work from the same ones.
- **Every book shares one standard and one codebase.** Book-level choices go in `content/<book>/BRIEF.md`; the code has no book-specific assumptions (books are discovered from `content/*/book.yaml`; see `DEVELOPMENT.md`).
- **Each completed book receives one author-approved illustration in a shared visual language (2026-10-04).** A post-authoring AI stage distills the finished book into a proposed visual brief, then has Codex draw one candidate from it (`scripts/book-art.mjs`). The author approves the candidate before it becomes a site asset; generation never runs as part of a build. See `PROCESS.md`, “Book illustration workflow.”
- **Content goes to `main`; changes to how the site looks wait for the author's approval** on a separate branch. See `AUTHORING.md`.
- **Process notes are temporary.** Status, plans, audit tracking, and open questions are kept while the work is under way and deleted when it is done. The project files say what is true now and what the rules are; how the work got there is in git (author, 2026-10-09).
