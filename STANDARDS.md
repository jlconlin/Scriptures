# Writing and sourcing standard

The standard for all commentary on this site, for every book. `AUTHORING.md` holds the status and how to work with the author; each book's `content/<book>/BRIEF.md` holds the decisions for that book; `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`, at the top of the repository, holds everything waiting for the author. Where they disagree, this file wins unless the author has decided otherwise in the brief.

The goal: **content that needs no audit afterward.** Sourcing is part of writing, not a later pass.

## 1. What the site is

- **A focused companion to the scriptures.** It gives one clear reading of each chapter. It is not a database and does not try to gather everything written about a chapter or cite every source. When another resource is especially good on a chapter, point to it once (usually in `explore`) rather than absorbing it.
- **Deeper than *Come, Follow Me*, and not a companion to it.** Don't repeat the manual or follow its outline.
- **Apply scripture to the reader, in statements, never in questions** (author, 2026-10-16, reversing the rule of 2026-09-30: “All scripture needs to be applied to the reader/student, otherwise, what's the point… What I don't want is the site to be asking questions like the *Come, Follow Me* manual”; of chapters: “Yes applies to chapters too”). A chapter's `christ` section, and a theme page after its arc (§8, “It applies the arc to the reader”), say where the principle reaches a disciple's life now. No questions to the reader, no invitations or exercises, no exhortation, no anecdotes; the “Liken it” sections removed in 2026-09 stay removed. Chapters close by pointing to Christ and what that means for a disciple (`christ`) and to what's worth exploring next (`explore`). A book's brief written before 2026-10-16 that still says “no personal application” is overruled by this rule. Chapters written before 2026-10-16 get it when there is a reason to open them, and all of them at the author's next review of their volume (`AUTHORING.md`, “Live books are open to change”).
- **Stay near Latter-day Saint theology** (author, 2026-10-01). The center of every chapter is what the text says and how Restoration scripture and latter-day prophets read it. Scholarship, history and language serve that: use them where they change how a reader understands the verse, not to survey what has been said. When an interpretation is speculative, or faithful readers disagree, say so.
- **Most content comes from the Church's own sources** (author, 2026-10-02). In order: the Church's sources (the scriptures and their study helps, manuals, the teachings of prophets and apostles, the Joseph Smith Papers); BYU publications (the Religious Studies Center, BYU Studies); Scripture Central and the Interpreter Foundation (the journal *Interpreter*; added by the author, 2026-10-07, as a place to look “like Scripture Central”). Other commentary and scholarship is “appropriate, but should be used sparingly when other LDS adjacent sources are not available.” Everything follows the Church's teaching and doctrine: an outside reading that differs from the Church's is not set beside it as an equal alternative. Where the Church's own sources differ, follow the newest and don't repeat the older wording (author, 2026-10-03): *Scripture Helps* (2025) before the Student Manual (1980).
- **The Student Manual is a starting point, not the spine** (author, 2026-10-03: “the Student Manual is kind of what this site is”; the site should be “a little higher level, more academic than the student manual”). The Church's Student Manuals (the *Old Testament Student Manual* above all) are good sources and anticipate readers' questions well, so a note may begin from one. Two rules of thumb keep them from becoming the substance of a chapter; `check-content.mjs` warns, and never fails, when a chapter breaks them.
  - **Concentration:** no more than about a third of a chapter's notes cite a Student Manual.
  - **Depth:** a note that draws on one should also rest on something from beyond the Church's own publications: the Hebrew or another ancient text, a commentary, a primary document, a BYU, Scripture Central or *Interpreter* article. Other scripture and the Church's own reference works don't count as beyond. The exception is a note that gives the Church's standard reading as such and says so.
  - Where the manual relays an author whose own work can't be read online, cite the manual for what it prints (rule 6 below); such a note still counts toward the limits.
  - The manual is the floor, not something to purge: the notes to deepen are those that rest only on the Church's sources. Scripture Central is the register to aim for (author, 2026-10-03: “Church-adjacent,” with “just the right amount of academic rigor”). The author accepted these two limits as proposed and was not sure where the line belongs, so the numbers may be adjusted.
- **Not a compendium** (author, 2026-10-01: “I just don't want this to be a compendium of everything that exists on the subject”). Asides from other traditions, later reception history (who quoted a verse, what a hymn or a theologian made of it), classical parallels and curiosities don't belong unless they answer a question the reader actually has about the text.
- **Don't replicate the Gospel Library** (author, 2026-10-03: “this site should not replicate the gospel library. What is easy to find while I read the scriptures shouldn't be here”). What the reader already has beside the verse there (the footnotes, the Joseph Smith Translation readings and appendix, the chapter heading, and *Scripture Helps*, which the author calls “really nice, but… alongside the scriptures in the Gospel Library”) is not repeated for its own sake. The Joseph Smith Translation is used judiciously: where it answers a question a reader would ask. Joseph Smith may have asked the same question, and the change may be his answer; then it belongs in the note.
- **Quote the Church's modern material sparingly** (author, 2026-10-07: “I'm cautious about using too much of the Church's material outright for 2 reasons: 1. I already have it in the Gospel library, and 2. I don't want to get in trouble with the copyright. I do think this is fair use, but we are pushing the limits by duplicating so much of the text”). It applies to everything the Church holds in copyright: *Scripture Helps*, the manuals, Topics and Questions and the Gospel Topics essays, chapter headings and footnotes, the current editions' wording. Say what such a page says in the site's own words and cite it; quote only the words that matter as words (a definition, a caution, a statement of the Church's position), a sentence at most as a rule; and never print the same passage on more than one page: say it once and link. Two exceptions (author, 2026-10-07): **a prophet's or apostle's teaching** may be quoted as a note on what he taught needs (a Latter-day Prophets note above all), since a paraphrase can misstate him, but kept short and never repeated on another page; and **the Church's statement of its position on a sensitive question** may be quoted in full where the book's brief calls for the Church's own words (the disavowals on race and the priesthood, for one). The rule sits beside “Most content comes from the Church's own sources” and does not change it: the Church's sources still come first, said in the site's words and cited. The ledger still records the page's own words as evidence; the limit is on what the site prints. The same restraint holds for any copyrighted source, though the Church's is where the site leans hardest. Checkers and reviewers report a note or section whose substance is the Church's quoted text. The rule is for new writing, from Abraham on; the books written before it are not gone over (author, 2026-10-07).

## 2. Audience

- **The primary reader is the author**, who reads mostly on an iPad mini. Favor what helps the author's study (their questions, readability, depth) over things that matter only for a public site (author, 2026-09-30: “this site's primary reader is—me”). Don't argue a design or a label from a hypothetical newcomer: the author chose the abbreviation “BoM Comparison” knowingly.
- **Semi-academic.** Write for an academically trained reader (for example, a PhD in a technical field) who is curious, comfortable with competing hypotheses, and expects claims to be documented, but who is not a specialist in biblical studies, Hebrew, or ancient Near Eastern history.
  - **Don't oversimplify.** Engage real questions directly: textual variants (Masoretic Text, Septuagint, Dead Sea Scrolls, Book of Mormon readings), dating and authorship, Near Eastern parallels and archaeology, translation choices, literary structure. State the positions fairly, including non–Latter-day Saint scholarship, say what is at stake, then give the Latter-day Saint reading and its basis.
  - **Explain the specialist tools.** Define a technical term the first time it appears (*qere/ketiv*, *Masoretic*, chiasmus, *prophetic perfect*). Transliterate Hebrew; explain morphology only when it changes the meaning.

## 3. Voice

- A **warm, knowledgeable teacher**. Conversational, a light touch of humor where it fits, willing to reframe a shallow reading.
- **Find each chapter's thread**: the argument that ties it together, not a list of topics. Notice structure and wordplay. Explain the history behind the text and *why* people thought as they did.
- **Hebrew (or Greek) only when it pays off.** (Author, 2026-10-11, of Psalms, where nearly half the notes had become Language notes and four in five cited the Hebrew or the lexicon: “Understanding the Hebrew can be helpful if it sheds insight into English text in answering the questions that come up. You don't need to make an index of the hebrew unless you really need it.”) The test for bringing in a Hebrew word is that a reader of the English verse has a question and the Hebrew answers it: the King James word misleads or is obscure, two English words hide one Hebrew word and the passage turns on it, or a rendering is disputed and the reading depends on it. What does not pass: saying what the Hebrew word behind an ordinary English word is; counting how often a word occurs; listing the verses where a word returns; noting that two lines share a root when nothing in the reading changes. The same holds for `thread` and `setting`. A chapter does not need a Language note, and most of its notes should not be one. It follows for the ledger: with no claim about the Hebrew there is no Hebrew row to write.
- Connect to Restoration scripture where the connection is real.
- **Read, ask, then seek** (author, 2026-10-02: “I might read a verse… and wonder what it means or who the person is that's in it…. read the text, try and postulate what someone might ask, and then find answers to those questions if they exist”). Read the chapter first and write down the questions a careful reader would ask; those questions decide which phrases get highlighted and what gets researched. Every note answers one of them. Don't go looking for material, and don't carry over what a source happens to cover. The questions are a working tool, not a record: they go in the writer's report, not in a file.
  - **Ask what a detail signifies, not only what it is** (author, 2026-10-04, asking of Isaiah 53:7, “What is the significance of its being dumb and not opening its mouth?”: “These are the kinds of questions that you should be asking as you develop the site as well”). When the text dwells on a detail (an image, a repeated phrase, something a figure does or doesn't do), the reader's question is why it is there. A note that only observes the detail (“the point of the image is silence”) hasn't answered it.
- **Open each note with a sentence that says what the note is about.** The site shows that first sentence as the note's preview card, so a hook (“This is the hardest line in the chapter”) doesn't work.
- **Quote with the reference; don't announce the source** (author, 2026-10-02). Not “The Church's *Old Testament Student Manual* says, ‘…’” but the quotation worked into the sentence, with its citation: the verses “bear strong witness that Jehovah… is the same person as Jesus Christ.”[@ot-manual-17] The citation carries the attribution. Scripture's own writers and speakers can be named (“John says…”, “Abinadi answers…”).
- **Keep attention on the message, not the scholar.** Don't name modern scholars in running text (“Oswalt argues…”); the note's `sources` carries the attribution. Ancient documents and ancient writers can be named when they are the evidence (“Sennacherib's annals claim…”, “Jerome connects…”).
- **Say how certain each claim is**: established fact, scholarly consensus, a plausible hypothesis, or a devotional reading. A devotional reading needs the same support as anything else (scripture, a Church source, or a scholar); an unsourced one is cut, however it is labeled.
- For graphic passages, describe rather than quote.
- **Selectivity.** There is no length target; a chapter is as long as its questions need. Depth over breadth: a few well-developed notes that make real connections beat many shallow ones. Where several sources make the same point, use the best one; where two notes overlap, merge or cut. Finding something is not a reason to include it. A note that is mostly a chain of cross-references, or a curiosity (a word fact or background detail that answers no question a reader would ask), is cut.
- **`setting` is short**, about 150–250 words: when, to whom, where the chapter sits in the book, how other scripture uses it. Debates about a verse go in that verse's note. A topic that spans many chapters is explained once on a theme page and linked.

## 4. Sourcing rules

**Everything is referenced; nothing is made up.**

1. **Every factual claim** (a word meaning, a textual variant, a date, a historical detail, a scholarly view, what a prophet taught) **comes from a source you opened and read in the working session, and is cited.** Your own memory is never a source, and neither is an entry under “Open questions.” If you can't find and read a source for a claim, cut the claim.
2. **Don't keep a detail by finding a source for it.** Before sourcing a claim, ask whether the chapter needs it (§1, “Not a compendium”). A peripheral detail is cut, not sourced. Reaching for a new outside work only to keep a side remark alive is the wrong move.
3. **Cite a work only for what you checked it says.** Never list a work because it is the kind of thing that work would say.
4. **Cite the work, not the website that hosts it.** Bible Hub, Scripture Central, BibleGateway, churchofjesuschrist.org and archive.org are where you read something; the source is the work: Delitzsch's commentary, BDB, the Westminster Leningrad Codex, the Septuagint, a specific KnoWhy or talk. The `sources.yaml` entry names the work (and may say where it was read); the ledger `url` is the page you actually read. Don't create keys for a website as such.
5. **The primary texts are evidence, not commentary.** The Hebrew text, the Septuagint, the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Book of Mormon's readings and the rest of scripture answer many questions directly; prefer them to a commentary that reports them. A note whose claims rest only on scripture cites `lds-scriptures` (the Church's editions of the scriptures, including their chapter and section headings), with a ledger row pointing at the chapter read.
6. **Cite the underlying work behind a secondary source.** If a KnoWhy, dictionary, or commentary passes along an idea from another work, read that work and cite it too. If you can't read it, cite only the secondary source for what the secondary source itself says, or leave the claim out.
7. **Wikipedia, other wikis, and popular history websites (such as Livius.org) are never sources** (author, 2026-09-30: “I don't want this to become a mini-wikipedia”; Livius ruled out 2026-10-01). Use the primary text or a scholarly or institutional source: the text itself, a lexicon, an encyclopedia, a museum's own catalog page. If only a wiki supports a claim, cut it. This includes wiki transcriptions of public-domain books (Wikisource and the like): read the work from a scan or an institutional copy (archive.org, a library, the publisher) and cite that URL. `check-content.mjs` rejects wiki URLs in `sources.yaml` and in ledgers.
8. **No unofficial copies of copyrighted works.** Don't read from, link to, or cite pirated PDFs or unauthorized uploads. Cite the official DOI or publisher URL.
9. **Commentaries that can't be read online are not cited unless genuinely read.** Known unreadable here: Oswalt (NICOT), Blenkinsopp (Anchor Bible), Childs, Shalom Paul, Westermann, *HALOT*, Parry's *Understanding Isaiah*, Williamson's *The Book Called Isaiah*, Tov's *Textual Criticism*, Matthews' *A Plainer Translation*. Each book's `BRIEF.md` lists what is and isn't readable for that book; add to both lists when you find another, and to `NOT_READABLE` in `scripts/check-content.mjs`. The checker lets a strict chapter cite one of these only with a quoted ledger row, so the quote must come from a copy you actually read.
10. **Confirm every URL before it goes into `content/sources.yaml`**: it must load a page with the right title. Bible Dictionary and Guide to the Scriptures URLs return 200 even for entries that don't exist, so check the page heading. Put page or chapter numbers in the entry's `pub` field; note `sources` take keys only. `node scripts/check-sources.mjs <book> <chapters…>` does the opening for the proposed entries (and `--keys a,b` for entries already in `sources.yaml`): it prints each entry's title, author and `pub` beside the page's own title and first heading and flags a page that didn't load, a block page, a Bible Dictionary, Guide to the Scriptures or Topical Guide entry that isn't there, a wiki, a PDF with no text, and a title that shares no word with the page's. The author, date and page numbers are still read from the page by whoever cites it.
11. **No single secondary source shapes a chapter.** Start from the text and the scriptures that quote it. As a rule of thumb, no one outside source is the main idea of more than one note. When an idea comes from a source, cite it, put it in your own words, and don't follow its outline. The Student Manuals have their own limits (§1).
12. **List in `sources` only what the chapter relies on.** A chapter-level `sources` list is the union of what its notes and prose actually cite, not a reading list.
13. **Quotations are checked**: scripture quotations with `check-quotes.mjs`; Book of Mormon variants against the text (`data/bom/`); quotations from talks and books against the page you read.
14. **Record guidance, not every judgment call** (author, 2026-10-11: “Recording every judgement call seems overkill. Recording guidance is important”). Guidance is what the next writer needs in order to do the same thing the same way: a rule the author gives, how the book handles a question that comes up in many chapters, where a shared term is explained. It is written down where it applies (here, `AUTHORING.md`, or the book's brief), in a line or two. A judgment call is one chapter's choice about one verse; it is made and not logged. What needs the author goes in `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` at the top of the repository, the one list for every book. A decision that sets how the *book* does something (where a shared term is explained, how a disputed question is handled in every chapter) is written into the book's brief as a rule, in a line. A reviewer's verse-by-verse decisions are not recorded anywhere: the chapter and its commit are the record (author, 2026-10-11: “We don't have to record every decision of every verse. That's absurd”).

## 5. The evidence ledger

Required for every chapter written or audited from now on. It is the record of what was read for each claim, and `scripts/check-content.mjs` validates it.

```yaml
# content/<book>/evidence/NN.yaml  (NN = two-digit chapter number)
chapter: 5
claims:
  - where: 'note 5:3 “Title of the note”'   # or: setting | thread | when | section 2 plain | christ | explore | parallels
    claim: 'Short statement of the factual claim'
    key: bdb                                  # key in content/sources.yaml
    url: 'https://…'                          # the exact page actually read
    quote: 'Verbatim supporting text, at most about 40 words'
```

- `where` names the part of the chapter file: `note <chapter>:<verse> “<note title>”` (the title as it is in the chapter now: if a note is retitled, its rows are), `setting`, `thread`, `when`, `section <n> plain` (sections numbered from 1), `christ`, `explore`, or `parallels`. `check-content.mjs` warns about a note with no row that names it, a key in a note's `sources` with no row for that note, a `where` that names no note or section, and a passage in curly quotes in a note that no row for the note quotes (a quotation followed by a `[[reference]]` is `check-quotes.mjs`'s).
- One source per row. A claim supported by two sources gets two rows.
- `url` is the exact page you read (the lexicon entry, the interlinear verse, the article), not a site's home page.
- `quote` is copied from that page, not paraphrased or recalled: from the page text as `scripts/source.mjs` prints it, or as `curl` returns it with the tags stripped (§9), never from a tool's summary of the page. Keep it short; for a lexicon or interlinear, quote the relevant line. Mark an elision with “…”.
- **Rules:**
  - Every factual claim in the chapter has at least one row. A claim with no row does not go in the chapter.
  - Every source key cited anywhere in the chapter appears in the ledger at least once.
  - What the chapter's own verses say, and scripture quoted with a `[[reference]]` (checked by `check-quotes.mjs`), need no row. A claim *about* a scripture beyond its plain words (its date, its Hebrew, a variant, how a tradition reads it) does. So does a plain-words rendering that departs from the KJV's sense.
  - The ledger is committed with the chapter, in the same commit.
  - Reviewers spot-check it by reopening the URLs.
  - If a source isn't in `sources.yaml` yet, use the key you propose for it; the orchestrator adds the entry before the checks pass. Until then `check-content.mjs` reports “unknown source key” for it; that error is expected, and every other error must be fixed. `check-content.mjs <book> <n>` adds the entries in `.cache/batch/<book>/proposed/NN.yaml` to `sources.yaml` in memory by itself and prints which keys came from there, so no scratch copy of `content/` is needed (`--root` still works).

## 6. Chapter file structure

Files for a book: `content/<book>/book.yaml`, `chapters/`, `guides/`, `themes/`, `evidence/`; `data/kjv/<book>.json`; optionally `data/bom/<book>-parallels.json`. “Adding a new book” in `DEVELOPMENT.md` lists the `book.yaml` fields. Markdown files at the top of `content/<book>/` (`BRIEF.md`, `THEME-CANDIDATES.md`) are project notes and aren't published.

`content/<book>/chapters/NN.yaml` (look at an existing chapter for the exact layout). Fields:

- `chapter`, `title`, `tagline`, `when`.
- `setting`: the historical background (150–250 words).
- `thread`: the argument that ties the chapter together.
- `sections`: each has `range`, `heading`, and `plain` (a plain-words paraphrase). The ranges cover every verse exactly once.
  - **Explain the passage in plain words** (author, 2026-10-11). Start from the King James text displayed on the page, but allow a well-supported clarification from the original language or another translation when it materially improves understanding. Explain a meaningful departure in a visible chapter note and support it in the ledger; a ledger row alone does not explain the difference to the reader. Where the reading is disputed, do not present an alternative as settled. A language pass must preserve these supported clarifications.
- `notes`: see below.
- `christ`: how the chapter points to Jesus Christ, then, in one to three plain sentences, where that same principle reaches a disciple's life now. Grounded in the scripture and the prophets the section cites, said as a statement, never a question or an exhortation (§1, “Apply scripture to the reader”).
- `explore`: what's worth exploring next (a short list of scripture threads, and a pointer to an outside resource only if one is especially good).
- `parallels`: `ref`, `note`, and `primary: true` for a Book of Mormon chapter that quotes the whole chapter.
- `sources`: keys from `content/sources.yaml`.

Each note has:

- `ref`: the verse. In Psalms, `ref: 0` is the psalm's title (“A Psalm of David, when he fled from Absalom his son”), which the page shows above verse 1 with no number; `phrase` is then a substring of the title as `data/kjv/psalms.titles.json` has it (`node scripts/show.mjs psalms <n>` prints it as verse 0), and the note's ledger rows say `note <psalm>:0 “<title of the note>”`.
- `phrase`: an **exact** substring of that verse, in the spellings of `data/kjv/<book>.json` (the Latter-day Saint edition). Phrases in the same verse must not overlap.
- `kind`: see “Note kinds.”
- `title`: plain and descriptive.
- `body`: Markdown.
- `sources`: keys from `content/sources.yaml`. Every note that makes a factual claim has them.

Write scripture references as `[[2 Ne. 25:4]]` or `[[D&C 113:1–6]]`; references to a book on the site become internal links. Use en dashes in ranges. Section headings shown to the reader (“Background”, “The thread through the chapter”, and the rest) come from `SECTIONS` in `src/site.mjs`; templates use `book.name`, never a hard-coded book title.

## 7. Note kinds

One set for every book. Labels and one-line descriptions live in `KINDS` in `src/site.mjs`; that is the authority.

| kind | label | use for |
|---|---|---|
| `words` | Language | What a word or phrase meant in the original language or in 1611 English. |
| `history` | Context | The people, places, and events behind the text. |
| `symbol` | Imagery | What an image would have meant to its first hearers. |
| `christ` | Witness of Christ | Where the passage points to Jesus Christ and His Atonement. |
| `scripture` | Related Scriptures | Other scripture that quotes, explains, or fulfills the passage. |
| `prophets` | Latter-day Prophets | What latter-day prophets and apostles have taught about it. |
| `structure` | Literary Structure | Only when the structure shows the message (a chiasm whose center is the point). Saying a passage is poetry or a chiasm is not enough. |
| `bom` | BoM Comparison | Generated from `data/bom/` where the Book of Mormon text differs. A hand-written `bom` note explains why a reading matters; it appears inside that verse's comparison panel, so the verse must have one (the build warns if not), and its phrase isn't highlighted. |

Anything new (a kind, a section, a label) gets a plain, non-clever name, and goes to the author first.

## 8. Guides and theme pages

**Enough historical context to understand the book** (author, 2026-10-11). Every book needs background that explains the circumstances its readers encounter: who is speaking, to whom, where, and what is happening. The depth depends on the book's length and complexity; a short book may need only its introduction and chapter settings, while a longer book may need a historical guide. Combine rulers and events where that makes the history easier to follow, rather than repeating it in an overview, timeline, and separate lists. State uncertainty about dates or settings where the sources leave it open.

**A guide belongs to the volume when its content is true of more than one book** (author, 2026-10-12): the kingdoms and the exile, the land, the law and the covenant, the temple, Hebrew poetry, the household and its customs are the Old Testament's background, not one book's. Such a guide is in `content/volumes/<volume>/guides/` and is listed on the volume's page and its Guides page, never on a book's (author, 2026-10-18: “No book should inherit the volume guides.”). **A book with no guides of its own has no Guides page and no Guides link** (author, 2026-10-18: “If a book doesn't have guides, it does not inherit the guides from the volume. Just don't have guides for that book.”); its chapters and book page still link to a volume guide's sections where they use them. A book's guides keep what is particular to the book and link to the volume's instead of repeating them, and a book gets guides of its own only when it has two or more topics its book page cannot hold; a single topic goes into the book page's intro or a volume guide. The author's decisions about a volume's guides are in `content/volumes/<volume>/GUIDES.md`.

Markdown files in `content/<book>/guides/` and `content/<book>/themes/`, with YAML front matter (`title`, `blurb`, `icon`, `sources`) and then prose. Cite with a numbered citation right after the claim: `[@key]` or `[@key, locator]`. A citation sits before a comma, not after it (`the nation[@bd-cyrus], and`; author, 2026-10-13: “The comma that precedes any citation is not helpful”); after a period or semicolon it follows the mark. This is required: `check-content.mjs` fails a page that lists sources but cites none. Sources in the page's `sources` that are never cited appear under “Further reading.” Give sections that chapters link to an explicit id (`<h2 id="song-2">…</h2>`). The same sourcing rules apply.

**The ledger for a guide or theme page** is `content/<book>/evidence/themes/<file name>.yaml` (or `evidence/guides/…`), in the format of §5 with `page: <file name>` in place of `chapter:`; `where` names the section heading (or `opening`). A page written from now on has one, committed with the page; `check-content.mjs` then holds it to the same rules as a chapter with a ledger, and warns when a theme page is over 1,000 words, when a `[@key]` is cited in a section whose ledger has no row for that key under that section (so `where` must be spelled as the heading is), and when a source is announced in the text (§3). It lists the rows whose claim begins “Connection made by this page:”, so a reviewer sees which connections are the page's own. `check-ledger.mjs <book> themes` (or `guides`, or one page's file name) reopens every row's URL, as for a chapter (§11).

### What earns a theme page

(Author, 2026-10-01.) **A theme is broader than a chapter.** Everything else on the site goes verse by verse or chapter by chapter; a theme page connects chapters in a bigger arc. It is worth writing only if it helps the reader understand **the bigger story that points to Jesus Christ**.

- **Short enough to look over quickly** (author, 2026-10-02: “I didn't really want a full essay, but something I can quickly look at”). A table of the passages in order carries the arc; each step gets a few sentences, mostly scripture and short quotations. Well under 1,000 words. If a step needs more, it belongs in a chapter note that the page links to.
- **The insight is about Christ's work, not a link to His words** (author, 2026-10-15, of Leviticus: “what insights it can give us about Jesus Christ, His Atonement, His mercy and redemption, and how it applies to the reader… It is not necessarily how it suddenly becomes Christ because He quoted it—although that can be a strong motivation”). A page earns its place by what the arc teaches about the Savior, His Atonement, His mercy and His redemption. An arc whose only way to Christ is that He or an apostle quoted a verse, or that a later passage shares a word or an image, is the weak kind: the quotation may close the page, but it is not the insight. “How it applies to the reader” is the next point. The measure is Elder D. Todd Christofferson's statement of what scripture is for (the author's guidance, 2026-10-15): “the central purpose of all scripture is to fill our souls with faith in God the Father and in His Son, Jesus Christ—faith that They exist; faith in the Father's plan for our immortality and eternal life; faith in the Atonement and Resurrection of Jesus Christ, which animates this plan of happiness; faith to make the gospel of Jesus Christ our way of life” (“The Blessing of Scripture,” April 2010 general conference, `https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2010/04/the-blessing-of-scripture?lang=eng&id=p16#p16`). Ask of a candidate which of those it builds. Proposers (Fable, Codex and the coordinator) judge every candidate by this, and say for each what it teaches of Christ's work.
- **It applies the arc to the reader** (author, 2026-10-16: “All scripture needs to be applied to the reader/student, otherwise, what's the point… What I don't want is the site to be asking questions like the *Come, Follow Me* manual. Certainly we should be identifying where this same principle applies to me”). After the arc and its witness of Christ, the page names where the same principle reaches a disciple's life now: Moses, refused the one thing he asked for himself after forty faithful years, and anyone who righteously asks for what the Lord in His wisdom does not give. Say it plainly, as a statement, and ground it in scripture and the words of prophets and apostles (§1's sources), not in the page's own counsel. Not questions to the reader (“What blessing have you…?”), invitations or exercises (“Ponder…”, “Consider writing…”), exhortation, or anecdotes. The application follows from what the arc teaches of Christ; it is not a lesson laid over the passage. A proposed theme says how it applies as well as how it leads to Christ.
- **It is an arc, not a list.** The page says what changes from the first passage to the last. If the passages only share a word or an image and nothing develops, it is not a theme page.
- **A topic that lives in one chapter or a few neighboring verses belongs in that chapter's notes.**
- **Few, not many.** A book gets a handful of theme pages. **`THEME-CANDIDATES.md` is a short list of proposed arcs, not a record of everything noticed** (author, 2026-10-11, of a Psalms file that had grown to some 170 unsorted leads: “When you suggest theme candidates, that doesn't mean you need to write down every note ever. You should be making connections that haven't been made before and suggest some new themes or multi-chapter arcs that would shed *new* insight”). A candidate is worth proposing when it joins several chapters in a way a reader would not see chapter by chapter and that changes how a passage is understood, above all how it points to Jesus Christ. A word, image or kind of passage that merely recurs is not a candidate. The coordinator proposes about ten at most, after the book is written, each with its passages, its arc, where it reaches Christ in scripture, and what it must not claim; Psalms' file is the model. Reviewers' leads are working notes (`.cache/batch/<book>/theme-leads.md`) and are not filed.
- **The page links, it doesn't repeat.** Verse-level detail stays in the chapter notes; the theme page points to them. When a chapter touches a theme that has a page, the chapter links to it.

### Where the meaning comes from

- **The text supplies the pattern.** That a word recurs, that the speaker changes, that one passage answers another in the same words: these are observations a reader can check in the text, and the text is the source (§4 rule 5). List every occurrence, including those that don't fit, before calling something a pattern, and check a repeated English word in the original language. `scripts/occurrences.mjs` makes the list (§10), so the count is the script's.
- **Scripture and the Church supply the meaning.** What a passage means is said as far as scripture, a Church source, or a scholar says it, and is cited. Where faithful sources read a passage differently, say so.
- **A theme page may make connections of its own** (author, 2026-10-03: “this is where AI can make a real contribution; we can make connections that perhaps may not have been made before, that could help us better understand Jesus Christ and His mission. So, only with the book themes, we can relax a little the connection to published sources. We don't want to be inventing doctrine or contradicting those things that have already been revealed”; confirmed 2026-10-11: these connections are foundational to the purpose of theme pages). This replaces the earlier rule that a page adds no readings of its own, and it applies to theme pages only; chapters and guides keep §4. So a theme candidate is not dropped only because no source carries it to Christ (two of Jeremiah's were, and the author wanted them written). The author's words set two boundaries, no new doctrine and nothing against what is revealed. The limits:
  - **The connection rests on words the reader can check.** Both passages are quoted, and what joins them is in the text: a shared word or image, the same act, one verse answering another. Check a shared English word in the original language.
  - **Facts are still sourced.** What a word means, what a passage's setting is, what a prophet or a manual said: these keep §4 and get ledger rows. Only the step from one passage to another may be the page's own.
  - **No new doctrine, and nothing against what is revealed.** Each side of a connection says what scripture and the Church already teach about it. A connection that would need a doctrine no scripture or Church source states is cut. Where a Church source reads a passage one way, the page does not read it another.
  - **Say which is which.** The reader can tell a connection the sources make from one the page makes: the sourced one carries its citation; the page's own is worded as a comparison (“set beside,” “the same image”) and not as a statement of what the passage means or what the prophet intended. The ledger gives it a row with `key: lds-scriptures` (or the text's key) whose claim begins “Connection made by this page:”.
  - **The arc still has to point to Christ from scripture.** A page's own connection can join Jeremiah to a passage about the Savior; what that passage says about Him comes from the passage and the Church's sources.
- **Summaries are claims too.** The blurb, the opening's statement of the arc, the transitions, and the closing say only what the sections establish from their sources. This is where a page most easily says more than its sources: by harmonizing readings that differ, by word order that implies an identification no source makes, or by a tidy line of the writer's own. (In the first page written this way, nearly every fix the reviewer required was a sentence of this kind.)

### How a theme page is written

Four steps, with a different model for each kind of work (the agent instructions are in `.claude/agents/`):

1. **Research** (`theme-researcher`, Sonnet): gathers every occurrence in the text, the scripture that quotes or explains the passages, what Church sources and faithful scholarship say, and what the site already says, into a dossier with verbatim quotes. It records what it looked for and did not find. It does not interpret. The coordinator starts the dossier with `node scripts/theme-dossier.mjs` (every occurrence of the words in the text and the Hebrew, the same phrases in the other books, the Church's footnotes and *Scripture Helps* for the key chapters, and what the site already says, each under the command that made it), and the researcher fills the rest, reading pages with `source.mjs` (§9); so the list of occurrences is the script's and not the agent's reading.
2. **Outline** (a Fable agent; author, 2026-10-14, of finding the candidates and setting the outline: “Let's set Fable for that task,” for every book from Amos on, not going back to books already done; the coordinator spawns it with the dossier and the chosen arc, and reads the outline before the writing starts): decides from the dossier whether there is an arc at all, what its steps are, and which source carries each step. Drops whatever the dossier can't support. This is the step where a weak connection can be made to sound convincing, so it is not delegated. Two things the outline does not contain (both were cut from pages on 2026-10-11): a section that only lists words two chapters share, with nothing drawn from them, and a comparison of the page's own that rests on a word the book uses everywhere (check the count) or that ends by saying only that the two passages differ. A connection of the page's own earns its place by changing what the reader sees in a passage; if the outline can't say what that is, the connection is left out. The page's title is not a chapter's title.
3. **Writing** (`theme-writer`, Sonnet): writes the page and its ledger from the outline and the dossier. Connections of the page's own come from the outline, not from the writer. It runs `check-content` for the page and `check-ledger` on its ledger and fixes what they report.
4. **Review** (`theme-reviewer`, Opus, which did not write the page): runs `check-ledger` on the page's ledger first and does not reopen a source only to see that a quote is on its page; reads the passage around the quotes the script can't judge (every row that carries a step of the arc or the connection to Christ, and a third of the rest), tests the page against “What earns a theme page” and “Where the meaning comes from” (counts and “only here” claims with `occurrences.mjs`), and returns a list of fixes, or says the theme doesn't hold up.

Then the author reads it in the preview (§11). The dossier is a working file and is not kept; the ledger is.

### How a guide is written

The same four steps and the same three agents, each of which has a section “When the page is a guide”; `DEVELOPMENT.md` (“Writing a book in batches”) has the commands. What differs:

- **A guide is background, not an arc.** It says what a period, a custom or an institution was, where the text shows it, and what scripture and the Church teach about it. Nothing has to develop, and it is not held to 1,000 words, though it is still a page to consult: “long enough to cover the subject, but short enough to still be interesting” (author, 2026-10-12, of a 4,000-word first draft of the women guide that was too long to read; about 2,000 words is the measure). Detail goes in the chapter notes the guide links to.
- **§4 holds in full.** A guide makes no connections of its own: every statement is a quoted verse's plain words or is cited, and its ledger has no “Connection made by this page:” row.
- **It explains; it doesn't argue.** No sentence defends, excuses or condemns beyond its source, an evaluative word is a claim with a row, and a hard passage is named plainly and given what the sources give it.
- **The outline is still the session's**, on the most capable model, and for a guide the author has asked to approve, the author reads the outline before anything is written.
- **Writing, review, repairs and the language pass are one workflow**, `.claude/workflows/write-guide.js`; the page starts with `status: preview` and every agent checks it with `node scripts/check-page.mjs <owner> <file name>`.
- **Afterwards the chapters link to it**: one sentence at the end of each note that touches the guide's subject, pointing to the section by its id.

## 9. Research tools and access

The scripts and their options are described once, in `DEVELOPMENT.md` (“Checks” and “Research scripts”); each prints its usage when run with no arguments. This section is what a writer needs to know about where things can be read.

- **Read a page with `node scripts/source.mjs '<url>'`, never a web-fetch tool.** It prints the page's own text, so a quote copied from it is verbatim, and it caches the page for a day so that every agent fetches it once. Search a page, don't read it through: `--find "words"` prints the paragraphs that have the words; `--para`, `--notes` and `--all` the rest. It reads the Gospel Library with its footnotes, Scripture Central KnoWhys (through the API; the public pages need a browser), Joseph Smith Papers transcripts, PDFs with a text layer, Bible Hub, *Interpreter*, rsc.byu.edu, byustudies.byu.edu and archive.org full texts (`…/download/<id>/<id>_djvu.txt`). If a page reads badly, `curl -sgL -A 'Mozilla/5.0' '<url>' | sed 's/<[^>]*>//g'` is the fallback. A web-fetch tool's summary is never a source of quotes.
- **Start a chapter with `node scripts/footnotes.mjs <book> <n>`:** the Church's heading, footnotes and the *Scripture Helps* chapter with its endnotes. ***Scripture Helps* is a finder** (author, 2026-10-03: “we can use the scripture helps to point us to good academic sources…. We *should* use them”; should, not must): its endnotes name the studies behind each statement; read the readable ones and cite them for what they say (§4 rule 6). The manual itself is beside the verse in the Gospel Library, so a note doesn't repeat it (§1).
- **Latter-day Saint studies first** (§1, and `AUTHORING.md`, “Keil and the Student Manual come last”). BYU Studies lists them chapter by chapter on its *Come, Follow Me* pages (`byustudies.byu.edu/come-follow-me/old-testament/<lesson>`) and by book in its bibliography of Latter-day Saint writing on the Old Testament, 1830–1997 (`byustudies.byu.edu/article/reading-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-restoration-a-comprehensive-bibliography-of-lds-writings-on-the-old-testament-18301997`; search it with `--find`). `node scripts/search-lds.mjs "<words>"` searches the Religious Studies Center's full text, the titles of BYU Studies and the *Interpreter* journal, and the KnoWhys at once, and prints the web search to make for what it could not reach. `node scripts/knowhys.mjs <book> <n>` searches Scripture Central's KnoWhys; its archive (`archive.bookofmormoncentral.org/content/<slug>`) holds articles and book chapters too, each linking a PDF. The *Interpreter* journal's articles are listed by `curl -sL -A 'Mozilla/5.0' https://interpreterfoundation.org/journal-sitemap.xml | grep -o '<loc>[^<]*' | grep -i '<word>'` (the site's own search needs a browser); when `interpreterfoundation.org/journal/<slug>` returns no text, read `journal.interpreterfoundation.org/<slug>/` before calling it unreadable. The Religious Studies Center (`rsc.byu.edu`) is readable; **ScholarsArchive PDFs are not** (only the abstract page), so cite only what the abstract says. Scripture Central and *Interpreter* are faithful and scholarly but not official: cite the article, never the site, and give an author's own reading as that author's.
- **The Hebrew** is the Westminster Leningrad Codex (`oshb-wlc`): `node scripts/hebrew.mjs <book> <c>:<v>` for a verse's words with Strong's numbers and lexicon pages, `--word <strongs>` or `occurrences.mjs --hebrew` for where a word occurs; both use the King James numbering and print the Hebrew reference beside a verse that differs. **Lexicon:** BDB (`bdb`), fullest on Bible Hub's lexicon pages (`biblehub.com/hebrew/<strongs>.htm`); quote only what the page says. **Septuagint:** Brenton on Bible Hub (`biblehub.com/sep/<book>/<c>.htm`), cited `lxx-rahlfs`. **Other translations** on BibleGateway, cited by their own key. **Older commentaries** (Keil and Delitzsch and the like) on Bible Hub (`biblehub.com/commentaries/kad/<book>/<c>.htm`); `source.mjs` decodes their Hebrew entities.
- **Church sources**: the scriptures, Bible Dictionary and Guide to the Scriptures (a missing entry still returns a page: check the heading), manuals, general conference, the Joseph Smith Papers, hymns. **Museum catalog pages** are good institutional sources for artifacts.
- **If a site fails twice, stop trying it** and say so in the report; don't probe other domains for a way around. A claim whose only source can't be reached is cut. Sefaria has not loaded from the shell. Each book's brief lists what was and wasn't readable for it; add what you learn there.

## 10. Checks

`node scripts/check-chapter.mjs <book> <n>` runs every check for one chapter (`fix-yaml`, the build, `check-quotes`, `check-content` with the chapter's proposed sources, `check-ledger`) and prints only the lines that concern it; `node scripts/check-page.mjs <book or volume> <page>` runs them for a theme or guide page (the build, `check-quotes`, `check-content <book> <page>`, `check-ledger <book> <page>`, and `check-sources` on its proposed entries). Fix every warning that concerns your chapter or page; a length warning on an older chapter is the author's to decide. What each check tests, and every other script, is in `DEVELOPMENT.md` (“Checks”); `node scripts/status.mjs` prints where each book stands.

## 11. Review process

How a chapter is written and reviewed, step by step, with the models and why, is in `AUTHORING.md` (“Plan for the next book”); the scripts for each step are in `DEVELOPMENT.md` (“Writing a book in batches”); a theme page's four steps are in §8. Three rules hold for every page:

- **Every new page ends with a second reader** (author, 2026-10-07): after its own review, Codex reads it for language only, the findings that hold up are applied without adding a fact, reference or connection, and the checks are rerun.
- **One commit per chapter**, with its ledger; a theme page and its ledger likewise.
- **Judgment calls** (§4 rule 14): what needs the author goes in `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`; what sets a rule for the whole book goes in the brief, in a line; the rest is not logged.
