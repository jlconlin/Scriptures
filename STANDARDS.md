# Writing and sourcing standard

The standard for all commentary on this site, for every book. `AUTHORING.md` holds how a book is written and how to work with the author; `PAGES.md` holds the standard for theme pages and guides; each book's `content/<book>/BRIEF.md` holds the decisions for that book; `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` holds everything waiting for the author. Where they disagree, this file wins unless the author has decided otherwise in the brief. A date in parentheses marks a rule as the author's.

The goal: **content that needs no audit afterward.** Sourcing is part of writing, not a later pass.

## 1. What the site is

- **A focused companion to the scriptures.** It gives one clear reading of each chapter. It is not a database and does not gather everything written about a chapter. When another resource is especially good on a chapter, point to it once (usually in `explore`) rather than absorbing it.
- **Deeper than *Come, Follow Me*, and not a companion to it.** Don't repeat the manual or follow its outline.
- **Apply scripture to the reader, in statements, never in questions** (2026-10-16). A chapter's `christ` section, and a theme page after its arc (§8), say where the principle reaches a disciple's life now. No questions to the reader, no invitations or exercises, no exhortation, no anecdotes. A brief written before 2026-10-16 that says “no personal application” is overruled.
- **Stay near Latter-day Saint theology** (2026-10-01). The center of every chapter is what the text says and how Restoration scripture and latter-day prophets read it. Scholarship, history and language serve that: use them where they change how a reader understands the verse, not to survey what has been said. When an interpretation is speculative, or faithful readers disagree, say so.
- **Most content comes from the Church's own sources** (2026-10-02). In order: the Church's sources (the scriptures and their study helps, the teachings of prophets and apostles, the Church's reference works, the Joseph Smith Papers; a teaching manual is a finder, below); BYU publications (the Religious Studies Center, BYU Studies); Scripture Central and the Interpreter Foundation's journal (2026-10-07). Other commentary and scholarship is used sparingly, when those have no answer. Everything follows the Church's teaching: an outside reading that differs from the Church's is not set beside it as an equal alternative. Where the Church's own sources differ, follow the newest and don't repeat the older wording (2026-10-03).
- **The Church's teaching manuals are finders, never sources** (2026-11-17, replacing 2026-10-03's “a starting point, not the spine”; author: “all those manuals are finders not primary source material”). *Come, Follow Me*, *Scripture Helps*, the seminary and institute manuals, and the Student Manuals (Old Testament, New Testament, Pearl of Great Price and the rest) are read to find what is worth reading, and are never cited. Read what a manual cites (the talk, the scripture, the Joseph Smith Papers document, the study) and cite that for what it says (§4 rule 6). A statement in a manual with nothing behind it is cited from scripture or from a prophet or apostle who says the same, or it is left out; so is a statement whose underlying work can't be read online. A manual's application is not the site's application: `christ` applies the chapter from scripture and the prophets (above), not by restating a lesson. *Teachings of Presidents of the Church* is a prophet's own words and may be cited as his teaching; the Bible Dictionary, the Guide to the Scriptures and Gospel Topics are reference works, not teaching manuals, and are cited as before. The rule is for new writing. Pages written before it still cite manuals, and they stay as they are until the books are revised (author, 2026-11-17: “I don't want to make a wide change now”; `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`, “Work waiting its turn”); `check-content.mjs` lists every manual a chapter cites.
- **Not a compendium** (2026-10-01). Asides from other traditions, later reception history (who quoted a verse, what a hymn or a theologian made of it), classical parallels and curiosities don't belong unless they answer a question the reader actually has about the text.
- **Don't replicate the Gospel Library** (2026-10-03: “What is easy to find while I read the scriptures shouldn't be here”). What the reader already has beside the verse there (the footnotes, the Joseph Smith Translation, the chapter heading, *Scripture Helps*) is not repeated for its own sake. The Joseph Smith Translation is used judiciously, where it answers a question a reader would ask: Joseph Smith may have asked the same question, and the change may be his answer; then it belongs in the note.
- **Quote the Church's modern material sparingly** (2026-10-07), for copyright and because the reader already has it. This covers everything the Church holds in copyright: *Scripture Helps*, the manuals, Topics and Questions, chapter headings and footnotes. Say what such a page says in the site's own words and cite it; quote only the words that matter as words (a definition, a caution, a statement of the Church's position), a sentence at most as a rule; and never print the same passage on more than one page: say it once and link. Two exceptions: a prophet's or apostle's teaching may be quoted as a note on what he taught needs, kept short and never repeated on another page; and the Church's statement of its position on a sensitive question may be quoted in full where the book's brief calls for it. The ledger still records the page's own words as evidence; the limit is on what the site prints. The same restraint holds for any copyrighted source. Checkers and reviewers report a note whose substance is the Church's quoted text. The rule is for new writing, from Abraham on; the books written before it are not gone over.

## 2. Audience

- **The primary reader is the author** (2026-09-30), who reads mostly on an iPad mini. Favor what helps the author's study (their questions, readability, depth) over what matters only for a public site, and don't argue a design or a label from a hypothetical newcomer.
- **Semi-academic.** Write for an academically trained reader (a PhD in a technical field, say) who is curious, comfortable with competing hypotheses, and expects claims to be documented, but is not a specialist in biblical studies, Hebrew, or ancient Near Eastern history.
  - **Don't oversimplify.** Engage real questions directly: textual variants, dating and authorship, Near Eastern parallels, translation choices, literary structure. State the positions fairly, including non–Latter-day Saint scholarship, say what is at stake, then give the Latter-day Saint reading and its basis.
  - **Explain the specialist tools.** Define a technical term the first time it appears (*qere/ketiv*, *Masoretic*, chiasmus). Transliterate Hebrew; explain morphology only when it changes the meaning.

## 3. Voice

- A **warm, knowledgeable teacher**. Conversational, a light touch of humor where it fits, willing to reframe a shallow reading.
- **Find each chapter's thread**: the argument that ties it together, not a list of topics. Notice structure and wordplay. Explain the history behind the text and *why* people thought as they did.
- **A connected explanation** answers its question, develops the answer across sentences and paragraphs, and says what its quotations and alternative readings contribute. Read for answer, progression and integration, and name a gap only when it actually obstructs the explanation; not every inference need be explicit. Useful repetition, legitimate uncertainty, several witnesses and length are not faults by themselves. Local wording or a slight transition is polish; a missing answer, relationship or relevance needs revision. A setting supplies orientation; a thread explains the chapter's movement without forcing independent episodes into one thesis.
- **Read, ask, then seek** (2026-10-02). Read the chapter first and write down the questions a careful reader would ask; those questions decide which phrases get highlighted and what gets researched. Every note answers one of them. Don't go looking for material, and don't carry over what a source happens to cover. The questions go in the writer's report, not in a file.
  - **Ask what a detail signifies, not only what it is** (2026-10-04). When the text dwells on a detail (an image, a repeated phrase, something a figure does or doesn't do), the reader's question is why it is there. A note that only observes the detail hasn't answered it.
- **Hebrew (or Greek) only when it pays off** (2026-10-11). The test: a reader of the English verse has a question and the Hebrew answers it. The King James word misleads or is obscure, two English words hide one Hebrew word and the passage turns on it, or a rendering is disputed and the reading depends on it. What does not pass: naming the Hebrew word behind an ordinary English one, counting how often a word occurs, listing where it returns, noting a shared root when nothing in the reading changes. The same holds for `thread` and `setting`. A chapter does not need a Language note, and most of its notes should not be one; with no claim about the Hebrew there is no Hebrew row to write.
- Connect to Restoration scripture where the connection is real.
- **Open each note with a sentence that says what the note is about.** The site shows that sentence as the note's preview card, so a hook (“This is the hardest line in the chapter”) doesn't work.
- **Quote with the reference; don't announce the source** (2026-10-02). Not “The Church's *Old Testament Student Manual* says, ‘…’” but the quotation worked into the sentence, with its citation carrying the attribution. Scripture's own writers and speakers can be named (“John says…”, “Abinadi answers…”).
- **Keep attention on the message, not the scholar.** Don't name modern scholars in running text (“Oswalt argues…”); the note's `sources` carries the attribution. Ancient documents and writers can be named when they are the evidence (“Sennacherib's annals claim…”, “Jerome connects…”).
- **Say how certain each claim is**: established fact, scholarly consensus, a plausible hypothesis, or a devotional reading. A devotional reading needs the same support as anything else; an unsourced one is cut, however it is labeled.
- For graphic passages, describe rather than quote.
- **Selectivity.** There is no length target; a chapter is as long as its questions need. A few well-developed notes beat many shallow ones. Where several sources make the same point, use the best one; where two notes overlap, merge or cut. Finding something is not a reason to include it. A note that is mostly a chain of cross-references, or a curiosity that answers no question a reader would ask, is cut.
- **`setting` is short**, about 150–250 words: when, to whom, where the chapter sits in the book, how other scripture uses it. Debates about a verse go in that verse's note. A topic that spans many chapters is explained once on a theme page and linked.

## 4. Sourcing rules

**Everything is referenced; nothing is made up.**

1. **Every factual claim** (a word meaning, a textual variant, a date, a historical detail, a scholarly view, what a prophet taught) **comes from a source you opened and read in the working session, and is cited.** Your own memory is never a source. If you can't find and read a source for a claim, cut the claim.
2. **Don't keep a detail by finding a source for it.** Before sourcing a claim, ask whether the chapter needs it (§1, “Not a compendium”). A peripheral detail is cut, not sourced.
3. **Cite a work only for what you checked it says.** Never list a work because it is the kind of thing that work would say.
4. **Cite the work, not the website that hosts it.** Bible Hub, Scripture Central, BibleGateway, churchofjesuschrist.org and archive.org are where you read something; the source is the work: Delitzsch's commentary, BDB, the Septuagint, a specific KnoWhy or talk. The `sources.yaml` entry names the work; the ledger `url` is the page you read. Don't create keys for a website as such.
5. **The primary texts are evidence, not commentary.** The Hebrew text, the Septuagint, the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Book of Mormon's readings and the rest of scripture answer many questions directly; prefer them to a commentary that reports them. A note whose claims rest only on scripture cites `lds-scriptures` (the Church's editions, including their headings), with a ledger row pointing at the chapter read.
6. **Cite the underlying work behind a secondary source.** If a KnoWhy, dictionary or commentary passes along an idea from another work, read that work and cite it too. If you can't read it, cite only the secondary source for what it itself says, or leave the claim out. A Church teaching manual is never that secondary source (§1): cite the work behind it, or leave the claim out.
7. **Wikipedia, other wikis, and popular history websites (such as Livius.org) are never sources** (2026-09-30). This includes wiki transcriptions of public-domain books: read the work from a scan or an institutional copy and cite that URL. If only a wiki supports a claim, cut it. `check-content.mjs` rejects wiki URLs.
8. **No unofficial copies of copyrighted works.** Don't read from, link to, or cite pirated PDFs or unauthorized uploads. Cite the official DOI or publisher URL.
9. **Commentaries that can't be read online are not cited unless genuinely read.** Known unreadable here: Oswalt (NICOT), Blenkinsopp (Anchor Bible), Childs, Shalom Paul, Westermann, *HALOT*, Parry's *Understanding Isaiah*, Williamson's *The Book Called Isaiah*, Tov's *Textual Criticism*, Matthews' *A Plainer Translation*. Each book's brief lists what is and isn't readable for that book; add to both lists when you find another, and to `NOT_READABLE` in `scripts/check-content.mjs`, which lets a chapter cite one of these only with a quoted ledger row, so the quote must come from a copy you actually read.
10. **Confirm every URL before it goes into `content/sources.yaml`**: it must load a page with the right title. Bible Dictionary and Guide to the Scriptures URLs return a page even for entries that don't exist, so check the heading. Put page or chapter numbers in the entry's `pub` field. `node scripts/check-sources.mjs <book> <chapters…>` opens each proposed entry and flags what is wrong; the author, date and page numbers are still read from the page by whoever cites it.
11. **No single secondary source shapes a chapter.** Start from the text and the scriptures that quote it. As a rule of thumb, no one outside source is the main idea of more than one note. Put a source's idea in your own words, and don't follow its outline.
12. **List in `sources` only what the chapter relies on**: the union of what its notes and prose cite, not a reading list.
13. **Quotations are checked**: scripture quotations with `check-quotes.mjs`; Book of Mormon variants against the text (`data/bom/`); quotations from talks and books against the page you read.
14. **Record guidance, not every judgment call** (2026-10-11). A choice about one verse is made and not logged. What sets how the *book* does something is written into the book's brief, in a line. What needs the author goes in `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md` (`AUTHORING.md`, “Decide, don't defer”).

## 5. The evidence ledger

Required for every chapter and page. It is the record of what was read for each claim, and `scripts/check-content.mjs` validates it.

```yaml
# content/<book>/evidence/NN.yaml  (NN = two-digit chapter number)
chapter: 5
claims:
  - where: 'note 5:3 “Title of the note”'   # or: setting | thread | when | section 2 plain | christ | explore | parallels
    claim: 'Short statement of the factual claim'
    key: bdb                                  # key in content/sources.yaml
    url: 'https://…'                          # the exact page actually read
    quote: 'Verbatim supporting text, at most about 40 words'
```

- `where` names the part of the chapter file: `note <chapter>:<verse> “<note title>”` (the title as it is in the chapter now; if a note is retitled, its rows are), `setting`, `thread`, `when`, `section <n> plain` (sections numbered from 1), `christ`, `explore`, or `parallels`.
- One source per row. A claim supported by two sources gets two rows.
- `url` is the exact page you read (the lexicon entry, the interlinear verse, the article), not a site's home page.
- `quote` is copied from that page as `scripts/source.mjs` prints it, not paraphrased or recalled, and never from a tool's summary of the page. Keep it short (for a lexicon or interlinear, the relevant line); mark an elision with “…”.
- **Every factual claim has at least one row**, and every source key cited anywhere in the chapter appears in the ledger. A claim with no row does not go in the chapter.
- **What needs no row:** what the chapter's own verses say, and scripture quoted with a `[[reference]]` (`check-quotes.mjs` checks those). A claim *about* a scripture beyond its plain words (its date, its Hebrew, a variant, how a tradition reads it) needs one. So does a plain-words rendering that departs from the King James sense.
- **The ledger documents the page; the page is not written to the ledger** (2026-10-26). When every sentence must have a row, writers write one sentence per row in the source's words and the prose takes the shape of the evidence file. Paraphrase is the default and a quotation is used where the words are the point; one row may stand behind a whole paragraph.
- The ledger is committed with the chapter, in the same commit.
- A source that isn't in `sources.yaml` yet is cited by the key you propose for it, listed in `.cache/batch/<book>/proposed/NN.yaml`; the checks read that file, and the coordinator adds the entry afterwards.

## 6. Chapter file structure

Files for a book: `content/<book>/book.yaml`, `chapters/`, `guides/`, `themes/`, `evidence/`; `data/kjv/<book>.json`; optionally `data/bom/<book>-parallels.json`. Markdown files at the top of `content/<book>/` are project notes and aren't published.

`content/<book>/chapters/NN.yaml` (look at an existing chapter for the exact layout). Fields:

- `chapter`, `title`, `tagline`, `when`.
- `setting`: the historical background (150–250 words).
- `thread`: the argument that ties the chapter together.
- `sections`: each has `range`, `heading`, and `plain` (a plain-words paraphrase). The ranges cover every verse exactly once.
  - **Explain the passage in plain words** (2026-10-11). Start from the King James text on the page, but allow a well-supported clarification from the original language or another translation when it materially improves understanding. Explain a meaningful departure in a visible chapter note and support it in the ledger. Where the reading is disputed, do not present an alternative as settled. A language pass preserves these clarifications.
- `notes`: see below.
- `christ`: how the chapter points to Jesus Christ, then, in one to three plain sentences, where that same principle reaches a disciple's life now, grounded in the scripture and the prophets the section cites (§1, “Apply scripture to the reader”).
- `explore`: what's worth exploring next (a short list of scripture threads, and a pointer to an outside resource only if one is especially good).
- `parallels`: `ref`, `note`, and `primary: true` for a Book of Mormon chapter that quotes the whole chapter.
- `sources`: keys from `content/sources.yaml`.

Each note has:

- `ref`: the verse. In Psalms, `ref: 0` is the psalm's title, which the page shows above verse 1; `phrase` is then a substring of the title (`node scripts/show.mjs psalms <n>` prints it as verse 0), and the note's ledger rows say `note <psalm>:0 “<title of the note>”`.
- `phrase`: an **exact** substring of that verse, in the spellings of `data/kjv/<book>.json`. Phrases in the same verse must not overlap.
- `kind`: see §7.
- `title`: plain and descriptive.
- `body`: Markdown.
- `sources`: keys from `content/sources.yaml`. Every note that makes a factual claim has them. No `[@key]` markers in a chapter file.

Write scripture references as `[[2 Ne. 25:4]]` or `[[D&C 113:1–6]]`, with en dashes in ranges; references to a book on the site become internal links.

## 7. Note kinds

One set for every book. Labels and one-line descriptions live in `KINDS` in `src/site.mjs`; that is the authority.

| kind | label | use for |
|---|---|---|
| `words` | Language | What a word or phrase meant in the original language or in 1611 English. |
| `history` | Context | The people, places, and events behind the text. |
| `symbol` | Imagery | What an image would have meant to its first hearers. |
| `christ` | Witness of Christ | Where the passage points to Jesus Christ and His Atonement. |
| `scripture` | Related Scriptures | Other scripture that quotes, explains, or fulfills the passage. |
| `prophets` | Latter-day Prophets | What latter-day prophets and apostles have taught about it. |
| `structure` | Literary Structure | Only when the structure shows the message (a chiasm whose center is the point). |
| `bom` | BoM Comparison | Generated from `data/bom/` where the Book of Mormon text differs. A hand-written `bom` note explains why a reading matters; it appears inside that verse's comparison panel, so the verse must have one, and its phrase isn't highlighted. |

Anything new (a kind, a section, a label) gets a plain, non-clever name, and goes to the author first.

## 8. Guides and theme pages

In `PAGES.md`, under the same section names (“What earns a theme page,” “How a theme page is written,” “One mind writes it,” “A shorter page,” “Where a page stands,” “Where the meaning comes from,” “How a guide is written”). A chapter's writer, checker and reviewer need only this from it: chapter notes make no connections of their own (a theme page may), and when a chapter touches a theme or a guide that has a page, the chapter links to it.

## 9. Research tools and access

The scripts and their options are described in `DEVELOPMENT.md` (“Checks” and “Research scripts”); each prints its usage when run with no arguments. This section is where things can be read.

- **Read a page with `node scripts/source.mjs '<url>'`, never a web-fetch tool.** It prints the page's own text, so a quote copied from it is verbatim, and it caches the page for a day so that every agent fetches it once. Search a page, don't read it through: `--find "words"` prints the paragraphs that have the words; `--para`, `--notes` and `--all` the rest. It reads the Gospel Library with its footnotes, Scripture Central KnoWhys, Joseph Smith Papers transcripts, PDFs with a text layer, Bible Hub, *Interpreter*, rsc.byu.edu, byustudies.byu.edu and archive.org full texts (`…/download/<id>/<id>_djvu.txt`). If a page reads badly, `curl -sgL -A 'Mozilla/5.0' '<url>' | sed 's/<[^>]*>//g'` is the fallback.
- **Start a chapter with `node scripts/footnotes.mjs <book> <n>`:** the Church's heading, footnotes and the *Scripture Helps* chapter with its endnotes. ***Scripture Helps* is a finder, and is never cited** (2026-10-03; a must since 2026-11-17, §1): its endnotes name the studies behind each statement; read the readable ones and cite them for what they say (§4 rule 6). The manual itself is beside the verse in the Gospel Library, so a note doesn't repeat it (§1).
- **Latter-day Saint studies first** (§1). `node scripts/search-lds.mjs "<words>"` searches the Religious Studies Center's full text, the titles of BYU Studies and the *Interpreter* journal, and the KnoWhys at once, and prints the web search to make for what it could not reach. `node scripts/knowhys.mjs <book> <n>` lists the KnoWhys on a chapter; cite one as `sc-knowhy-<number>`. Scripture Central's archive (`archive.bookofmormoncentral.org/content/<slug>`) holds articles and book chapters too. BYU Studies lists studies chapter by chapter on its *Come, Follow Me* pages (`byustudies.byu.edu/come-follow-me/old-testament/<lesson>`) and by book in its bibliography of Latter-day Saint writing on the Old Testament (`byustudies.byu.edu/article/reading-the-old-testament-in-light-of-the-restoration-a-comprehensive-bibliography-of-lds-writings-on-the-old-testament-18301997`; search it with `--find`). The *Interpreter* journal's articles are listed by `curl -sL -A 'Mozilla/5.0' https://interpreterfoundation.org/journal-sitemap.xml | grep -o '<loc>[^<]*' | grep -i '<word>'`; when `interpreterfoundation.org/journal/<slug>` returns no text, read `journal.interpreterfoundation.org/<slug>/`. The Religious Studies Center (`rsc.byu.edu`) is readable; **ScholarsArchive PDFs are not** (only the abstract page), so cite only what the abstract says. Scripture Central and *Interpreter* are faithful and scholarly but not official: cite the article, never the site, and give an author's own reading as that author's.
- **The Hebrew** is the Westminster Leningrad Codex (`oshb-wlc`): `node scripts/hebrew.mjs <book> <c>:<v>` for a verse's words with Strong's numbers and lexicon pages, `--word <strongs>` or `occurrences.mjs --hebrew` for where a word occurs; both use the King James numbering and print the Hebrew reference beside a verse that differs. **Lexicon:** BDB (`bdb`), on Bible Hub's lexicon pages (`biblehub.com/hebrew/<strongs>.htm`); quote only what the page says. **Septuagint:** Brenton on Bible Hub (`biblehub.com/sep/<book>/<c>.htm`), cited `lxx-rahlfs`. **Other translations** on BibleGateway, cited by their own key. **Older commentaries** (Keil and Delitzsch and the like) on Bible Hub (`biblehub.com/commentaries/kad/<book>/<c>.htm`).
- **The Greek** is the Textus Receptus, the Greek behind the King James Version (Maurice Robinson's public-domain edition; Scrivener's 1894 reading, or Stephanus's of 1550 with `--stephanus`): `node scripts/greek.mjs <book> <c>:<v>` for a verse's words with Strong's numbers, parsing codes and lexicon pages, `--word <strongs>` for where a word occurs in the book, `--nt --word <strongs>` for a count across the New Testament. The text is printed without accents. **Lexicon:** Thayer, on Bible Hub's lexicon pages (`biblehub.com/greek/<strongs>.htm`). The King James translators read Stephanus and Beza, so where the editions part the script's text may not be theirs: say what the script's text shows, and name a word by its Strong's number and lemma, never from memory. The keys for the Greek text and for Thayer are in each New Testament book's brief.
- **Church sources**: the scriptures, Bible Dictionary and Guide to the Scriptures (check the heading: a missing entry still returns a page), manuals, general conference, the Joseph Smith Papers, hymns. **Museum catalog pages** are good institutional sources for artifacts.
- **If a site fails twice, stop trying it** and say so in the report; don't probe other domains for a way around. A claim whose only source can't be reached is cut. Sefaria has not loaded from the shell. Each book's brief lists what was and wasn't readable for it; add what you learn there.

## 10. Checks

`node scripts/check-chapter.mjs <book> <n>` runs every check for one chapter and prints only the lines that concern it; `node scripts/check-page.mjs <book or volume> <page>` does the same for a theme or guide page. Fix every warning that concerns your chapter or page; “unknown source key” for a key you propose is expected, and a length warning on an older chapter is the author's to decide. What each check tests is in `DEVELOPMENT.md` (“Checks”).

## 11. Review process

How a chapter is written and reviewed, with the models, is in `AUTHORING.md` (“Plan for the next book”); the coordinator's steps are in `PROCESS.md` (“Writing a book in batches”); a theme page's steps are in `PAGES.md`. Three rules hold for every page:

- **Every new page has a second reader** (2026-10-07; prose check strengthened 2026-10-28): Codex reads it for language and connected explanation (§3) before the author sees it, and every finding is decided. A real gap that needs added reasoning or a connection is repaired from a source, with its ledger row, or by cutting what raises it; it is never declined because a copy edit cannot repair it, and never bridged by invention. The page is complete only when no substantive work remains (`PROCESS.md`, “The second reader”).
- **One commit per chapter**, with its ledger; a theme page and its ledger likewise.
- **Judgment calls** (§4 rule 14): what needs the author goes in `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`; what sets a rule for the whole book goes in the brief, in a line; the rest is not logged.
